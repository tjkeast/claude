# Familial Hypercholesterolaemia in Australia: Cascade Testing, PBS Architecture, and the Cardiovascular Cost of 90% Under-Diagnosis

**Report 100 — 2026-09-18**

**Summary:** Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) affects approximately 1 in 250 Australians (~108,000 people), confers a 20-fold elevated risk of premature coronary artery disease, and is highly treatable from childhood with inexpensive statins — yet over 90% of carriers remain undiagnosed. Australia added MBS cascade genetic testing items in May 2020, listed PCSK9 inhibitors evolocumab and alirocumab from 2021, and added inclisiran (a first-in-class siRNA therapy) from 1 April 2024. The payment architecture exists. But cascade testing uptake is critically low (~12 items per year in New South Wales in 2020–2022), the national FH registry reveals a high burden of established coronary artery disease in diagnosed patients, and no systematic national detection program has been announced.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Reports 097–099 documented Australia's genomic medicine landscape through Lynch syndrome, BRCA/HBOC, and the National Health Genomics Policy Framework. FH is the third hereditary condition in the DNA Screen pilot (alongside BRCA1/2 and Lynch MMR genes) and the leading hereditary cause of premature cardiovascular disease. It represents the same structural problem — a well-characterised hereditary condition with proven, cost-effective interventions — channelled through a different organ system and a different payment pathway.

---

## Background: What Is Familial Hypercholesterolaemia?

Familial hypercholesterolaemia is an autosomal dominant condition caused by pathogenic variants in one of three genes:

- **LDLR** (low-density lipoprotein receptor): responsible for approximately 80–85% of all identified FH-causing variants; reduces hepatic LDL-C clearance
- **APOB** (apolipoprotein B): causes FH in ~5% of cases; impairs LDL binding to its receptor
- **PCSK9** (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9, gain-of-function variants): rare (<1%); accelerates LDLR degradation

Heterozygous FH (HeFH) — one pathogenic variant, one normal allele — affects approximately **1 in 250** Australians, implying a national prevalence of roughly **108,000 people**. Homozygous FH (HoFH) — two pathogenic variants — is far rarer (approximately 1 in 160,000–300,000) but causes extreme LDL-C elevation (often >10 mmol/L), leading to cardiovascular events in childhood or adolescence if untreated.

HeFH typically raises LDL-C to 5–10 mmol/L (vs 3–3.5 mmol/L in the general population). Without treatment, men with HeFH face a **20-fold elevated risk of coronary artery disease** by age 50; women face similar risk with approximately a decade's lag. Tendon xanthomata and premature arcus cornealis are clinical signs, but many patients are asymptomatic until a cardiovascular event.

The hereditary mechanism means that when one person is diagnosed, on average half their first-degree relatives carry the same variant. This makes **cascade testing** — systematically testing relatives of known cases — the most efficient detection strategy available.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of Under-Diagnosis

With an estimated 108,000 Australians affected and fewer than 10–15% diagnosed, at least 90,000 people with a highly treatable hereditary condition are unaware of their risk. The **FH Australasia Network national registry** — which enrolled 1,528 adults across 28 lipid clinics — tells the story of what delayed diagnosis looks like in practice:

- **Mean age at enrolment: 53.4 years.** Patients are reaching specialist care decades into their untreated risk accumulation.
- **28% of registry patients have established coronary artery disease** — compared with approximately 5–6% in the age-matched general Australian population. This is a more than fivefold elevated CAD prevalence, and it represents a cohort that survived to diagnosis.
- **Only 14% of registry patients were identified via cascade testing**, the single most cost-effective detection pathway available. The remainder were detected after a cardiovascular event, incidentally through lipid testing, or via high-risk family history.

The FH diagnosis gap is not primarily a testing or treatment gap — it is a detection gap. The payments and medicines exist; the systematic identification pipeline does not.

### 2. The MBS Cascade Testing Architecture: Added in 2020, Barely Used

On **1 May 2020**, the MBS was expanded to include dedicated items for FH genetic testing. The structure:

**Index case testing:** MBS items cover genetic testing of a patient suspected of having FH (typically referred by a GP or specialist with elevated LDL-C and clinical diagnostic criteria), looking for a pathogenic variant in LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9.

**Cascade testing of relatives:** Once an index case variant is confirmed, GPs can order cascade genetic testing for first- and second-degree biological relatives. This was a structural improvement — previously, cascade testing was dependent on specialist referral and was inconsistently available.

**The uptake problem:** Despite the MBS additions, utilisation has been negligible. A study examining NSW data found **a mean of just 12 cascade genetic tests per year** ordered under these MBS items across 2020–2022. By any measure, this represents a near-complete failure to translate a policy change into clinical practice.

Identified barriers include:

- **GP awareness and confidence**: A general practice of 10,000 patients should theoretically have approximately 40 patients with FH — but GPs are rarely trained in FH recognition or cascade testing initiation.
- **Notification and consent complexity**: Informing at-risk relatives requires the index patient to contact family members who may be geographically dispersed, estranged, or unaware of their risk. No funded infrastructure exists to support this.
- **Limited specialist capacity**: Lipid clinics in Australia are concentrated in tertiary centres. Referral wait times can be 6–12 months. Primary care models that allow GPs to manage FH diagnosis and cascade testing with specialist input exist in guideline form but are poorly resourced.
- **Absence of a centralised cascade program**: Countries with well-functioning FH detection programs (the Netherlands, Norway, the United Kingdom) have centralised registries and dedicated outreach workers who contact at-risk relatives. Australia has no equivalent nationally funded structure.

### 3. The PBS Medicines Architecture: Increasingly Comprehensive, Incompletely Accessed

Australia's PBS roster for FH is now multi-tiered and encompasses the full evidence-based treatment spectrum:

**Statins (rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, others):** PBS-listed at very low cost (general patient co-payment of $31.60; concession $7.70 as of 2025). The mainstay of FH treatment since the 1990s. HeFH patients typically require high-intensity statin therapy to reduce LDL-C by 50–60%. Even high-intensity statins rarely achieve guideline targets (<1.8 mmol/L or <1.4 mmol/L at high cardiovascular risk) without combination therapy.

**Ezetimibe:** PBS-listed as a second-line agent, typically added to statin therapy. Reduces LDL-C by an additional 15–25% by inhibiting intestinal cholesterol absorption.

**Evolocumab (Repatha, PCSK9 monoclonal antibody):** PBS-listed from August 2021. Annual government cost approximately **AU$4,035** (91.8% of AU$4,394 annual list price). PBS criteria require documented failure to achieve LDL-C targets despite maximally tolerated statin plus ezetimibe therapy, with specific thresholds for patients with established ASCVD or HeFH. Must be initiated by a specialist (cardiologist, lipidologist, endocrinologist, or general physician).

**Alirocumab (Praluent, PCSK9 monoclonal antibody):** PBS-listed but **not currently marketed in Australia** as of 2026. Manufacturer commercial decisions have effectively removed it from the market, leaving evolocumab as the only available monoclonal antibody PCSK9 inhibitor.

**Inclisiran (Leqvio, siRNA therapy):** PBS-listed from **1 April 2024** — a genuine policy milestone. Inclisiran targets PCSK9 mRNA in the liver, reducing LDL-C by approximately 50% with an unusual dosing schedule: a loading dose, a second dose at three months, then **twice-yearly injections** thereafter. PBS criteria mirror the PCSK9 monoclonal antibody criteria. Inclisiran represents the first RNA-based therapy on the Australian PBS and substantially lowers the adherence burden for patients who receive it.

**The access gap in practice:** Despite this architecture, only **5.3% of patients in the national FH registry** were on PCSK9 inhibitors — a striking figure given that many registry patients, with their established cardiovascular disease and high LDL-C burden, would appear to meet PBS criteria. The mean treated LDL-C in the registry was **3.3 mmol/L** — nearly double the guideline target of ≤1.8 mmol/L for high-risk FH patients. The PBS criteria — requiring specialist initiation, documented treatment failure, and navigating specific threshold rules — appear to function as a genuine access barrier for many eligible patients.

### 4. The Cost-Effectiveness Case: Compelling and Largely Unacted On

Economic analyses consistently find FH cascade testing to be one of the most cost-effective interventions in Australian healthcare:

- **Cascade testing with statin treatment (adult focus):** An ICER of approximately **AU$4,155 per QALY** — orders of magnitude below the informal AU$50,000–80,000/QALY threshold typically applied in Australian HTA. This analysis used data from Western Australia's centralised cascade testing service, which found that testing relatives from 100 index cases yielded 188 mutation-positive relatives from 366 tested — **about 2 new cases per index case**.

- **Childhood screening (age 10) with treatment initiation:** Cascade screening of children was found to be **cost-saving** — each year of treatment initiated in childhood reduces decades of LDL-C accumulation and cardiovascular event risk.

- **Population genomic screening (young adults):** If a genomic screening test costs ≤AU$300, population screening of young adults for FH would be cost-effective at an ICER below AU$28,000/QALY. This is directly relevant given that LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9 were three of the genes screened in the DNA Screen pilot.

The critical barrier is implementation funding: cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrates value per QALY but does not fund the infrastructure — the cascade counsellors, the centralised registry, the GP training, and the outreach programs — required to deliver that value at scale.

### 5. Childhood and Newborn Screening: An Emerging Policy Frontier

FH ideally should be diagnosed in childhood, allowing treatment to begin before significant LDL-C accumulation and vascular damage occurs. Statin therapy is safe and evidence-based in children with FH from age 8–10 years (with dietary management from diagnosis).

Approximately **22,000 children and adolescents** in Australia and New Zealand are estimated to have FH. The paediatric detection rate mirrors the adult gap — most are undiagnosed.

**Western Australia pilot (2021, *Journal of Clinical Lipidology*):** Universal cholesterol screening at the routine 1–2-year immunisation visit proved feasible, acceptable to parents, and highly effective at detecting both children and their parents. It is an efficient entry point: positive lipid screening in a child triggers cascade testing across the family.

**Queensland healthcare professional surveys (2025–2026):** Surveys of healthcare professionals in Queensland found strong support for introducing FH into newborn bloodspot screening. The main barriers identified were cost, healthcare system burden, absence of a formalised consent process, and incomplete follow-up pathways — not clinical or scientific objections. This mirrors the debate around genomic newborn screening more broadly (Report 099).

**National policy status:** FH is not currently included in Australia's national newborn bloodspot screening program, which covers metabolic, endocrine, and haematological conditions but does not include hereditary cardiovascular conditions. No formal MSAC evaluation of FH newborn screening has been completed.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Why has cascade testing uptake remained so low four years after MBS addition?** The May 2020 MBS changes created the payment mechanism but no supporting infrastructure. A mean of 12 cascade tests per year in NSW suggests the items are largely unknown in general practice. The funding model treats FH cascade testing as a routine clinical activity rather than a disease-specific program requiring dedicated outreach, counsellor support, and registry linkage.

**2. Are PBS PCSK9 inhibitor criteria too restrictive?** The 5.3% PCSK9 inhibitor uptake in the national registry, combined with mean treated LDL-C well above guideline targets, raises the question of whether strict PBS criteria — specialist initiation, documented treatment failure thresholds, complex restriction codes — are suppressing access for patients who are technically eligible. Whether PBAC should review the criteria for FH-specific access (separate from the ASCVD-focused listings) is an open policy question.

**3. Should Australia fund a national FH detection program?** Norway, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom have run or continue to run centralised national FH cascade testing programs with dedicated counsellors and registries. Australia has a national FH registry through the FH Australasia Network, but no nationally funded outreach program. Given the cost-effectiveness evidence, the case for program investment is strong — but FH falls outside the cancer and mental health framings that have historically attracted health policy investment.

**4. Where does FH sit in the National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030?** The Framework (Report 099) lists cardiovascular genomics as one application domain for genomic medicine expansion. But FH — despite being the most common serious monogenic disease in Australia — receives no specific program commitment in the Framework or its accompanying implementation plan. Whether the Framework's "priority two" (dynamic and sustainable genomics ecosystem) translates into a funded FH detection program over 2026–2030 remains to be seen.

**5. The LDL-C target gap as a system performance indicator.** A mean treated LDL-C of 3.3 mmol/L in the FH national registry, against a guideline target of ≤1.8 mmol/L, is a quantifiable measure of underperformance. Australia's 2023 National Heart Foundation guidelines and the 2021 integrated FH guidance both specify these targets. The target is documented; the system is not delivering it.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Mackenzie's Mission and the case for universal reproductive genetic carrier screening** — The parallel debate from Report 099's thread list: an Australian Genomics report (June 2025) makes the case for a universal, free reproductive carrier screening program covering 750+ conditions. What would that program cost, how would it be delivered, and what Budget decision is required?

2. **The genetic counselling workforce as the binding constraint on hereditary disease programs** — Every program discussed in this series — FH cascade testing, Lynch syndrome, BRCA/HBOC, DNA Screen, Mackenzie's Mission — ultimately hits the same workforce constraint: approximately 432 genetic counsellors across Australia and New Zealand. What does a credible workforce expansion plan look like?

3. **Cardiovascular genomics beyond FH: the polygenic risk score debate** — FH is a monogenic, high-penetrance condition. But polygenic risk scores (PRS) for coronary artery disease can identify the top 8% of the population with threefold elevated cardiovascular risk. MSAC is beginning to consider PRS testing submissions. This would represent a fundamentally different genomic medicine model — population-scale risk stratification rather than single-gene cascade testing.

4. **National Heart Foundation guidelines and cardiovascular prevention funding in primary care** — Australia's 2023 absolute cardiovascular risk guidelines (endorsed by the NHFA and RACGP) recommend absolute-risk-based treatment of hypercholesterolaemia. How is this guideline funded in practice? What MBS items support cardiovascular risk assessment, and are they used?

5. **Inclisiran and the siRNA medicine era on the PBS** — Inclisiran's April 2024 PBS listing is the first RNA-based therapy on the Australian PBS. What does its arrival mean for PBAC processes, pricing models, and the pipeline of RNA medicines now entering clinical development?
