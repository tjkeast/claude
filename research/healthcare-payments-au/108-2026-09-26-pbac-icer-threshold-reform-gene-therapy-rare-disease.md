# The PBAC's Implicit ICER Threshold: Why Australia's Cost-Effectiveness Framework Is Under Pressure from Gene Therapies, CAR-T, and the Rare Disease Pipeline

**One-line summary:** Australia's Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) uses an implicit cost-effectiveness threshold of approximately AU$50,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), but the arrival of gene therapies, CAR-T cell therapies, and a growing rare disease pipeline is forcing a fundamental reconsideration of what this threshold means, who it excludes, and whether it needs to be fundamentally rewritten.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 107 on CAR-T delivery noted that four CAR-T products are funded through the Highly Specialised Therapy (HST) program outside normal PBS cost-effectiveness rules — because their ICERs, typically hundreds of thousands of dollars per QALY, would fail any conventional threshold. That funding bypass raises a deeper question: what does Australia's PBS cost-effectiveness framework actually permit, and what is it quietly excluding?

---

## Background

The Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) is Australia's independent expert body responsible for recommending which medicines should be listed on the PBS and at what price. Every medicine recommended for PBS listing must be assessed as cost-effective relative to existing alternatives — a process that has been the backbone of Australia's rational drug funding system since the PBS was modernised in 1987.

Australia's approach differs from countries like the United Kingdom in one critical respect: there is **no explicit, published cost-effectiveness threshold**. The PBAC does not state a maximum price per QALY that it will accept. Instead, it makes decisions based on a range of factors — comparative clinical effectiveness, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), budget impact, clinical need, disease severity, and the strength of evidence — using what academics call a "deliberative process."

This deliberative approach has significant advantages: it allows the PBAC to consider the full context of a medicine rather than reducing a complex clinical reality to a single number. But it also creates uncertainty for manufacturers, patients, and policymakers about what the system will and will not fund — and why.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Implicit Threshold: What the Evidence Shows

Despite the absence of an official figure, empirical research on past PBAC decisions has identified a de facto implicit threshold. The evidence shows:

- Medicines are **most likely to be recommended** when their ICER is approximately **AU$30,000–$45,000 per QALY**
- Medicines with ICERs above **AU$70,000 per QALY** face substantially elevated rejection risk
- The **industry-assumed threshold** — the figure manufacturers use when constructing PBS submissions — is approximately **AU$50,000 per QALY**, a number that has no official basis but is widely treated as the operative ceiling
- For rare diseases specifically, a derived implicit range of **AU$45,000–$75,000 per QALY** has been observed, reflecting PBAC's stated willingness to accept higher ICERs in the face of significant clinical uncertainty

This implicit threshold has not been formally updated in decades. The AU$50,000 figure was not calibrated against Australian willingness to pay, healthcare opportunity costs, or gross domestic product benchmarks — it emerged from accumulated precedent. In real terms, it has eroded significantly since it became embedded in industry practice.

### 2. How Australia Compares Internationally

The contrast with other major HTA systems is instructive:

| Country | Agency | Standard Threshold | Rare Disease/ATMP |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Australia** | PBAC | ~AU$50,000/QALY (implicit) | ~$45,000–$75,000/QALY (implicit, higher under uncertainty) |
| **United Kingdom** | NICE (Technology Appraisal) | £20,000–£30,000/QALY (explicit) | £100,000–£300,000/QALY (HST pathway, ultra-rare) |
| **Scotland** | SMC | £20,000–£30,000/QALY (explicit) | Separate ultra-orphan pathway |
| **Canada** | CDA-AMC | ~CAD$50,000/QALY (implicit, varying by province) | No separate rare disease pathway |
| **New Zealand** | PHARMAC | Fixed budget allocation; no explicit threshold | No separate pathway; historically lowest reimbursement rates |

The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) runs a separate **Highly Specialised Technologies (HST)** process for ultra-rare conditions (prevalence ≤1 in 50,000 people) with a markedly higher cost-effectiveness threshold — up to **£300,000 per QALY** for incremental QALY gains greater than 10. This explicitly acknowledges that the standard QALY framework is inadequate for conditions affecting very small numbers of patients with severe, progressive disease.

Australia has no equivalent HST-style pathway for ultra-rare conditions. The Life Saving Drugs Program (LSDP) funds enzyme replacement therapy for some rare metabolic conditions outside the PBS cost-effectiveness framework, but it is disease-specific, heavily restricted, and does not provide a systematic pathway for the growing rare disease pipeline.

### 3. Where the Threshold Breaks Down: Gene Therapies and CAR-T

The crisis the implicit threshold faces is most visible when examining the ICERs for advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs):

- **Zolgensma** (gene therapy for SMA Type I): ICER of approximately **AU$1.8 million per QALY** compared to standard care (2022 modelling). PBS-listed at approximately AU$2.5 million via an outcomes-based managed entry agreement — meaning standard cost-effectiveness analysis was essentially set aside.
- **CAR-T cell therapies** (tisagenlecleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel): A 2025 systematic review identified ICERs ranging from **US$37,490,000 to US$7,972,845 per QALY** across published cost-effectiveness studies. None comes close to any conventional threshold.
- **Hemgenix** (haemophilia B gene therapy): MSAC rejected listing in 2024 citing clinical uncertainty and price — but the product has been funded via "payment by results" in Italy and through NHS processes in the UK.

The pattern is consistent: the medicines offering the most transformative benefits for the most severely ill patients have ICERs that are orders of magnitude above any conventional threshold. The PBS either funds them through workarounds (outcomes-based deeds, HST pathways, LSDP) or rejects them entirely — leaving patients with no subsidised access.

### 4. The Rare Disease Access Crisis

For the broader rare disease population — beyond the handful of ultra-high-cost ATMPs — the problem is systemic:

- Between 2021 and 2023, the PBAC issued **22 negative recommendations across 20 different orphan medicines**, according to a comprehensive analysis published in Value in Health
- The most frequent reasons for rejection: high treatment costs (77% of negative recommendations), uncertainties in estimating long-term clinical effectiveness (50%), limited clinical evidence (36%), and issues with economic modelling (32%)
- Approximately **39% of orphan-designated medicines** with TGA registration ultimately achieve PBS reimbursement, placing Australia **14th of 15 comparable OECD countries** (IQVIA "All Too Rare" report)
- Only **25% of innovative medicines launched globally** over the past decade have been reimbursed through the PBS, compared with **46% in the UK and 88% in the US**
- Australians wait an average of **391 days** from TGA registration to PBS listing for a proven innovative medicine; for rare diseases, the wait is materially longer

These figures represent real patients. An orphan medicine that fails PBS assessment is not a medicine most Australians can access through any other mechanism — private insurance typically does not cover experimental or high-cost therapies not on the PBS, and the Pharmaceutical Benefits Safety Net provides no relief for medicines not listed.

### 5. The Discount Rate Problem

A specific technical feature of PBAC's framework magnifies the access barrier for gene therapies: the **5% discount rate** applied to future health benefits in cost-effectiveness models.

A gene therapy that cures a patient at age 1 produces health benefits across a 70–80 year lifetime. At a 5% annual discount rate, health benefits occurring 30 years in the future are worth approximately 23% of their face value; benefits at 50 years are worth less than 9%. This dramatically inflates the ICER for any therapy whose benefits are concentrated in the future rather than the near term.

The PBAC itself recognised this problem. Following a multi-year review process and consultation:
- The PBAC recommended reducing the base-case discount rate from **5% to 3.5–4%**, broadly consistent with international practice
- **Medicines Australia** argued for a reduction to 1.5%, noting that the PBAC recommendation left Australia as an outlier
- Implementation of even the modest 3.5–4% reduction has been **delayed**, with industry calling for the change to be accelerated

The practical impact is substantial. Modelling for Zolgensma found the discount rate assumption was one of the most sensitive parameters in the ICER calculation. A reduction from 5% to 3.5% alone could reduce ICERs for gene therapies by 20–30%, materially affecting the PBS listing decision.

### 6. The HTA Review and the IAG Reform Roadmap

In 2024, the Australian Government completed a comprehensive **Health Technology Assessment Review** that identified 50 recommendations for reform. In February 2025, the Government established the **HTA Review Implementation Advisory Group (IAG)** — chaired by the PBAC chair — to translate those recommendations into a sequenced, funded roadmap.

The IAG's interim report, released in **August 2025**, consolidated the 50 recommendations into five interconnected reform focus areas:

1. **Improved access and equity** — ensuring the PBS and MSAC reach patients in need, with particular attention to rare diseases, First Nations health, and geographic disparities
2. **Greater transparency and engagement** — making PBAC decision rationale clearer and expanding consumer and patient voice
3. **Modernised assessment pathways** — streamlining duplicative processes, including for co-dependent technologies requiring both PBAC and MSAC assessment; piloting direct PBAC recommendation on some LSDP medicines
4. **Better data and evidence** — improving real-world evidence infrastructure, outcomes monitoring, and managed entry agreement data
5. **Building HTA workforce capacity and capability** — addressing the expert workforce shortage within PBAC, MSAC, and the Office of Health Technology Assessment

Critically, the IAG was exploring whether to formally acknowledge different thresholds for different categories of medicine — rare diseases, ATMPs, end-of-life conditions — rather than continuing to apply a single implicit figure. The final IAG report was due in **January 2026**, but its public release and the government's response have not yet been announced (as of September 2026), creating uncertainty about implementation timelines.

### 7. The Broad Listing Paradox

One workaround Australia has already implemented — but which creates its own threshold governance problem — is the **broad, multi-indication listing** model used for pembrolizumab and nivolumab/ipilimumab (Report 095). By listing a checkpoint inhibitor across multiple tumour types under a single weighted-average price with a confidential utilisation-based rebate, the PBAC effectively replaces the per-indication ICER with a portfolio-level commercial negotiation.

This model sidesteps the threshold question rather than answering it. It allows access to medicines that would fail individual ICER tests but does so without establishing a transparent framework. A 2026 PharmacoEconomics analysis identified that this approach raises governance concerns: clinical discretion replaces molecular gating, and the cost-effectiveness of the listing as a whole depends heavily on confidential rebate arrangements that the public, prescribers, and researchers cannot scrutinise.

### 8. Stakeholder Views on Reform

A 2025 stakeholder survey of 44 Australian HTA participants (academics and private sector) published in the International Journal of Technology Assessment in Health Care found:

- **>68%** agreed that managed entry agreements, financial risk-sharing, multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA), and increased ICERs for rare diseases should all be used in reimbursement decisions for rare disease medicines
- **77%** of private sector respondents (vs 40% of academics) favoured an increased willingness-to-pay threshold for rare disease medicines — a significant gap reflecting differing views on fiscal sustainability vs equity
- **>70%** agreed that labour productivity, disease severity, equity considerations, and caregiver burden should be formally considered in HTA, but only **27%** believed current PBAC processes were transparent enough about which value elements were being considered

The gap between what stakeholders want and what the framework currently delivers is substantial.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**The sustainability floor vs the access ceiling.** The PBAC's implicit threshold was never calibrated against what Australia's healthcare system can afford — but neither was it calibrated against what patients need. There is genuine tension between maintaining PBS fiscal sustainability (which requires some threshold) and funding the growing pipeline of transformative but expensive therapies.

**The consistency problem.** Zolgensma was listed via an outcomes-based deed that effectively bypassed cost-effectiveness. CAR-T is funded through the HST program. Pembrolizumab is listed via a portfolio-weighted broad listing. Each workaround was created ad hoc. The result is a system where the threshold applies to some medicines but not others, with no published criteria for which category a medicine falls into.

**The opportunity cost question.** Every dollar spent on a $2.5 million gene therapy is a dollar not spent on a primary care consultation or a mental health service. The PBAC implicitly makes these trade-offs, but without a transparent framework, there is no public accountability for the choices being made.

**Whether the IAG roadmap will deliver.** The HTA Review identified 50 recommendations. The IAG has been working on a roadmap since early 2025. The final report is overdue for public release. Without a government response, reform implementation remains uncertain — and the pipeline of gene therapies, ATMPs, and rare disease medicines is not waiting.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **NZ vs Australia rare disease access** — PHARMAC's fixed-budget model funds no commercial CAR-T and has historically provided the lowest rare disease medicine access rates in the developed world; comparing the two systems illuminates different kinds of inequity
2. **Managed entry agreements (MEAs) and outcomes-based deeds in the PBS** — the workaround infrastructure for medicines that fail standard ICER tests; how many are in operation, what outcomes are being measured, and whether they produce real-world evidence that improves future decisions
3. **The rare disease registry gap** — outcomes-based agreements for high-cost medicines are only as good as the data they generate; examining whether Australia's rare disease registry infrastructure can support the obligations being built into these agreements
4. **The PBAC process for repeated rejection** — when a medicine fails PBAC once or twice, what happens to patients in the interim; examining the compassionate access, special access scheme, and hospital funding pathways that fill the gap
5. **PHARMAC's approach to gene therapies and ATMPs** — New Zealand has not funded any commercial gene therapy through PHARMAC; what this means for New Zealanders with rare diseases and whether PHARMAC's fixed budget model is fundamentally incompatible with single-administration high-cost therapies
