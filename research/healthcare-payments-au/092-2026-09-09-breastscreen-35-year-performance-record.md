# BreastScreen at 35: What Has Australia's Breast Cancer Screening Program Actually Achieved?

**Report 092 — 2026-09-09**

**Summary:** Thirty-five years after its 1991 launch, BreastScreen Australia has screened more than 26.7 million women, detected over 137,000 invasive breast cancers, and contributed to a 50% reduction in breast cancer mortality in its target population. Yet its headline achievement is shadowed by a participation rate of just 52% — never having approached its 70% target — a contested overdiagnosis problem, an unresolved equity gap for First Nations women, and a National Policy and Funding Review whose reform ambition is backed by only $1.3 million against a modelled $2 billion reform need.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 091 documented the NBCSP's 20-year record and raised the parallel question of how BreastScreen compares — both programs launched in the same decade with similar call-recall architectures and share the same stubborn failure to reach their participation targets.

---

## Background: BreastScreen Australia's Origins and Architecture

BreastScreen Australia launched as a pilot in 1991 and rolled out nationally between 1991 and 1995, becoming Australia's first population breast cancer screening program. Its design rationale rested on the evidence base of the late 1980s: randomised controlled trials (principally the Swedish Two-County Trial and HIP trial) had demonstrated that biennial mammographic screening in women aged 50–69 reduced breast cancer mortality by approximately 30% in the screened population. Australia adopted a free, publicly funded, two-yearly mammogram for women in that age bracket.

The program's structure has remained largely unchanged since launch:

- **Delivery model:** Screening-only centres (not diagnostic), operated by state and territory health authorities, funded through a Commonwealth-state cost-sharing agreement
- **Target population:** Initially 50–69 year olds; expanded in 2013 to include 70–74 year olds (bringing the official target band to 50–74)
- **Invitation model:** Women self-refer or are re-invited every two years via letter or SMS reminder; no active call-recall registry comparable to the UK National Health Service Breast Screening Programme
- **MBS relationship:** BreastScreen operates outside Medicare — the service is free to women regardless of Medicare status. However, diagnostic follow-up after an abnormal screening result requires GP and specialist referrals billed through Medicare
- **Participation target:** 70% of women aged 50–74; the National Preventive Health Strategy (2021–2030) also sets a 65% target for the same cohort. Neither has ever been achieved

Over 35 years, the program has performed more than **26.7 million** screening mammograms and detected over **137,000** invasive breast cancers (AIHW BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2025).

## Key Findings

### 1. The Mortality Achievement: Real but Partially Attributed to Treatment

Breast cancer mortality in the target population has fallen dramatically since the program's launch — from **74 deaths per 100,000 women aged 50–74** in 1991 to approximately **37 deaths per 100,000** in 2023 (age-standardised). That is a 50% reduction in the age-specific mortality rate over 35 years (AIHW 2025).

At the population level (all ages), breast cancer mortality has fallen approximately 32% since the program began. AIHW monitoring consistently attributes the decline to a combination of:

1. **Earlier detection through screening:** Screen-detected cancers are caught at earlier stages, materially improving survival
2. **Adjuvant systemic therapy improvements:** Tamoxifen (widespread from the early 1990s), aromatase inhibitors (standard from 2000s), trastuzumab for HER2-positive disease (PBS-listed 2006), CDK4/6 inhibitors for advanced HR-positive/HER2-negative disease (PBS-listed 2017 onwards), and now immunotherapy combinations have each shifted the mortality curve independently of screening
3. **Surgical improvements:** Breast-conserving surgery, sentinel node biopsy, and improved reconstructive techniques

**The attribution problem is acute.** A 2012 analysis in *Breast Cancer Research and Treatment* estimated that adjuvant therapy, not mammographic screening, accounted for the majority of the observed breast-cancer-specific mortality reduction in Australian women since 1991 — specifically, that approximately 60% of the decline could not be attributed to BreastScreen. More recent modelling (PHRP 2017) confirmed that while screening has contributed meaningfully, treatment improvements are the larger driver. This finding parallels the NBCSP's attribution challenge documented in Report 091, and matters for the same reason: cost-effectiveness estimates that credit the full mortality decline to the screening program overstate its ICER.

The AIHW's own benchmark estimate — approximately **8 breast cancer deaths prevented per 1,000 women screened** over a biennial programme lifetime — represents the most defensible central estimate and is used in BreastScreen's regulatory framework.

### 2. Participation: Thirty-Five Years at the Wrong Ceiling

The program's participation rate has never broken its 70% target — or even its 65% secondary target. The long-run record:

| Period | Participation Rate (50–74) |
|--------|---------------------------|
| 2014–15 | 53.2% |
| 2015–16 | 53.8% |
| 2016–17 | 54.2% |
| 2017–18 | 54.0% |
| 2018–19 | 53.4% |
| 2019–20 | 49.4% (COVID impact) |
| 2020–21 | 47.1% (COVID impact) |
| 2021–22 | 50.0% |
| 2022–23 | ~52% |
| 2023–24 | **52%** |

(Source: AIHW BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Reports 2024–2025; National Cancer Control Indicators)

In 2023–24, approximately **1.9 million women aged 50–74** were screened — representing 52% of the eligible population. The program has, in effect, found a new post-COVID equilibrium at around 52%, materially below the pre-pandemic peak of approximately 54% and far below either participation target.

The Breast Cancer Network Australia (BCNA) described the 2025 monitoring data as showing that "BreastScreen progress has flatlined" — a characterisation the data support. More than two in five eligible women are not being screened biennially. At an 8-deaths-prevented-per-1,000-screened rate, each percentage point of participation below the 70% target represents thousands of preventable deaths over a generation.

Unlike the NBCSP's call-recall model failure, BreastScreen's gap is structural: the program has never operated a systematic, name-based call-recall system comparable to the UK's NHS Breast Screening Programme, which has maintained 60–65% participation since the early 1990s using active registry-based invitations. Australian women who miss a screening round are re-invited by letter, but no national registry ensures every eligible woman receives a personalised invitation tied to her GP.

### 3. The Overdiagnosis Problem: 8 per 1,000 May Be Unnecessary

Unlike the NBCSP — where overdiagnosis is not a significant issue given the biological behaviour of bowel cancer — breast cancer screening carries a well-documented overdiagnosis risk. **Overdiagnosis** refers to the detection and treatment of cancers that would never have progressed, caused symptoms, or threatened life during the woman's natural lifespan.

Current evidence for BreastScreen Australia:

- RCT-based estimates of overdiagnosis in mammographic screening: **11–19%** of screen-detected cancers (Cancer Council Australia)
- AIHW estimate for the Australian program: approximately **8 invasive breast cancers per 1,000 women screened** may be detected and treated unnecessarily — the same figure as deaths prevented
- This creates a 1:1 ratio of lives saved to lives overtreated in the central estimates — a ratio that is ethically and clinically contested

The harms of overdiagnosis include unnecessary surgery (mastectomy or lumpectomy), radiotherapy, hormone therapy, and associated psychological impact. The 2025 MJA analysis of BreastScreen NSW's transition from film to digital mammography (2002–2016) found that digital technology increased recall rates and false positives — and may have increased overdiagnosis — alongside genuine improvements in detection rates.

The program's false positive recall rates are:
- **First-screen** attendees: ~11% recalled for further investigation
- **Subsequent screen** attendees: ~4% recalled

For a woman attending biennially from age 50 to 74 (12 screens), her cumulative probability of experiencing at least one false-positive recall over a screening lifetime is approximately 25–30%.

This is the central harm-benefit tension that the BreastScreen National Policy and Funding Review (see below) is attempting to address through risk-stratified screening — tailoring frequency and modality to individual risk rather than applying the same biennial mammogram uniformly across all eligible women.

### 4. The Equity Gap: First Nations Women at 36% Participation

The participation gap for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women is stark and has not materially closed in a decade:

- **Indigenous women aged 50–74:** 36.1% participation (2022–23)
- **Non-Indigenous women aged 50–74:** 51.2% participation (2022–23)
- **Gap:** 15 percentage points; age-adjusted participation is 30% lower for Indigenous women

The mortality consequences are severe. After adjusting for age, breast cancer mortality is **26% higher** for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women than for non-Indigenous women (AIHW BreastScreen 2025). This mortality excess reflects multiple compounding factors:

- Lower participation in screening, delaying diagnosis to later stages
- Barriers to diagnostic follow-up after abnormal screens (geographic isolation, cost of specialist consultations, transport, cultural safety of services)
- Higher rates of more aggressive tumour subtypes in some First Nations populations
- Lower access to oncology services and PBS-listed targeted therapies in remote communities

The Northern Territory is a persistent outlier: AIHW data consistently identifies the NT as having the lowest BreastScreen participation among all jurisdictions and the worst Indigenous equity outcomes. Unlike the cervical screening program (see Report 088), which invested $48.2 million in a targeted Indigenous equity strategy including NACCHO co-design and mobile outreach, BreastScreen has no equivalent funded Indigenous equity program at national scale.

### 5. The Dense Breast Notification Policy: Reform Delayed

Breast density is both a risk factor for breast cancer (dense breasts reduce mammography sensitivity by masking tumours) and an independent marker of increased cancer risk. Approximately **40–45% of women aged 50–74** have heterogeneously or extremely dense breasts on mammography.

BreastScreen's approach to breast density notification has been:
- **2020 position statement:** BreastScreen Australia should *not* routinely record or report breast density to clients
- **May 2025 revised position statement:** BreastScreen Australia recommends women *be informed in writing* of their mammographic density and may seek GP advice about supplemental screening

The 2025 policy reversal is significant — but implementation remains uneven. As of mid-2026, not all jurisdictional BreastScreen programs have implemented the notification. The inforMD resource notes that inconsistency across state programs has created "significant health promotion challenges, with low awareness and knowledge of breast density as a risk factor and confusion regarding assessment and risk management."

The policy gap matters clinically: for women with extremely dense breasts, mammography sensitivity falls to approximately 61% — meaning roughly 4 in 10 cancers are missed. Supplemental ultrasound or MRI substantially improves detection in this cohort, but neither is funded through BreastScreen, and MBS rebates for breast MRI remain restricted to high-risk genetic populations (BRCA1/2 carriers, Li-Fraumeni syndrome). The financial cost of supplemental screening falls entirely on the woman or her PHI.

### 6. The National Policy and Funding Review: Ambition vs. Resources

The Federal Government announced a **BreastScreen Australia National Policy and Funding Review** in 2023, intended to address:
- Outdated program architecture (unchanged since 1991)
- Stagnant participation
- Equity gaps
- Dense breast notification inconsistency
- The question of expanding access to younger women (40–49) or older women (75+)
- Risk-stratification as a programme design principle

The review has completed its consultation phase. BCNA's response and publicly available recommendations include:

1. **A national annual-screening policy** (shifting to annual mammograms for higher-risk women)
2. **A National Participation Strategy** to lift rates toward 70%
3. **A strengthened Quality and Safety Framework**
4. **A dedicated Indigenous equity investment** modelled on the cervical screening strategy
5. **Breast density notification implemented uniformly** across all jurisdictions

The government's own modelling shows that implementing these reforms could cost up to **$2 billion over the next decade**. The funding allocated to begin the work is **$1.3 million** — a 1,538:1 gap between the modelled reform cost and the initial investment. BCNA publicly warned that the funding announced "is not enough to deliver the scale of reform needed."

The contrast with the cervical screening program is instructive: the NCSP invested in a complete redesign in 2017 (Pap to HPV, two-yearly to five-yearly, universal self-collection from 2022) backed by substantial capital funding. BreastScreen's review, by contrast, has produced a consultation document with no equivalent implementation budget.

### 7. Emerging Technologies: AI and Tomosynthesis

Two technological developments are reshaping what BreastScreen could deliver:

**AI-assisted reading:** A 2023 study of 7,533 consecutive prevalent screening mammograms from BreastScreen Australia found AI matched radiologists' cancer detection performance — identifying 51/54 invasive cancers and 12/13 DCIS cases detected by radiologists. AI could augment double-reading (required under BreastScreen quality standards) and address the radiologist workforce constraint. However, no MBS funding or national BreastScreen framework for AI-assisted reading exists as of mid-2026.

**Digital Breast Tomosynthesis (DBT):** An interim analysis of 5,000 screens from the Maroondah BreastScreen prospective trial found a DBT/digital mammography hybrid achieved a cancer detection rate of **10.6 per 1,000** with a recall rate of 4.2% — substantially higher detection than standard digital mammography (~6.1 per 1,000) at comparable recall rates. DBT is standard of care in several European breast screening programs. It is not currently funded through BreastScreen Australia nationally.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Is the Overdiagnosis Harm Acceptable at Current Participation?

The central ethical tension in breast cancer screening is that the same program that prevents 8 deaths per 1,000 women also overtreats approximately 8 women per 1,000. At 52% participation, the program prevents more deaths than it causes overtreatment. At higher participation — which all reform proposals seek — both benefits and harms scale proportionally unless screening is risk-stratified. Moving to annual screening for high-risk women (a BCNA proposal) would increase both detection and overdiagnosis in that cohort. Risk stratification is, in theory, the solution — but no population-scale risk-stratification model for breast screening has been fully implemented anywhere in the world.

### Tension 2: The Under-50 Question

The US Preventive Services Task Force updated its breast cancer screening guidelines in 2024, recommending mammograms starting at age **40** (moved from 50) for all women. This puts Australian policy at odds with the US approach and aligns Australia more closely with UK and European guidelines, which generally retain 47–50 as the starting age. Australian modelling (Cancer Epidemiology & Biomarkers Prevention, 2018) found that extending BreastScreen to 40–49 year olds would produce 1,376 false positives and 4–37 overdiagnosed cancers per 1,000 women screened — substantially higher harm rates than in the 50–74 cohort. Women aged 40–49 currently may self-refer to BreastScreen at no cost, but are not routinely invited. The Review's position on this question will be critical.

### Tension 3: Structural Under-Investment in Equity

BreastScreen has operated for 35 years with a 15-percentage-point participation gap for First Nations women — roughly comparable to the NBCSP's Indigenous gap. For cervical screening, a $48.2 million dedicated equity strategy (Report 088) with NACCHO co-design produced measurable participation improvements. No equivalent strategy has been funded for BreastScreen. The Review's $1.3 million initial investment makes a national Indigenous equity program structurally impossible.

### Tension 4: The NBCSP Comparison — Same Problem, Different Numbers

Both programs are now running at approximately the same structural point:
- BreastScreen: 52% participation vs 70% target (18 points below)
- NBCSP: 42% participation vs 60% target (18 points below)
- Both programs have 35 and 20 years respectively of operating below target
- Both have significant First Nations participation gaps (~15 points each)
- Neither has a funded national participation strategy

The lesson from 35 years of BreastScreen is not that the NBCSP's ceiling is temporary. It is that without structural investment in GP integration, active call-recall, and equity-targeted outreach, screening participation stabilises far below its target and stays there.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHI premium affordability and the screening aftermarket.** Women with dense breasts or abnormal BreastScreen results are channelled into diagnostic services (breast MRI, ultrasound, specialist consultation) that fall outside the free BreastScreen program. PHI coverage gaps, specialist fees, and gap payments create a two-tier diagnostic follow-up system analogous to the NBCSP colonoscopy gap. What does that cost women, and who is left behind?

2. **UK NHS Breast Screening Programme vs BreastScreen Australia: divergent trajectories after 35 parallel years.** The UK launched its program in 1988 — three years before Australia. The NHS BSPP has consistently achieved 60–65% participation using a name-based call-recall registry and mandatory GP endorsement, while BreastScreen has plateaued at 52%. What structural differences explain the gap, and can Australia close it?

3. **Risk-stratified breast screening: the global evidence and Australian feasibility.** The PERSPECT study (UK, 2024), the Karolinska MyPeBs trial (Europe), and the Wisdom Study (US) are evaluating personalised screening intervals and modalities based on polygenic risk score, breast density, family history, and BMI. If Australia moves to risk stratification, what population infrastructure (genetic testing, density registries, risk algorithms) is needed and what does it cost?

4. **Breast cancer treatment costs and the PBS:** PBS-listed CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib) and PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib) are transforming advanced breast cancer treatment — and its cost. At approximately $8,000–$12,000 per patient per month pre-PBS listing, these drugs represent the fastest-growing component of the oncology PBS budget. Earlier detection through higher screening participation would reduce the number of patients reaching the advanced-disease treatment pipeline.

5. **Tomosynthesis and AI: the case for an MBS-funded upgrade to BreastScreen.** If DBT improves cancer detection by ~4 per 1,000 screens over digital mammography, and AI can match double-reading performance, what is the cost-effectiveness case for a national technology upgrade — and who would bear the capital cost under the current Commonwealth-state funding architecture?
