# Multi-Target Stool DNA Testing: A Higher-Sensitivity Screening Alternative That Australia Hasn't Funded

**Report 086 — 2026-08-31**

**Summary:** Multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) testing — the technology behind Cologuard Plus, FDA-approved in October 2024 — detects colorectal cancer with 95% sensitivity and advanced precancerous lesions at 43% sensitivity, compared to 67% and 23% respectively for the fecal immunochemical test (FIT/iFOBT) that underpins Australia's NBCSP. Australia has not funded, registered, or commenced a formal health technology assessment of any mt-sDNA product for population screening; biennial iFOBT remains the sole funded non-invasive screening modality. The gap between what the science now supports and what Australia has assessed is widening rapidly.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 085 documented that CT colonography — a structural alternative to optical colonoscopy for NBCSP follow-up — is unavailable as a funded NBCSP pathway despite US and UK adoption. This report examines the upstream equivalent: alternative primary screening modalities that could replace or supplement the iFOBT test itself, with a focus on multi-target stool DNA testing.

---

## Background: What the NBCSP Currently Does

Australia's National Bowel Cancer Screening Program sends biennial immunochemical fecal occult blood test (iFOBT) kits to all Australians aged 50–74 (with an opt-in extension to 45–49 in partial rollout as of 2025, following the early-onset colorectal cancer evidence documented in Report 078). The kit detects blood in stool using antibodies to human haemoglobin — a straightforward, cheap, and durable test that has been the program's primary tool since 2006.

The iFOBT's strengths are its low cost, ease of use, and acceptable performance for detecting established colorectal cancer. Its weaknesses are its low sensitivity for advanced precancerous lesions (the lesions the program most needs to intercept early) and its particular difficulty detecting right-sided cancers and sessile serrated lesions — the lesion type responsible for a significant proportion of interval cancers (Report 082).

The NBCSP program cost $42.1 million in 2023–24, covering 6.28 million invitations over the 2022–2023 rolling period (AIHW, 2024). Of those invited, only **41.7% participated** — meaning approximately 3.7 million Australians eligible for free bowel cancer screening did not return their test kit. This stubborn non-participation rate has been one of the program's central failures for over two decades.

## Key Findings

### 1. The iFOBT's Performance Gap: What It Misses

The FIT/iFOBT's diagnostic performance in population screening is well characterised:

- **Sensitivity for colorectal cancer: ~79%** — meaning roughly one in five established cancers is missed in the round in which they exist
- **Sensitivity for advanced precancerous lesions (advanced adenomas, high-grade dysplasia): ~23%** — meaning more than three quarters of the high-risk polyps the program aims to intercept are not detected
- **Specificity: ~94%** — meaning approximately 6% of participants receive a positive result without cancer or significant adenoma

These figures reflect a test designed for cost-effective population screening at scale, not maximal detection. The tradeoff was explicitly modelled and accepted when the NBCSP was designed: biennial repetition partially compensates for each round's sensitivity gaps, such that cumulative multi-round performance is substantially better than any single-round figure implies. AIHW modelling consistently found iFOBT to be cost-effective (approximately $2,693–$3,048 per life-year saved vs no screening; Canfell et al., Lancet Public Health, 2017).

But the program's right-sided cancer problem is structural. The sessile serrated lesions that precede many right-sided cancers — and that account disproportionately for interval cancers (Report 082) — do not reliably bleed, and so do not reliably trigger a haemoglobin-based test like iFOBT. This is the core biological limitation that has driven interest in molecular alternatives.

### 2. First-Generation Cologuard (mt-sDNA 1.0): What It Proved

Exact Sciences launched the first-generation Cologuard stool DNA test in the United States in 2014 following FDA approval. The test combined iFOBT with detection of aberrant DNA methylation (NDRG4, BMP3) and KRAS mutations in stool. The pivotal CONFIRM trial (DeeP-C; NEJM, 2014) in 9,989 average-risk adults found:

- **Sensitivity for CRC: 92.3%** (vs 73.8% for FIT alone)
- **Sensitivity for advanced precancerous lesions: 42.4%** (vs 24% for FIT alone)
- **Specificity: 86.6%** (vs 94.9% for FIT alone)

The sensitivity gain over iFOBT came with a specificity cost: mt-sDNA generated significantly more false positives, each of which requires a diagnostic colonoscopy. This false-positive burden has been a central concern about mt-sDNA's population screening economics from the outset.

The first-generation test was approved for screening every three years in the US. It was never submitted for TGA registration or MSAC assessment in Australia.

### 3. Next-Generation Cologuard Plus: The BLUE-C Study Results

In March 2024, the NEJM published results from the BLUE-C study (approximately 20,000 average-risk participants), evaluating Exact Sciences' next-generation mt-sDNA test (Cologuard Plus). The test adds novel methylation markers and a next-generation sequencing component to the first-generation platform. Compared to FIT in the same participants:

| Metric | Cologuard Plus | FIT |
|--------|---------------|-----|
| CRC sensitivity | **95%** | 67.3% |
| Advanced precancerous lesion sensitivity | **43%** | 23.3% |
| Specificity | **94%** | ~95% |
| Screening interval (US) | Every 3 years | Annually |

The FDA approved Cologuard Plus on **October 4, 2024**, making it the highest-sensitivity non-invasive stool-based colorectal cancer screening test ever cleared by a major regulatory agency. Medicare reimbursement was set at **$592 per test** (2025 Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule), with a self-pay price of approximately $599. Testing frequency: every three years.

The specificity improvement over the first-generation test (94% vs 86.6%) is clinically significant — it means fewer false positives requiring diagnostic colonoscopy, partially addressing the cascade burden concern that made the first-generation test less economically attractive.

No equivalent assessment has been undertaken in Australia as of this report.

### 4. Why mt-sDNA Addresses the iFOBT's Specific Structural Weakness

The methylation markers in mt-sDNA tests are particularly valuable for detecting:

- **Sessile serrated lesions (SSLs)** — the flat right-sided lesions that iFOBT frequently misses (they tend not to bleed) and that represent the precursor to a disproportionate share of interval cancers and right-sided CRC
- **Right-sided cancers** — which bleed less predictably than left-sided cancers, producing lower haemoglobin signals in stool

In the Australian context, where interval cancers (especially in the Northern Territory, at 8.4 per 10,000 — Report 082) represent a known quality failure partly attributable to lesion biology, the methylation-marker advantage of mt-sDNA is directly relevant. The iFOBT's interval cancer problem and the mt-sDNA's particular sensitivity for the lesion types that cause those cancers are aligned arguments — not coincidentally so, since mt-sDNA was engineered with the iFOBT's known biological limitations in mind.

### 5. Blood-Based Alternatives: The Shield Test

A further layer of alternative screening technology arrived in July 2024 when the FDA approved Guardant Health's **Shield blood test** for average-risk colorectal cancer screening. Shield detects circulating tumor DNA in blood. Its performance is substantially lower than mt-sDNA:

- **CRC sensitivity: 83%** (55–65% for Stage I)
- **Advanced precancerous lesion sensitivity: 13%** (does not detect 87% of precancerous lesions)
- **Specificity: 90%**

Shield's clinical utility as a primary population screening tool is contested — its precancerous lesion detection is far too low for a test intended to prevent cancer, not merely detect it. Its primary appeal is as a "participation bridge" for the population segment that will not complete any stool-based test, including iFOBT.

**ColoSTAT® (Rhythm Biosciences, Australia)** is a blood-based test available in Australia, but it is a diagnostic aid for symptomatic patients, not a population screening tool; it is not comparable to Shield or Cologuard Plus in role or performance.

No blood-based screening test has been submitted to MSAC for assessment in Australia as of mid-2026.

### 6. Australia's Position: No Assessment, No Pathway

Australia's current regulatory and HTA landscape for alternative stool-based screening modalities:

- **TGA registration of Cologuard or Cologuard Plus: none**
- **MSAC application for mt-sDNA as primary NBCSP screening modality: none**
- **PBAC involvement: none** (mt-sDNA is a pathology service, not a pharmaceutical)
- **NHMRC clinical guidelines:** The Prevention, Early Detection and Management of Colorectal Cancer guidelines recommend biennial iFOBT; DNA tests were assessed as *not* cost-effective in the 2017 analysis

The 2017 modelling (Canfell et al., Lancet Public Health) that concluded iFOBT was the most cost-effective screening approach evaluated faecal DNA tests but pre-dated both the next-generation mt-sDNA technology and the dramatically improved specificity of Cologuard Plus. The cost inputs used were based on US prices, which are substantially higher than any price Australia could reasonably expect through government procurement.

A 2025 Australian modelling study (ESMO Gastrointestinal Oncology) re-evaluating screening strategies for early-onset CRC assessed five-yearly stool biomarker screening as one alternative to biennial iFOBT, but no assessment has been undertaken specifically for next-generation mt-sDNA at the performance and pricing parameters now established by the BLUE-C trial and FDA approval.

### 7. The Participation Dividend Hypothesis

One argument for mt-sDNA adoption that Australia's policy community has not formally evaluated is the **participation dividend**: would a less frequent test (every 3 years vs every 2 years) and a test that detects a broader range of lesions attract higher participation from the 58% of Australians currently not returning their iFOBT?

US adherence data for Cologuard shows real-world re-screening rates of approximately 37–45% at the three-year interval — roughly comparable to or slightly below FIT adherence in many US settings. Whether a lower-frequency test would improve participation in the Australian context, where barriers include health literacy, kit disgust, and cultural factors rather than frequency alone, is unknown. No Australian feasibility study of mt-sDNA participation rates has been reported.

### 8. The Cost Equation: What Would mt-sDNA Cost Australia?

At the US Medicare rate of $592 per test every three years, the cost of mt-sDNA as a population screening tool at Australia's eligible population scale would be substantial. Applying a rough government procurement discount and the annualised cost of triennial testing:

- NBCSP eligible population (50–74): approximately 7 million
- At 41.7% participation (current iFOBT rate): ~2.9 million participants per 2-year rolling period
- Biennial iFOBT program cost 2023–24: $42.1 million (~$14 per participating person per year)
- mt-sDNA at $592 per test every 3 years (annualised ~$197 per person): **~$572 million per year** at current participation

Even at a 70% government procurement discount (bringing the per-test cost to approximately $178 — speculative), the annualised program cost would be in the order of $170 million per year at current participation levels, versus $42.1 million for iFOBT. This order-of-magnitude cost difference is the central economic obstacle to any near-term Australian adoption, regardless of sensitivity gains.

Whether the gains in prevented cancers — particularly through better advanced precancerous lesion detection — justify the cost differential in the Australian payment context is precisely the question a MSAC health technology assessment would need to answer, and that assessment does not yet exist.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Sensitivity Gain vs Program Cost
The mt-sDNA test detects more cancers and far more advanced precancerous lesions per round than iFOBT. But biennial iFOBT's cumulative multi-round performance, combined with its dramatically lower cost, means the cost-per-cancer-detected differential may not be as stark as single-round comparisons imply. The relevant modelling — for the Australian context, at procurable Australian prices, with Australian colonoscopy follow-up constraints — has not been done.

### Tension 2: Specificity and the Colonoscopy Pipeline
The first-generation mt-sDNA's lower specificity (86.6%) compared to iFOBT (~94%) meant significantly more false-positive colonoscopies — each adding cost and risk to the NBCSP's already-strained follow-up pipeline. Cologuard Plus's improved specificity (94%) substantially narrows this concern, but the Australian colonoscopy capacity crisis (Reports 080–085) means any increase in false-positive diagnostic referrals has significant system-level consequences.

### Tension 3: Who Conducts the Assessment?
mt-sDNA is a pathology service, not a pharmaceutical — PBAC is not the relevant committee. MSAC assesses medical services including diagnostic tests. But the NBCSP is a Department of Health population health program, separate from MBS funding. A decision to replace or supplement iFOBT in the NBCSP would require a coordinated assessment across MSAC, the Department of Health's cancer screening policy branch, and potentially the NBCSP's own clinical guidelines. No pathway exists to commission this assessment in the absence of either a formal MSAC application (typically industry-sponsored, and Exact Sciences has no Australian market presence) or a government-initiated review.

### Tension 4: Three Years vs Two Years
Triennial mt-sDNA testing means each person is screened less frequently than with biennial iFOBT. If a fast-growing right-sided cancer develops 18 months after a negative mt-sDNA, the next scheduled test is 18 months away rather than 6. The biological assumption that triennial testing is adequate rests on cancer growth kinetics — the same assumption underlying colonoscopy surveillance intervals. Whether this assumption holds equivalently for the full spectrum of Australian CRC biology is untested.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **First Nations colorectal cancer screening equity: the NBCSP participation gap and what targeted investment exists.** The NT has the highest interval cancer rate nationally (8.4 per 10,000); First Nations Australians face compounding access barriers. Does the NBCSP address these, and would any alternative screening modality help?

2. **The private health insurance and colonoscopy out-of-pocket gap.** NBCSP positive-result follow-up colonoscopy in the private sector carries significant out-of-pocket exposure — anaesthetic gaps, specialist gaps, day hospital facility fees. What is the real cost to an uninsured or underinsured Australian, and how does this affect program equity?

3. **The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025 and compliance architecture.** The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care published a new Standard in 2025 governing colonoscopy quality. What does it require, and does it address the systemic quality failures documented in Reports 082–085?

4. **CADx and optical biopsy: the "resect-and-discard" strategy and its downstream cost implications.** Computer-aided characterisation (CADx) can optically diagnose polyp histology in real time, potentially eliminating routine histopathology for sub-centimetre polyps. This has major implications for NBCSP downstream costs — and for the colonoscopy cascade triggered by positive mt-sDNA results.

5. **Bowel cancer incidence trends and the NBCSP's 20-year performance record.** With NBCSP mortality rates down from 44 to 23 per 100,000 between 2006 and 2025, what proportion of the improvement is attributable to screening vs treatment advances vs demographic change — and what does this tell us about the value of investing in screening technology upgrades?
