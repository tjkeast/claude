# Australia's Genomics Policy Architecture and the Population Screening Decision

**Report 099 — 2026-09-16**

**Summary:** Australia has built one of the world's most advanced frameworks for integrating genomics into healthcare — a $500 million research mission, rapidly expanding MBS coverage, a released National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030, and a landmark law banning genetic discrimination in life insurance from October 2026. Yet the central policy question raised by reports 097 and 098 — whether Australia will fund a national preventive genomic screening program for adults at high hereditary risk of cancer and cardiovascular disease — remains unanswered. A pilot program has proved the concept; the cost-effectiveness case is established; the discrimination barrier is falling. The funding decision has not been made.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Reports 097 (Lynch syndrome) and 098 (BRCA/HBOC) each documented a structural gap: Australia's MBS testing architecture is reactive, triggered by cancer diagnosis or family history, leaving 74.5% of carriers with pathogenic hereditary variants outside the current funded system. Both reports identified a potential national population genomic screening program — evidenced by the DNA Screen pilot — as the systemic response. This report examines what that policy architecture actually looks like, what has been decided, and what remains undecided.

---

## Background: The Multi-Layer Genomics System

Australia's genomic health system operates across three interlocking layers:

**Layer 1 — Research and translation (MRFF Genomics Health Futures Mission):** A $500.1 million, 10-year investment (2018–19 to 2028–29) funding genomic health research. This is not clinical funding — it supports research programs that may eventually produce MBS-listable tests and population-level programs. Major funded programs include Australian Genomics, Mackenzie's Mission (reproductive carrier screening), DNA Screen (adult preventive genomic screening), and multiple newborn genomic sequencing studies (GenSCAN consortium).

**Layer 2 — Clinical funding (MBS and PBS):** The Medicare Benefits Schedule is the mechanism by which genomic tests become reimbursed for patients. Between 2014 and 2023, the number of publicly subsidised genetic and genomic tests grew from 32 items across 20 test types to 102 genetic tests and 9 genomic tests across 65 distinct clinical indications. Service volumes grew 50% (from 250,881 to 376,140) and benefits paid grew 83% (from AU$42.0 million to AU$76.8 million). Genomic testing expenditure grew at 6.9% per annum — nearly double the 4.1% annual growth rate of the overall MBS. Nevertheless, genomic testing represents just 0.3% of the AU$27.6 billion total MBS expenditure.

**Layer 3 — Intergovernmental coordination (National Health Genomics Policy Framework):** The Australian Government, in partnership with all state and territory governments, released the National Health Genomics Policy Framework and Implementation Plan 2026–2030 in 2026. The Framework followed a consultation process opened in July 2025 and provides a coordinated, national approach to embedding genomics into the health system. It is a coordination mechanism — not a funding commitment.

## Key Findings

### 1. The National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030: Coordination Without Capital

The National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030 was jointly developed by the Australian Government and all state and territory governments, with input from consumers, communities, clinicians, researchers, and industry. Its vision is that "all people living in Australia benefit from appropriately embedded genomics within the health system."

The Framework identifies four strategic priorities:

1. **Person-, Family- and Community-Centred Approach** — ensuring genomic health services are accessible, equitable, and culturally safe, with specific emphasis on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities and culturally and linguistically diverse populations.

2. **Dynamic and Sustainable Genomics Ecosystem** — building the workforce, infrastructure, and system capacity required to support expanded genomic health services over the five-year period.

3. **Genomic Samples, Data and Information Management** — establishing shared standards for data governance, interoperability, and consent frameworks across jurisdictions.

4. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander-led Genomics** — a dedicated priority ensuring that First Nations communities lead and co-design genomic health initiatives affecting their communities.

The Framework succeeds the 2017 National Health Genomics Policy Framework and the Health Ministers' Roadmap for genomic testing. The significance of a new intergovernmental framework is real: it signals shared political will across Commonwealth and state and territory governments to embed genomics into routine care. But the Framework does not announce a national population genomic screening program, and it does not provide new funding for clinical genomic services beyond what MRFF and MBS already cover.

### 2. The DNA Screen Pilot: The Evidence Australia Has Not Yet Acted On

The most important research input to the population screening decision is the DNA Screen pilot, led by Professor Paul Lacaze and colleagues at Monash University and published in *Nature Health* in January 2026.

**Key results:**
- **30,017 people registered**; 18,573 were invited; **10,263 completed genomic screening** (aged 18–40, median age 31.9, 45.5% men, 30.0% culturally or linguistically diverse)
- **202 (2.0%) of completers** had a pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant in one of 10 genes screened (BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM [Lynch MMR genes], LDLR, APOB, PCSK9 [FH genes])
- **97.9% of referred participants accepted** clinical follow-up; **87.3% attended** appointments — unusually high uptake for a screening program
- **74.5% of referred participants were ineligible** for government-funded genomic testing under current MBS criteria — they had no cancer diagnosis and insufficient family history to trigger existing funded tests
- The pilot was funded by a **AU$2.97 million MRFF grant** — a very small investment for nationally significant evidence

The headline statistic — approximately **1 in 50 young Australians** carries a pathogenic variant in a high-risk hereditary disease gene, and three in four of those carriers would not be identified under the current reactive clinical testing architecture — is the quantitative argument for population screening reform.

The DNA Screen team is now calling for a **100,000-person translational phase** to determine the optimal implementation model for a national program. As of mid-2026, no government commitment to fund this phase has been publicly announced.

### 3. The Cost-Effectiveness Case: Established, Not Acted On

The economic case for adult preventive population genomic screening was established by the Reeves et al. modelling study, published in *eClinicalMedicine (Lancet)* in November 2023.

The study modelled a combined genomic screening program for BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome MMR genes, and familial hypercholesterolaemia genes, offered to the 8.3 million Australians aged 18–40, with:
- **Base-case cost assumption:** AU$200 per sample (realistic at current sequencing costs)
- **Testing uptake modelled:** 50%

**Projected health outcomes per 100,000 individuals screened:**
- **747 QALYs gained**
- **63 cancers prevented** (breast, ovarian, colorectal, endometrial)
- **31 coronary heart disease events prevented** (through FH identification and treatment)
- **97 deaths prevented**

**Cost-effectiveness:**
- **ICER: AU$20,734 per QALY gained** — well below the AU$50,000–$150,000 threshold typically used in Australian health technology assessment
- Cost-effective in **70% of sensitivity analysis simulations**
- Cost-saving (negative ICER) in **25% of simulations**
- Not cost-effective in only **5% of simulations**

At AU$200 per test — which is achievable with current panel sequencing costs — population genomic screening for these three conditions meets the cost-effectiveness standards Australia applies to MBS and PBS listing decisions. The ICER is comparable to or better than many currently funded cancer screening and treatment programs.

The Australian modelling has been cited internationally as among the strongest economic evidence for adult population genomic screening. The policy gap is not evidential.

### 4. The MBS as the Scaling Mechanism: What Has Been Listed

The practical path from a research-proven genomic test to a funded clinical program in Australia runs through the MBS — specifically through MSAC (Medical Services Advisory Committee) evaluation and positive MSAC recommendations to the Minister. Between 2014 and 2023, this pathway delivered:

- MBS items for BRCA1/2 germline testing in breast and ovarian cancer (2017, expanded 2023–2025)
- MBS items for Lynch syndrome MLH1 promoter methylation and MMR protein expression in CRC (though gaps remain, as documented in Report 097)
- MBS items for exome and genome sequencing in paediatric rare disease (2020 onward)
- MBS items for mitochondrial disease genome sequencing (November 2023)
- MBS items for heritable kidney disease (2022) and heritable cardiac conditions
- HRD testing for ovarian cancer companion diagnosis (2024)
- New items across cardiac, reproductive, and rare disease testing throughout the period

The total MBS genetics and genomics budget of AU$76.8 million (2023) is growing at nearly double the rate of overall MBS spending. The MRFF 2026 Genomics Health Futures grant — AU$74 million over the grant cycle — includes a specific Stream 1 focused on "genomic tools and technologies to identify genetic predisposition to cancer and improve screening and targeted intervention," including liquid biopsy access. This research funding may generate future MSAC submissions.

**What is not yet listed:** No MBS item exists for preventive adult population genomic screening (i.e., screening healthy adults without cancer diagnosis or qualifying family history for BRCA, Lynch syndrome, or FH variants). The 74.5% ineligibility figure from DNA Screen directly reflects this gap. Getting from a positive DNA Screen translational phase to an MBS listing would require a formal MSAC application, a cost-effectiveness assessment, and a ministerial decision — a pathway that typically takes 3–7 years.

### 5. The Genetic Discrimination Reform: Removing the Last Major Barrier

A critical enabler for expanded population genomic screening has just been legislated. The **Treasury Laws Amendment (Genetic Testing Protections in Life Insurance and Other Measures) Act** received Royal Assent on **8 April 2026**, with the ban on insurer use of genetic test results taking effect on **8 October 2026**.

The legislation:
- **Prohibits life insurers** from using an individual's genetic test results — or the fact that a person has undergone or been recommended genetic testing — in decisions to offer cover or set terms and conditions
- **Amends the Disability Discrimination Act 1992** to align anti-discrimination law with the new ban
- Includes **civil and criminal penalties** for insurers who violate the ban
- **Does not bar** insurers from using information about diagnosed conditions or symptoms — only genetic test results and testing history

This legislation replaces a voluntary industry moratorium introduced in 2019, following the AGLIMMER study, which found that insurers were continuing to use genetic information in underwriting decisions despite the moratorium — a finding that drove the push for binding legislation.

The significance of this reform for population genomic screening is direct: fear that a positive genetic test would make an individual uninsurable was a documented barrier to participation in screening programs. The Monash research team explicitly linked the genetic discrimination ban to the expansion of life-saving DNA screening — the legislation removes the insurance penalty that had deterred some individuals from genomic screening participation. With the ban effective October 2026, this barrier is resolved.

### 6. Parallel Screening Programs: The Architecture Being Built

Population genomic screening is not a single program in Australia — it is an emerging ecology of overlapping programs targeting different life stages:

**Newborn bloodspot screening expansion ($64 million, 2022–2028):** The federal government has committed $64 million to expand newborn bloodspot screening, increasing the number of conditions screened and improving consistency across jurisdictions. An open call for new conditions closed January 2026.

**GenSCAN: Genomic Newborn Screening Consortium:** Six research studies exploring whole genome or exome sequencing in newborns are running in parallel under the GenSCAN consortium, funded through MRFF Genomics Health Futures Mission. These studies are testing whether genomic sequencing can expand newborn screening from the current ~25 conditions to over 500.

**Mackenzie's Mission: Reproductive Carrier Screening:** The $20 million Mackenzie's Mission research project screened over 9,000 couples for approximately 750 severe recessive genetic conditions. A June 2025 report from Australian Genomics concluded the program is "feasible, acceptable, cost-effective and ethically defensible" at national scale and recommended national implementation — a free, opt-in program available to all Australians of reproductive age. A national program has not yet been announced, but the evidence base for it mirrors that for adult preventive screening: the research is done; the policy decision is awaited.

**DNA Screen: Adult Preventive Genomic Screening (18–40 years):** As detailed above — pilot completed, translational phase funding sought, national program decision pending.

The combined picture is of a government investing heavily in the scaffolding for population genomic health — research programs, workforce, data infrastructure, intergovernmental frameworks — while making funding decisions program by program, and at a pace that lags the evidence.

### 7. The Genetic Counselling Workforce: The Non-Financial Constraint

Any funded population genomic screening program immediately confronts the same workforce constraint documented in Reports 097 and 098: Australia has approximately 432 clinical genetic counsellors (2022 workforce survey). The DNA Screen pilot's 97.9% follow-up acceptance rate is encouraging but also demanding — near-universal acceptance means near-universal counselling need.

Scaling to 100,000 test-positive participants (roughly 2% of the 5 million Australians aged 18–40 who would be eligible for adult preventive screening) would generate approximately 2,000 new confirmed carriers requiring genetic counselling, cascade testing notifications, and ongoing management — each year, as cohorts turn 18 and age through the eligible window. This is not the genetic counselling workforce Australia currently has.

The National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030's Strategic Priority 2 (Dynamic and Sustainable Genomics Ecosystem) explicitly addresses workforce capacity, but does not commit to specific training pipeline investments. Without a parallel workforce expansion plan, a funded population screening program risks creating a testing supply that outpaces counselling capacity — generating anxiety and incomplete clinical follow-through at scale.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The framework-without-funding problem.** The National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030 is a genuine policy achievement — intergovernmental coordination on genomics is not trivial. But a framework that commits to "embedding genomics into the health system" without specific funding for population screening programs, genetic counselling workforce, or MBS extensions may be less a decision than a decision-deferral. Researchers, clinicians, and patient advocates are watching whether the Framework translates into a 2026–27 Budget investment in the DNA Screen translational phase or a Mackenzie's Mission national program announcement.

**2. The three-program sequencing problem.** Should Australia fund newborn genomic screening, reproductive carrier screening, or adult preventive screening first? Each has an evidence base; each has a different clinical pathway and ethical framework; all compete for the same genetic counselling workforce, sequencing laboratory capacity, and clinical genetics infrastructure. The government has not publicly articulated a sequencing framework.

**3. The 2% detection rate and its implications.** DNA Screen's 2.0% detection rate across three combined conditions is used to argue for the program. But 2% detection means 98% of participants receive a reassurance result — a "negative" finding in 10 genes that may be clinically valuable but is not equivalent to a comprehensive cancer-risk assessment. As panel sizes expand (beyond BRCA1/2, MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2, LDLR/APOB/PCSK9), detection rates will rise — but so will variants of uncertain significance (VUS), which require genetic counselling that is even harder to deliver at scale than definitive findings.

**4. Cost-effectiveness vs. implementation cost.** An ICER of AU$20,734/QALY is compelling. But the upfront cost of a 50% uptake program for 8.3 million Australians — even at AU$200 per test — is AU$830 million in testing costs alone, before counselling, follow-up, cascade testing, or downstream clinical management. Health technology assessment in Australia evaluates cost-effectiveness, not affordability; cost-effectiveness does not guarantee that a program fits within the health budget envelope in any given year.

**5. The insurance reform runway.** The life insurance discrimination ban takes effect 8 October 2026. There will be an adjustment period as clinical genetics services, consumer advocates, and insurers implement the new framework. Whether and how the ban changes genomic screening participation rates in practice — and whether it triggers a surge in testing that the system is not prepared to absorb — is unknown.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) in Australia**: The third hereditary condition in the DNA Screen triad, FH affects approximately 1 in 250 Australians (~100,000 people) and is the leading genetic cause of early coronary artery disease. The FH Australasia Network has a cascade testing register; statins are PBS-listed and highly cost-effective; the payment and clinical systems are quite different from the cancer pathway. A natural next report to examine what FH's model reveals about managing hereditary cardiovascular risk at scale.

2. **Mackenzie's Mission and reproductive carrier screening: the case for a national program**: The June 2025 Australian Genomics report made the case for a universal, free reproductive carrier screening program. This report would examine what that program would cost, how it would be delivered, and what policy decision is required from Canberra.

3. **Genetic counselling workforce: the binding constraint on genomic medicine expansion**: With only 432 genetic counsellors across Australia and New Zealand, every expansion of genomic testing creates downstream pressure on counselling capacity. This is the single most consistently identified structural constraint. What does a genuine workforce expansion plan look like — training pipeline, scope of practice, telehealth models?

4. **Genomic newborn screening: the BabyScreen+ and GenSCAN programs**: What genomic conditions could be screened in newborns via sequencing? What are the ethical complexities (consent, incidental findings, predictive testing in minors)? What has Australia's citizens' jury on genomic newborn screening recommended?

5. **Liquid biopsy and circulating tumour DNA testing on MBS**: The MRFF 2026 grant Stream 1 specifically targets liquid biopsy. ctDNA tests for cancer monitoring and minimal residual disease are entering MSAC evaluation. This is an emerging and high-cost frontier in genomic oncology that could substantially increase genomic testing expenditure.
