# Obstetric Anaesthesia: The Invisible Workforce Bottleneck in Australia's Private Maternity Collapse

**Summary:** The closure of private maternity units across Australia is often framed as a story about obstetricians and midwives — but anaesthetists are an equally essential and increasingly scarce element of the equation. Without 24/7 anaesthetic cover, no private maternity unit can safely operate. As MBS rebates stagnate, public hospitals attract talent away from private practice, and workforce modelling forecasts a 5.7% national anaesthesia shortfall by 2032, obstetric anaesthesia is emerging as a structural constraint on what private maternity looks like in Australia by the end of the decade.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Yesterday's report (132) documented how PHI policy downgrades and falling private birth numbers are driving private maternity unit closures. This report examines one of the operational triggers those closures point to: the inability of private hospitals to maintain the around-the-clock anaesthetic rosters that safe maternity care requires.

---

## Background: Why Anaesthetists Are Non-Negotiable in Maternity Care

Anaesthetists occupy a unique position in maternity care. Unlike other specialists, their presence is not optional or episodic — they must be available at any hour for:

- **Labour epidurals**: The most common pain management intervention in private maternity care; demand is unpredictable and cannot be scheduled.
- **Emergency caesarean sections (Cat 1 and Cat 2)**: General or spinal anaesthesia required, typically within 15–30 minutes of a decision-to-deliver.
- **Postpartum haemorrhage management**: A leading cause of maternal mortality requiring rapid anaesthetic intervention.
- **Instrumental delivery complications and failed epidurals**: Requiring expert rescue anaesthesia.

This means a private maternity unit does not need one anaesthetist — it needs a *roster* that can guarantee cover around the clock, 365 days a year. That roster requirement is a fixed operational cost that does not scale down with declining birth volumes. A unit delivering 600 births per year has nearly the same 24/7 cover requirement as one delivering 1,200. As births fall — private births declined 22% between 2012 and 2023 — the per-birth cost of that fixed cover rises, making smaller units increasingly unviable.

## Key Findings

### 1. Workforce Modelling Projects a Growing Anaesthesia Shortfall

The Australian Society of Anaesthetists (ASA) commissioned HealthConsult to model the national anaesthesia workforce in 2024. Their modelling projected:

- Demand for anaesthetic services will increase **35.7%** between 2017 and 2032, driven by population ageing, procedure volume growth, and the rise of day surgery.
- The anaesthetist workforce will grow only **31.8%** over the same period, from 4,594 to 6,055 specialists.
- A **4% shortfall** between forecast workforce and required workforce is projected for **2027**.
- The shortfall widens to **5.7% by 2032**, equivalent to roughly **343 anaesthetists below demand**.
- Approximately **one-fifth of anaesthetists** are expected to retire within five years, based on National Health Workforce Dataset age profiles.
- Meeting demand would require individual anaesthetists to increase their workload by 2% over five years — but ASA member survey data and the NHWDS suggest this is unlikely to occur.

The ASA's recommendation: at least **50 additional anaesthesia trainees** per year above current projections. This is a training pipeline recommendation requiring Commonwealth investment in accredited training posts, which has not been acted upon as at October 2026.

*(Source: ASA HealthConsult Workforce Modelling Final Report, 2024; republished May 2025)*

### 2. Private Practice Is More Exposed to the Shortfall

The distribution of anaesthetists across sectors amplifies the private maternity risk:

- **44.1%** of anaesthetists work across both public and private sectors.
- **38.4%** work exclusively in private practice.
- **16.6%** work only in public hospitals.
- **Public hospitals are increasingly competitive** on pay, conditions, and workload predictability — particularly for younger anaesthetists with families, for whom unpredictable on-call rosters in private practice are unattractive.

As the overall workforce tightens, the private sector — which cannot match public sector conditions dollar-for-dollar — absorbs a disproportionate share of the shortage. Obstetric on-call in private hospitals is among the least attractive rosters: overnight, unpredictable, with high medicolegal exposure and no loading in the MBS structure for that unpredictability.

### 3. MBS Rebates Have Not Been Adequately Indexed for 20 Years

In April 2026, the ASA published a specialist fees survey of 1,121 anaesthetists (approximately 24% of those in private or mixed practice), conducted in January–February 2026. Key findings:

- Anaesthetists report charging on average **almost 30% less per unit** than their preferred rate — driven largely by no-gap and known-gap arrangements with private health insurers.
- The shortfall between preferred rate and actual rate is estimated at **$8.20 per unit** on average.
- **84% of anaesthetists** participate in no-gap or known-gap arrangements, accepting reduced fees to minimise patient out-of-pocket costs.
- The ASA attributes the growing gap between preferred and actual rates to **lack of adequate MBS indexation over approximately 20 years**.

The MBS anaesthesia billing structure uses a combination of *base units* (reflecting procedure complexity) and *time units* (reflecting duration). The schedule fee for each unit — estimated at approximately $94 per unit as of late 2022, and somewhat higher since — sets the baseline against which Medicare pays 75% for inpatients. Private health insurers cover the remainder up to their no-gap or known-gap thresholds, which in turn have not kept pace with what anaesthetists consider commercially viable rates.

For obstetric procedures specifically:
- Labour epidurals are billed under **Group T7** of the MBS (items 18216, 18219 for standard; 18226, 18227 for after-hours), which have their own base unit allocations.
- Caesarean section anaesthesia is billed as a surgical anaesthetic item with complexity and time-based loading.
- Out-of-pocket costs for patients not covered by no-gap arrangements can range from **$500–$1,000** for an epidural, and higher for caesarean section anaesthesia where the procedure runs long or is categorised as complex.

*(Sources: ASA Fee Survey April 2026; Services Australia MBS Anaesthesia Billing Guide; Finder cost of childbirth guide)*

### 4. Anaesthetist Shortages as a Direct Trigger for Maternity Unit Closures

The connection between anaesthesia roster failure and unit closure is increasingly explicit in hospital announcements:

- **Darwin Private Hospital** (Healthscope, closed maternity June 2025): Its FAQ cited "limited access to paediatricians, obstetricians, midwives, and *at times anaesthetists*, to cover on-call and in-patient services safely" as one of the operational reasons for closure. The NT had no remaining private obstetrician by early 2026.
- **Hobart Private Hospital** (Healthscope, closure August 2025): Workforce shortages and declining births cited.
- **St Vincent's Private Toowoomba** (October 2025): Closed its special care nursery for approximately a week due to staffing shortages; NASOG president described this as a nationwide pattern.
- **Western Australia**: St John of God Mount Lawley, St John of God Bunbury, and Glengarry Private Hospital all closed private maternity units in the two years to mid-2024, with AMA WA linking closures to rebates failing to keep pace with costs.

A 2024 MJA Insight opinion piece stated bluntly: *"Anaesthetists and paediatricians are essential and must be available around the clock. Difficulties in securing enough of these specialists to work in private hospital maternity rosters is recognised as one of the drivers of private maternity hospital closures."*

In Queensland, state government data (to be read with context, as politically partisan) reported **anaesthetic vacancy rates of 17.9%** in regional facilities, compared to 14.2% in obstetrics and 11.5% in midwifery — suggesting anaesthesia is the tightest of the three essential maternity workforces in regional areas.

*(Sources: Darwin Private Hospital FAQ; AMA media release February 2025; MJA Insight July 2024; Queensland Government media statement)*

### 5. The Roster Economics Problem

A private maternity unit of modest scale typically requires a minimum of **4–6 anaesthetists** in a functioning on-call rotation to ensure 24/7 cover without burnout. In regional and rural areas — where the Modified Monash model (covered in report 012) recognises existing GP and specialist shortages — recruiting and retaining even 4 anaesthetists is functionally impossible when:

- Public hospital on-call pays more reliably with better superannuation and leave entitlements.
- Private maternity generates lower case volumes than the metropolitan equivalent.
- The MBS unit value does not carry a loading for the on-call nature of the work.
- Rising medical indemnity premiums (covered in report 128) apply to anaesthetists in obstetrics as a high-medicolegal exposure specialty.

Private hospital operators cannot solve this through wages alone when the MBS structure limits revenue per case. And unlike public hospitals, private facilities cannot cross-subsidise obstetric anaesthesia from high-volume elective surgery in the same way a large public tertiary centre might.

### 6. The 2026 Senate and Parliamentary Landscape

In March 2026, the ASA submitted to the Senate Community Affairs Legislation Committee on private health insurance legislation, calling for coordinated action on specialist access and affordability — specifically including a reform to anaesthesia indexation. In August 2026, the ASA submitted separately on informed financial consent and split-billing practices, which affect how anaesthetists communicate costs to patients.

Neither submission had received a government response as at October 2026. The Department of Health's Private Health Sector Reform Consultation Paper 1 (July 2026, submissions closed August 2026) named maternity and mental health as the two most acute viability pressures in the private system — but did not specifically address anaesthesia workforce or MBS rebate adequacy for obstetric procedures.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**The indexation standoff:** The ASA argues that MBS rebates for anaesthesia have been inadequately indexed for ~20 years and that this is the root cause of gap fees and workforce drain from private practice. Treasury and the Department of Health have not publicly accepted that frame. The 2020 Medicare Benefits Schedule Review Taskforce's Anaesthesia Clinical Committee identified scope for rationalisation of some items, but did not recommend substantial fee increases. Who is correct is not merely technical: it turns on whether the MBS is meant to fully fund private specialist services, or only to anchor a mixed-funding system in which PHI and private fees fill the gap.

**The chicken-and-egg of training posts:** ANZCA accredits training posts, and most obstetric anaesthesia training occurs in large public or teaching hospitals. If trainees follow public-sector jobs into their career, private maternity never builds the pipeline it needs. But if private maternity closes, there are fewer obstetric cases — and potentially fewer training opportunities for future anaesthetists seeking experience in obstetrics.

**Who absorbs the transfer births?** When a private maternity unit closes, those births largely transfer to the public system. But public obstetric anaesthesia services are themselves not unlimited. The NSW Health workforce modelling page (updated 2019) relied on data from 2010 — meaning there is no publicly available current modelling of public-sector obstetric anaesthesia capacity. The capacity to absorb transfers is essentially unknown at the system level.

**Equity and geographic dimension:** Rural and regional areas face the steepest workforce pressures. Patients in regional areas who previously had access to private maternity (with private health insurance giving them choice of provider) are being defaulted to the public system, but the public system in those areas may itself be stretched. The Modified Monash framework provides GP loading payments — it has no analogue for private hospital specialist rosters.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Paediatric and neonatal workforce in private maternity**: The twin workforce gap — anaesthetists AND neonatologists/paediatricians are both required 24/7 for private maternity — and whether one is a binding constraint before the other.
2. **PHI hospital contracting reform: floor rates and service continuity clauses**: Whether private health funds should be required to guarantee minimum service continuity under their contracts with private hospitals, particularly for maternity.
3. **ANZCA training pipeline reform and obstetric anaesthesia case requirements**: Whether the accreditation framework for anaesthesia training is producing enough specialists with obstetric confidence.
4. **International models for funding 24/7 obstetric anaesthetic cover**: How the UK (NICE/NHS anaesthesia workforce model), Canada, and New Zealand fund maternity anaesthesia differently — and whether a direct Commonwealth payment for 24/7 obstetric on-call cover is feasible outside the MBS structure.
5. **The public system capacity question**: State-by-state mapping of public obstetric anaesthesia capacity and the plausibility of absorbing ongoing private-to-public birth transfers.
