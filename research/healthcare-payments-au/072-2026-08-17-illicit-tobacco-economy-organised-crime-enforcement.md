# The Illicit Tobacco Economy: Australia's Black Market Crisis and the Collapse of Price-Based Tobacco Control

**Report 072 — 2026-08-17**

**Summary:** Australia's tobacco excise policy — the world's steepest, pushing legal packs to $50–$60 — has produced an extraordinary and self-defeating outcome: the illicit tobacco market now supplies an estimated 50–80% of all tobacco consumed in Australia, has collapsed excise revenue by more than $8 billion since 2019, and is fuelling a wave of organised crime violence (200+ attacks in Melbourne by early 2026) while simultaneously destroying the price mechanism that was the primary driver of smoking reduction. The Government has responded with a new enforcement agency, Operation PRINTWALL, and the Combatting Illicit Tobacco Bill 2026 — but enforcement alone has not stemmed the tide, and public health advocates are urgently debating whether the tax model itself must be reconsidered.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 071 examined the First Nations tobacco target — the 27% by 2030 goal — and found that illicit tobacco is now penetrating remote community supply chains, potentially undermining the excise price signal that has been one of the few effective tobacco control tools in those communities. This report examines the illicit market in depth: its scale, the organised crime networks driving it, the fiscal consequences, and whether enforcement or tax reform can resolve it.

---

## Background: The Excise Escalation That Built the Black Market

Australia's tobacco excise has the distinction of being among the highest in the world — a deliberate public health policy sustained across successive governments since the 2010s. The structure involves:

- **Per-stick excise:** As of March 2025, AUD $28.06 per 20-pack (approximately $1.40 per cigarette), with the tax component comprising more than 80% of the retail price
- **Biannual indexation:** To Average Weekly Ordinary Times Earnings (AWOTE), applied 1 March and 1 September each year
- **Additional 5% above-indexation surcharge:** Introduced from 2023, stacked on top of AWOTE indexation to accelerate price increases

The result is a legal pack of 20 cigarettes now retailing at approximately $50–$60 in major cities, among the highest prices in the world. The policy goal was explicit: price-induced cessation. And for two decades, it worked. From 1991 to 2020, daily smoking prevalence fell from approximately 27% to 11%, with the steepest declines coinciding with the sharpest excise increases.

But from 2019 onward, something changed.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Market Has Inverted: Illicit is Now the Majority

The scale of the illicit tobacco market in Australia is, by any measure, extraordinary. Multiple independent sources now converge on the same finding:

| Source | Estimate of illicit market share (tobacco) | Year of estimate |
|--------|-------------------------------------------|------------------|
| ITEC Commissioner Report | ~50% of all cigarettes | 2024–25 |
| FTI Consulting | 50% of cigarette consumption | June 2025 |
| ABS wastewater-based model | ~80% of all nicotine consumed | 2025 |
| National Drug Strategy Household Survey | 34% of current smokers report recent illicit use (up from 8.8% in 2016) | 2025 |

The ABS figure — 80% of all nicotine consumption from illicit sources — is the most striking. It incorporates both illicit tobacco and the largely unregulated illicit vaping market, which the ITEC Commissioner estimated accounts for **95.7% of the entire Australian e-cigarette market** operating outside health and regulatory safeguards. When legal tobacco excise revenue is compared against the volume of illicit product being seized, the ITEC Commissioner estimated excise evaded in 2024–25 alone at **AUD $7.7 billion to $11.8 billion** — remarkably, similar in magnitude to the total excise paid on all legal tobacco imports in the same year.

The price differential driving this market is stark. A legal pack of 20 cigarettes costs approximately $50. Illicit cigarettes (factory-made counterfeit or foreign-branded products smuggled without excise) sell for approximately $10–$20 per pack, a 60–80% discount that no price-sensitive smoker can be expected to ignore.

### 2. The Fiscal Damage: $8.5 Billion in Lost Revenue and a $22.3 Billion Budget Hole

The revenue consequences of the illicit market are catastrophic for the Commonwealth budget:

- **Tobacco excise revenue (2019):** approximately $16 billion
- **Tobacco excise revenue (2025):** approximately $7.4 billion
- **Revenue fall:** approximately $8.5 billion — occurring simultaneously with excise rate increases of nearly 50% over the same period

This is a textbook Laffer Curve outcome: as the tax rate on legal tobacco increased beyond a threshold, substitution into the untaxed illicit market reduced total revenue faster than the rate increase could generate it.

The 2025–26 Budget incorporated a material downgrade in tobacco excise receipts of **$6.9 billion over five years to 2028–29**. The Mid-Year Economic Fiscal Outlook confirmed the trend has continued, with the Canberra Times reporting a five-year forecast revision of **$22.3 billion** — from $44.9 billion expected two years previously to $22.6 billion — reflecting the government's admission that it no longer expects legal tobacco revenue to recover.

At $156.7 million in new enforcement funding (2025–26 Budget), enforcement is consuming a fraction of the revenue it was meant to recover.

### 3. Organised Crime: The Melbourne Tobacco Wars and the Syndicate Structure

The illicit tobacco trade in Australia has not emerged organically from individual smugglers. It is controlled, in large part, by transnational organised crime syndicates — with the violence that accompanies that control.

**The Melbourne Tobacco Wars** are the most visible manifestation. By the start of 2026, the running tally of attacks on tobacco shops — firebombings, arson, extortion, shootings — had passed **200 incidents**. Key features:

- Attacks are mostly targeting small retailers as part of turf battles between competing syndicates over control of the retail distribution network for illicit product
- The most destructive single firebombing to date (in Richmond, 2026) damaged a dozen businesses
- Victoria Police's Gang Crime Squad (formerly Taskforce Lunar) has executed **more than 350 raids**, made **at least 212 arrests**, and seized more than **AUD $50 million** in cash, illicit tobacco, cigarettes, and vapes
- The ABC's *Four Corners* investigation (March 2025, "Tobacco Wars") documented the syndicate structure in detail

The syndicate architecture, as described by the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission (ACIC) and the ITEC Commissioner, has the following features:

- **Most syndicates operate diversified criminal businesses** — tobacco profits fund other activities including firearms, drugs, and money laundering
- **International sourcing:** The primary import routes are from China, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Eastern Europe, with product frequently misdeclared (e.g. labelled as food or consumer goods) to evade ABF detection
- **Outlaw motorcycle gangs (OMCGs):** Intelligence links OMCG members to distribution networks in multiple states; two OMCG-linked individuals were sentenced for importing and distributing 10.6 million illicit cigarettes with AUD $13 million in estimated duty evasion
- **Diaspora criminal networks:** In what has become a politically sensitive element of the enforcement story, the ACIC and media investigations have identified Middle Eastern organised crime networks as significant players; one key figure (deported in 2023 after an eight-year sentence for heroin trafficking) is alleged to have continued overseeing smuggling operations from Dubai

The connection between tobacco trafficking and other serious organised crime is a central concern of enforcement agencies. The Illicit Tobacco and E-cigarette (ITEC) Commissioner, Amber Shuhyta, has described tobacco as functioning as a "gateway" product for criminal networks — the legal status of tobacco (versus drugs) makes the supply chain easier to establish, the margins are extraordinary, and the same networks can then be adapted for other contraband.

### 4. Enforcement Response: Operation PRINTWALL and the ITEC Commissioner

The Government's enforcement response has been substantial, if overdue.

**The ITEC Commissioner:** Australia established the Illicit Tobacco and E-cigarette Commissioner as a dedicated coordination role to bring together the Australian Border Force (ABF), the Australian Federal Police (AFP), the ACIC, state and territory police, and revenue agencies. The Commissioner's inaugural annual report (2024–25, published December 2025) is the first comprehensive government assessment of the market.

**Operation PRINTWALL (ABF):** Launched December 2025, this is the ABF's largest sustained illicit tobacco interdiction operation. Results to mid-2026:

- Between December 2025 and 31 March 2026: **998.5 tonnes** of illicit cigarettes and loose-leaf tobacco seized
- Within Operation PRINTWALL, one week in April 2026 produced **87 tonnes** in a single week — the largest week of seizures in the operation's history
- By mid-2026, total seizures within the operation surpassed **one kilotonne** of illicit tobacco
- Total evaded excise duty intercepted (across the border continuum): over **AUD $3.5 billion**
- Pre-border interceptions (in cooperation with international partners): 35.6 million cigarette sticks, 257.4 kg loose-leaf tobacco, 500,000 vapes stopped before reaching Australia

**However, the seizures, while impressive in headline terms, must be contextualised.** The ITEC Commissioner estimated excise evaded at $7.7–$11.8 billion in a single year. At $3.5 billion in evaded excise detected over the life of Operation PRINTWALL, seizures are intercepting a significant but far from controlling share of the illicit flow. The market continues to operate at near-majority scale.

### 5. The Legislative Response: Combatting Illicit Tobacco Bill 2026

The Combatting Illicit Tobacco Bill 2026, introduced by Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke and passed through the House in mid-2026, represents the most significant legislative escalation of anti-illicit-tobacco penalties in Australian history:

- **Penalty increases:** Up to **15 years imprisonment** for the most serious offences (large-scale importing, manufacturing, or distributing)
- **New offences:** Specific offences for large-scale illicit tobacco activity linked to organised crime
- **Expanded powers:** Enhanced law enforcement tools including telephone interception warrants for serious tobacco offences (previously unavailable)
- **Proceeds of crime:** Expanded unexplained wealth and proceeds of crime tools; confiscation of cash, real estate, vehicles, cryptocurrency linked to illicit tobacco profits
- **Equipment offences:** Increased penalties for possession of equipment for illegal tobacco manufacture

The Bill's penalty framework is informed directly by the ITEC Commissioner's 2024–25 Report, which recommended stronger consequences as a deterrent to syndicate involvement.

### 6. The Public Health Paradox: Illicit Tobacco Destroys What Excise Built

The most significant consequence of the illicit market for healthcare payments is not fiscal — it is the destruction of the public health mechanism that tobacco excise was designed to operate.

**The price mechanism is broken.** The entire logic of high tobacco excise is that price increase → reduced affordability → smoking cessation or prevention of uptake. This chain breaks when a readily available substitute at 20–40% of the legal price is accessible at the same retail locations.

The public health consequences are multi-dimensional:

1. **Cessation effect neutralised.** Smokers who cannot afford legal cigarettes do not necessarily quit — they switch to illicit product. The NDSS Household Survey confirms 34% of current smokers now use illicit tobacco. At a price point of $10–$20 per pack, the economic pressure to quit evaporates.

2. **Youth initiation.** Illicit tobacco products frequently lack plain packaging, carry no health warnings on the sticks (required for all legal products), may have prohibited additives (flavourings that make them more palatable to first-time users), and have no minimum age restriction enforced at point of sale. The PHAA, in its submission to the NSW inquiry (August 2025), identified the illicit retail network as a youth initiation pathway comparable to the vaping access problem documented in the vaping policy reports.

3. **Equity and disadvantage.** The populations most affected by illicit tobacco availability are those for whom legal price increases have the greatest impact — low-income, socioeconomically disadvantaged smokers. These are the same populations who were the target of the price-induced cessation strategy. The market has effectively created a tiered system: wealthier smokers can afford legal product or afford to quit; poorer smokers switch to illicit product and continue smoking. Research by Professor Ron Borland (published in *Addiction*, 2025) found that heavy, dependent, and disadvantaged smokers are disproportionately represented among illicit tobacco users — the people least equipped to quit are the people most exposed to a readily available cheap alternative.

4. **Remote First Nations communities** (documented in Report 071) face a specific risk: if illicit supply chains reach remote community stores, the excise price sensitivity documented in research (5.8–8.2% decline in tobacco sales following 12.5% price rises, 2016–2018) will be negated for the hardest-to-reach population with the highest smoking rates.

5. **Wastewater-based surveillance.** The ABS has pioneered wastewater epidemiology to measure nicotine consumption, which bypasses self-report limitations in household surveys. The ABS finding that illicit sources account for 80% of all nicotine consumed does not simply mean smoking rates are higher than survey data suggest — it means the entire regulatory, payment, and public health infrastructure built around legal tobacco and vaping is reaching only 20% of the nicotine market.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Laffer Curve Problem — Can More Enforcement Substitute for a Broken Price Signal?

The government's position is that the tax level is correct and the problem is insufficient enforcement. Critics (including the e61 Institute in a widely discussed 2025 paper, "Chop, chop: Time to cut tobacco excise rates?") argue that excise has crossed the threshold at which increasing rates reduces rather than raises revenue and health outcomes — and that enforcement cannot seal a market of this size. The public health community is strongly resistant to any excise reduction, arguing that it would lock in smokers and reward the criminal networks. This debate has no clean resolution.

### Tension 2: The ITEC Commissioner's Scope — Coordination vs. Operational Authority

The ITEC Commissioner is a coordination role, not an operational agency. The Commissioner cannot direct the ABF, AFP, or state police — only convene and report. Critics argue that this structure is insufficient for a market of this size and that Australia needs either a dedicated anti-illicit-tobacco agency with operational powers or a formal lead-agency model with accountability. The Commissioner's 2024–25 report recommended stronger penalties and better data-sharing; it did not recommend structural change to the enforcement architecture.

### Tension 3: The Vaping-Tobacco Nexus

The illicit market is not only a tobacco problem. The ITEC Commissioner found 95.7% of the e-cigarette market operating outside regulatory safeguards. The same criminal distribution networks that sell illicit tobacco are also selling illicit vapes, often from the same retail premises. Policy responses treating tobacco and vaping as separate regulatory problems are structurally mismatched with a market that treats them as the same product category distributed through the same channels.

### Tension 4: Border vs. Domestic Production

ABF seizures focus on the border. But there is increasing evidence of **domestic tobacco production** — illicit leaf-growing operations and on-shore manufacturing — that bypasses the border entirely. Domestic production is harder to detect and intercept than imported product, and the supply chain for domestically produced illicit tobacco (leaf cultivation → processing → distribution) is largely invisible to border-focused enforcement operations.

### Tension 5: The First Nations Remote Community Exposure

As detailed in Report 071, illicit tobacco's penetration into remote First Nations communities — where smoking rates are 52–53% and have not declined in a decade — represents the intersection of two crises. Price sensitivity is real in these communities (legal price increases did reduce store tobacco sales), but that price signal is now being undermined by cheap illicit supply. Neither the TIS health promotion program nor the PBS cessation pharmacotherapy pathway was designed to address illicit tobacco access in remote communities. There is no program, no funding stream, and no enforcement reach specifically targeting illicit tobacco in remote First Nations community supply chains.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The e-cigarette regulatory collapse: Australia's prescription vaping model, the 95.7% illicit market, and what reform looks like.** (From both the ITEC Commissioner finding and the UK NHS vaping comparison flagged in Reports 070–071.) The vaping market's near-total illicit operation is a distinct policy story — its own thread.

2. **Tobacco-related disease burden and the healthcare cost of smoking in 2026.** The COPD, lung cancer, and cardiovascular disease cost to the hospital and primary care system quantifies what cessation investment is actually worth — and what the illicit market's price-signal destruction is costing in future health spend.

3. **First Nations youth vaping as a new initiation pathway.** The 2022–23 NATSIHS found 22% of young First Nations people had used vapes. The same illicit vaping channels that supply the general population are also reaching Indigenous youth. Whether vaping is substituting for tobacco or layering on top of it is unresolved.

4. **Domestic illicit tobacco production.** Border seizures capture imported product; domestically grown and processed illicit tobacco is a growing and under-measured problem. The regulatory and enforcement architecture for domestic illicit production is largely absent.

5. **The health insurance and healthcare payment system interaction with smoke-related disease.** Tobacco-related disease (COPD, lung cancer, CVD) is a major driver of hospitalisation costs, specialist referrals, and PBS pharmaceutical expenditure. Quantifying this would complete the circle from excise policy to healthcare system cost.
