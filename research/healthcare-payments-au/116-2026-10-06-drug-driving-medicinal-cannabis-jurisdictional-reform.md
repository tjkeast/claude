# Drug-Driving Law Reform: Australia's Diverging Jurisdictional Experiment on Medicinal Cannabis and Roads

**One-line summary:** As of October 2026, Australia's six states and two territories have taken fundamentally different approaches to whether patients legally prescribed THC-containing cannabis can drive — ranging from NSW's new 50 ng/mL threshold scheme to Queensland doubling its zero-tolerance fines — creating a patchwork that is simultaneously a road safety debate, an equity crisis, and a direct consequence of the $1 billion SAS-B prescription economy.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 115 documented the three-way collision between medicinal cannabis prescriptions, workplace drug testing, and workers' compensation — noting that drug-driving charges were one of the key legal exposure points for patients. That thread is now a major policy battleground in its own right.

---

## Background

Australia legalised medicinal cannabis prescription from February 2016. Within a decade, TGA's SAS-B pathway was processing 177,762 approvals per year, and the total national patient base had reached approximately 350,000 active patients. According to the TGA and prescriber data, roughly 70% of those patients have some THC in their prescribed medication — meaning approximately 245,000 Australians are receiving a lawfully prescribed medicine that makes it a criminal offence to drive in most jurisdictions.

The fundamental architecture of drug-driving law in every Australian state and territory is presence-based, not impairment-based. Unlike alcohol, where a 0.05 g/100mL blood-alcohol concentration has a validated relationship to crash risk, no equivalent threshold has been established for THC. The detection of THC in oral fluid or blood indicates prior use, not current impairment. Yet until 2026 every jurisdiction prosecuted solely on detection.

The mismatch has generated an unusual legal situation: a patient prescribed opioids for chronic pain may be able to rely on a statutory defence to drug-driving charges (in some circumstances); a patient prescribed THC for the same condition, in the same jurisdiction, generally cannot. That asymmetry has been a flashpoint for patient groups, legal advocates, and health researchers for years.

In 2026, the jurisdictions diverged sharply.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Problem: 245,000 Prescribed Patients Cannot Drive

The clearest measure of the system failure is patient-level: approximately 350,000 Australians hold active medicinal cannabis prescriptions, 70% with THC-containing products, and all of them face potential prosecution for driving in most jurisdictions regardless of whether they are impaired.

A survey of more than 6,000 medicinal cannabis patients (Montu, 2025) found that 91% reported increased anxiety around driving as a direct result of drug-driving laws. Patients described being unable to drive to their GP or pharmacy to collect the medication itself. Many reported being forced to choose between continuing prescriptions and maintaining employment that required driving.

The Cannabis as Medicine Survey 2022–23 (CAMS-22), published by UNSW's National Drug and Alcohol Research Centre, found that 55.5% of respondents said the presence of roadside drug testing deterred them from driving after consuming medicinal cannabis. A further 28.3% reported they drove anyway, with 69.1% of those doing so because they believed themselves to be unimpaired.

The policy implication is significant: zero-tolerance laws are simultaneously failing to stop impaired driving (by deterring compliant patients while creating incentives for those who feel safe to drive regardless) and actively removing legal access from compliant patients.

### 2. The Science: THC Concentration Is a Poor Proxy for Impairment

The legislative rationale for THC thresholds depends on the assumption that blood or oral-fluid THC concentration correlates with impairment. Research has consistently challenged that assumption.

The Lambert Initiative for Cannabinoid Therapeutics at the University of Sydney (2021) conducted a systematic review of all available studies on the relationship between driving performance and THC concentration. Its core finding: blood and oral-fluid THC concentrations are "relatively poor or inconsistent indicators of cannabis-induced impairment." Critically, the relationship differs between experienced and naive users:

- **Naive/occasional users**: Can be substantially impaired at relatively low THC concentrations
- **Regular/medicinal users**: May show minimal impairment at high oral-fluid THC concentrations, as tolerance develops specifically for psychomotor functions

This means any single numeric threshold will simultaneously over-identify unimpaired experienced users and under-identify impaired naive users. The Lambert submission to the NSW Legislative Council inquiry stated that blood and saliva tests "cannot tell us whether or not an individual is impaired."

THC's fat-soluble pharmacokinetics compound the problem. Chronic users can produce positive oral-fluid tests for 24–72 hours after last use; urine tests can detect metabolites for several weeks. A medicinal cannabis patient who last used their medication 36 hours ago and is completely unimpaired may still test positive at roadside.

### 3. NSW: Reform Passed September 2026 — A Threshold Scheme With Significant Limits

The Road Transport Legislation Amendment (Medical Cannabis and Driving Offences) Bill 2026 passed both houses of NSW Parliament on 17–18 September 2026, awaiting assent and commencement by proclamation (expected late 2026 or early 2027).

The scheme's key mechanics:

**The threshold:** Eligible registered patients will not face automatic drug-presence penalties provided a laboratory test finds their oral fluid contains less than **50 nanograms per millilitre (ng/mL)** of THC. The blood equivalent is **3.5 ng/mL**. Above those levels, a warning applies for a first or second detection within two years; subsequent detections or findings of actual impairment attract normal penalties.

**The 24-hour roadside ban:** Even registered patients face an immediate 24-hour driving ban upon a positive tongue-swab result. The ban is lifted if the confirmatory laboratory sample comes back below the threshold. This creates a practical deterrent — patients who know they may test positive must plan around potential road-side prohibition even under the reformed scheme.

**The registration requirement:** Eligible drivers must register with Transport for NSW, provide a valid prescription, and complete an online education program about cannabis and driving safety. The registration system is not yet operational as of the legislation's passage; it will be implemented "late 2026 or early 2027."

**Excluded populations:** The scheme explicitly does not cover:
- Learner and provisional (P-plate) licence holders
- Heavy vehicle and commercial licence holders
- Professional drivers

The commercial driver exclusion is particularly significant. An estimated 1.6 million Australians hold heavy vehicle licences. Any medicinal cannabis patient in this group — including many in regional and rural areas using cannabis for chronic pain associated with manual labour — receives no protection under the NSW reform.

**The equity concern about the threshold itself:** Given the Lambert Initiative's finding that THC concentration does not reliably indicate impairment, the 50 ng/mL threshold is somewhat arbitrary from a scientific standpoint. It represents a pragmatic compromise rather than evidence-based policy. Patients who use cannabis the day before driving may still exceed it; patients who are actually impaired may fall below it.

Drug driving offences now make up approximately 8% of all NSW court matters, with the highest per-capita detection rates concentrated in regional areas: Broken Hill, Cootamundra-Gundagai, Snowy Monaro, and Lismore. Between 2019 and 2023, NSW Police conducted 677,494 roadside drug tests.

### 4. Queensland: Zero Tolerance Doubled Down

While NSW was legislating reform, Queensland moved in the opposite direction. The Transport and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2026, passed June 2026 and effective **1 December 2026**, doubles the maximum fine for drug-driving from 14 to 28 penalty units (approximately $4,000 at current Queensland rates).

Queensland maintains explicitly that a valid medicinal cannabis prescription provides no defence. The Alcohol and Drug Foundation's Queensland state manager publicly warned the changes would "negatively impact people using legal medicinal cannabis prescriptions," noting that patients could face fines, licence disqualification, and aggravated penalties "simply for taking their medication as directed."

The Queensland government's position is that until an impairment-based roadside testing tool is validated, zero-tolerance is the only defensible road-safety framework. This argument has force at the margins — but it also ignores the existing evidence that zero-tolerance does not correlate with impairment detection, and it creates a direct confrontation with the healthcare system that has authorised those prescriptions.

Queensland's approach also creates an incentive asymmetry: with higher fines and no prescription defence, patients face greater financial risk from compliance (not driving while medicated) than from non-compliance (driving when they judge themselves unimpaired). This may perversely increase the rate of driving-while-medicated among Queensland patients who make an independent impairment judgement.

### 5. Victoria: A Trial Underway But Stalled; Magistrate Discretion From March 2025

Victoria's approach is more nuanced but slower to resolve. From **1 March 2025**, Victorian magistrates gained discretion to determine whether a driver was actually impaired at the wheel — a partial shift away from pure presence-based enforcement, though not a full defence. In practice, this provides uncertain and variable protection depending on the magistrate and the evidence presented.

Victoria also funded an 18-month closed-circuit driving trial led by Swinburne University, backed by a **$4.9 million state grant**, commencing August 2024. The trial tests steering, braking, and speed control with patients prescribed cannabis for sleep disorders, chronic pain, or mental health conditions.

However, the trial has encountered delays. The government will not receive Swinburne's report until **2027**. More critically, due to budget constraints, the trial scope was reduced and will **not** include evaluation of impairment-testing technologies — the most policy-relevant component. Without validated impairment technology, even Victoria's 2027 reform pathway remains constrained.

### 6. Tasmania: The Pre-Existing Defence Model

Tasmania is the only Australian jurisdiction that has consistently provided a legal defence for prescribed patients. Under the Poisons Act 1971, driving with detectable THC is an offence *unless* the product was "obtained and administered in accordance with the Act." In practice, this means a valid medicinal cannabis prescription is a defence — subject to demonstrating the medicine was taken as prescribed.

Tasmania's approach is the model most consistent with how other prescribed medicines are handled. Patients prescribed opioids, benzodiazepines, or other centrally acting substances face a similar framework: the prescription is a defence against the presence finding, but actual impairment remains a separate and prosecutable offence.

Tasmania's model is not uncontroversial — critics argue it relies on prescription compliance checks rather than any independent assessment of driving safety. But it has not generated measurable evidence of increased crash rates among medicinal cannabis users relative to other jurisdictions.

### 7. ACT: Technology Argument Used to Maintain Zero Tolerance

The ACT Government declined in **March 2026** to introduce a prescription defence, citing the absence of a reliable roadside impairment test. The government acknowledged that detecting THC "is not the same as proving a driver is impaired" — an important concession — but argued that without validated impairment technology, any prescription exemption creates an unacceptable evidentiary gap.

The ACT's position is arguably the most honest articulation of the underlying policy tension: the zero-tolerance framework exists not because THC detection equals impairment, but because there is no better instrument. The logical implication is that impairment-based reform requires validated technology first.

### 8. The Equity Dimension: Aboriginal Australians and Regional Patients Bear the Disproportionate Burden

Drug-driving enforcement in Australia is not evenly distributed. Harm Reduction Australia's *Drive Change NSW* report (February 2025) documented that Aboriginal Australians are charged with cannabis-related offences at **8 times the rate** of non-Aboriginal people in Victoria and **2.5 times the rate** in NSW. Drug-driving detection rates per 100,000 population are substantially higher in regional and remote areas than in metropolitan centres.

The equity implications for medicinal cannabis are significant. Chronic pain, mental health conditions, and sleep disorders — the three leading indications for SAS-B cannabis prescriptions — are more prevalent in remote and regional Australia, and in First Nations communities. The patients most likely to be prescribed cannabis are, in many cases, the same patients most heavily surveilled by drug-driving enforcement.

Additionally, regional and rural patients often have no public transport alternative to driving. In urban areas, a patient who cannot drive due to zero-tolerance laws can use public transport to access their GP, pharmacy, or specialist. In rural and remote areas, that option often does not exist. The enforcement cost therefore falls more heavily on patients with fewer alternatives — a classic equity inversion.

### 9. The Commercial Driver Exclusion: A Workforce Gap With Direct Payment-System Consequences

The NSW scheme's exclusion of commercial and heavy vehicle licence holders leaves a large, economically significant patient population with no protection. Truck drivers, bus drivers, and other heavy vehicle operators who have been prescribed medicinal cannabis for chronic back pain, sleep disorders, or PTSD — conditions common in transport and manual industries — remain subject to full zero-tolerance enforcement and face immediate licence loss on detection.

This connects directly to the workers' compensation system documented in Report 115. A heavy vehicle driver whose chronic pain was caused by a workplace injury, prescribed medicinal cannabis by a workers' comp-approved treating practitioner, and required to cease driving to comply with drug-driving law, has a measurable productivity and income loss that may be compensable — or may not be, depending on whether the insurer agreed to fund the cannabis in the first place. The legal boundaries remain unresolved.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Is the 50 ng/mL threshold evidence-based or legally defensible?**
The Lambert Initiative's evidence suggests that no oral fluid threshold reliably correlates with impairment. The NSW threshold is a pragmatic compromise, not a validated safety instrument. Courts may face challenges to prosecutions above 50 ng/mL where defendants can show they were unimpaired.

**2. Will the NSW scheme survive its administrative complexity?**
The registration, education, and prescription verification requirements create a bureaucratic burden. Evidence from similar overseas frameworks (Netherlands, Israel) suggests that uptake of formal registration schemes for medicinal cannabis driving exemptions is often well below the eligible patient population — particularly among older or less digitally engaged patients.

**3. The commercial driver exclusion creates a second-tier access problem.**
Medicinal cannabis is approved precisely because it can be effective for conditions that make other prescribed medicines difficult to tolerate — opioids with addiction risk, benzodiazepines with cognitive impairment, antidepressants with performance effects. Commercial drivers excluded from the NSW scheme may face a de facto choice between their occupation and their treatment.

**4. Queensland's escalating penalties may produce the opposite of their intended effect.**
Higher fines for patients who cannot afford to comply with zero tolerance (because they have no transport alternative) will deepen financial hardship without deterring driving-while-medicated by those who feel unimpaired. The ADF has explicitly raised this concern.

**5. Victoria's trial without impairment technology evaluation is a missed opportunity.**
The decision not to fund impairment technology evaluation within the Swinburne trial means Victoria cannot advance to evidence-based reform before 2027, and even then may lack the technical foundation to act.

**6. SA, WA, and NT have yet to announce reform pathways.**
Three jurisdictions with significant rural/remote populations and First Nations communities — precisely the populations most affected by the equity inversion documented above — have not publicly committed to any reform timeline.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Impairment-based drug testing technology**: What roadside instruments are under development to distinguish cannabis impairment from mere cannabis presence? The US (NIDA, DRUID app), Israel (Nanit), and Victoria's own trial are the key reference points. This is the technical precondition for national reform.

2. **Commercial driver health and safe work exemptions**: Heavy vehicle drivers prescribed centrally acting medicines (opioids, benzodiazepines, cannabis) navigate a complex overlap between CASA/NHVR standards, workplace WHS law, and the absence of drug-driving defences. A dedicated report on this intersection would extend directly from both Reports 115 and 116.

3. **SA, WA, and NT drug-driving postures**: Three jurisdictions with significant rural/remote populations that have not yet moved, and where the equity burden is most acute. Their political calculus is different from eastern states and worth examining in detail.

4. **Cannabis and road crash data**: Does any Australian jurisdiction have evidence that medicinal cannabis prescription has increased crash rates? The research literature is contested; the Australian data gap is significant and directly relevant to whether reform is safe.

5. **The opioid driving defence asymmetry**: Prescribed opioid users have a partial statutory defence in some jurisdictions (they can argue the medication was taken as prescribed and they were not impaired), while THC users cannot. This asymmetry is a direct product of the indexing of drug-driving law to detection chemistry rather than pharmacology, and is increasingly difficult to defend.
