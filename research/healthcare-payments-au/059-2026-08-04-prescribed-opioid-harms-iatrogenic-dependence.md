# Prescribed Opioid Harms and Iatrogenic Dependence: How Australia's Legitimate Pain System Created Its Own Crisis

**Report 059 — 2026-08-04**

**Summary:** Australia's opioid crisis is substantially a product of its own healthcare system. A 15-fold rise in prescription opioid use between 1995 and 2015 — driven overwhelmingly by oxycodone — created a generation of patients who entered legitimate pain treatment and left with dependence. Three people still die and approximately 150 are hospitalised from opioid harm every day. The reforms since 2018 — codeine rescheduling, PBS prescribing restrictions, and real-time monitoring — have meaningfully reduced prescribed opioid harm; but they have not closed the gap between what the system prescribes and what good pain care looks like, and some displacement to illicit markets has occurred. The demand side of the equation — adequate chronic pain management — remains underfunded, under-resourced, and structurally misaligned with MBS payment incentives.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 058 documented Australia's 57,754-person opioid treatment system and noted that a substantial proportion of OAT patients entered dependence through a legitimate prescription. This report examines that pathway: how Australia's medical system creates opioid dependence, what the payment architecture has to do with it, and what reform has and has not achieved.

---

## Background

### The Pharmaceutical Origins of Australia's Opioid Problem

Australia does not have an opioid crisis in the American sense — the mass-market prescribing of OxyContin, the Sackler family, the collapse of rural communities to fentanyl. But Australia does have a pharmaceutical opioid problem, and it is substantially a creation of its own healthcare system.

Between approximately 1995 and 2017, prescription opioid use in Australia rose by roughly 15-fold, a trajectory that tracked — with a time lag — the prescribing expansion that preceded the US crisis. The dominant driver was **oxycodone**, a synthetic opioid whose marketing to Australian GPs emphasised its controlled-release formulation as "safer" and less addictive than immediate-release alternatives. This claim proved false in the same way it proved false in North America: when the controlled-release coating was defeated (through crushing or chewing), oxycodone delivered an intense and addiction-forming opioid effect.

By the mid-2010s, Australia had a well-documented and serious problem with pharmaceutically-sourced opioid harm. Pharmaceutical opioids were involved in more opioid deaths and more opioid poisoning hospitalisations than heroin — a remarkable inversion of the older image of "the drug problem" as something that happened outside the healthcare system.

The policy response from 2018 onwards has been substantial, multi-component, and — by some measures — successful. But the success has been uneven, and the demand side of the equation remains largely unaddressed.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Burden: 3 Deaths and 150 Hospitalisations Per Day

The AIHW's surveillance data on opioid harm provides the clearest headline numbers:

- **Three Australians die from opioid-related harm every day** — approximately 1,095 deaths per year
- **Approximately 150 Australians are hospitalised from opioid-related harm daily** — over 54,000 hospitalisations per year
- **Approximately 14 emergency department admissions per day** involve opioid use issues

Within opioid-induced deaths in 2024:
- All opioids **excluding heroin** were involved in **62% (635 deaths)** of opioid-induced deaths
- **Heroin** was involved in **49% (501 deaths)** — a dramatic rise from 22% (237 deaths) in 2014

This shift in the death profile tells an important story. In 2014, pharmaceutical opioids were involved in 83% of opioid-induced deaths; in 2024, that has fallen to 62%. Meanwhile, heroin deaths have risen from 22% to 49%. The pharmaceutical opioid reforms have had an effect — but some displaced demand has found its way to illicit markets.

For hospitalisations, the picture remains dominated by pharmaceutical products. Pharmaceutical opioids (oxycodone, morphine, and other natural and semi-synthetic formulations) accounted for **55% of all opioid poisoning hospitalisations in 2022–23**, compared with:
- Heroin: 20%
- Synthetic opioids (tramadol, fentanyl): 14%
- Methadone: 6.6%
- Other opioids: 4.9%

### 2. How Prescribed Opioids Create Dependence: The Iatrogenic Pathway

Iatrogenic dependence — dependence caused by medical treatment itself — is the central dynamic in Australia's prescribed opioid problem. The pathway is now well-understood:

1. **Acute pain or injury.** A patient presents to a GP with legitimate, time-limited pain — post-surgical, post-injury, dental pain, musculoskeletal strain.
2. **Opioid prescription.** The GP prescribes an opioid analgesic — historically oxycodone or tramadol, now more commonly tapentadol or lower-dose formulations following PBS restrictions.
3. **Tolerance and dose escalation.** With repeated use, tolerance develops: the same dose produces less pain relief, and the patient (and sometimes their prescriber) responds by increasing the dose.
4. **Dependence.** The body adapts to the presence of the opioid. When the prescription ends or is delayed, withdrawal symptoms — which can include severe anxiety, insomnia, nausea, sweating, and pain amplification — occur. These symptoms feel like the original pain returning, making it clinically and subjectively difficult to distinguish pain from withdrawal.
5. **Chronic opioid therapy for chronic non-cancer pain.** A significant proportion of patients transition from acute to chronic opioid use, often without a formal decision having been made. An estimated **8.5% of Australians receiving opioids for chronic non-cancer pain in 2015 met the clinical criteria for opioid dependence** — a proportion derived from population-level modelling that suggests hundreds of thousands of people were caught in this pathway at its peak.
6. **OAT or illicit transition.** Some patients whose prescriptions are restricted or discontinued transition to heroin or other illicit opioids to manage dependence — a documented harm of supply-restricting policies that does not appear uniformly in population-level data but is consistently reported in qualitative research with people who have lived experience.

The role of the MBS in this pathway is direct and underappreciated. **Approximately 70% of opioids in Australia are prescribed in primary care**, and most non-fatal overdoses treated in emergency departments and most opioid-related deaths involve prescription opioids (in the sense that the patient was prescribed an opioid at some point before harm occurred).

The GP consultation, reimbursed at $43.90 for a Level B attendance under Item 23 (from 1 July 2025), is the gateway. As documented in Report 009, GPs operate under severe time pressure — a standard consultation runs 6–20 minutes, which is inadequate for the kind of biopsychosocial pain assessment that could identify whether an opioid prescription is actually appropriate. It is far easier — and, under MBS incentives, economically rational — to write a prescription than to undertake a time-intensive conversation about non-pharmacological alternatives.

A 2025 qualitative study in the *Medical Journal of Australia* (Thomas et al.) examining rural GPs' opioid prescribing practice identified two interconnected system failures:
- **Systemic constraints**: insufficient consultation time for alternative management strategies, and the influence of MBS remuneration in shaping prescribing decisions
- **Limited access to multidisciplinary pain management**: GPs who might prefer to refer a patient to a pain psychologist, physiotherapist, or multidisciplinary pain clinic simply cannot, because waiting lists are "unacceptably long" or services do not exist in their area

Rural opioid prescribing, the study found, is **driven more by health care system deficiencies than by lack of prescriber knowledge**.

### 3. The Codeine Rescheduling: A Necessary but Partial Fix

The 2018 codeine rescheduling was the first major supply-side intervention. From 1 February 2018, all codeine-containing products — previously available over-the-counter in pharmacies for mild to moderate pain — became Schedule 4 (prescription-only). The objective was to interrupt the pattern in which patients self-medicated chronic pain with escalating doses of codeine-containing products, developing dependence without any clinical oversight.

The epidemiological case for rescheduling was strong. Prior to 2018, the proportion of people seeking opioid substitution pharmacotherapy for codeine dependence had risen from 2.7% to 4.6% of OAT clients between 2014 and 2016 — a material share of the treatment system. Codeine-combination products (paracetamol with codeine, ibuprofen with codeine) were widely available and heavily marketed for headache, period pain, and musculoskeletal complaints.

The impact of rescheduling was measurable but mixed:
- **No sustained increase in prescription codeine use** was observed — patients did not simply shift their codeine use from OTC to GP prescribing at scale
- **No substantial shift to stronger prescribed opioids** occurred at population level — the feared substitution effect (patients moving from OTC codeine to prescription oxycodone) was not confirmed in PBS claims data
- The rescheduling did **increase a significant administrative burden on GPs** who now handle prescription requests for what were formerly self-managed pain issues

The most important limitation of codeine rescheduling was that it addressed supply without addressing demand: the chronic pain conditions that drove OTC codeine use did not go away when the product became prescription-only. Patients now had to see a GP to access pain relief — an improvement in some respects (clinical oversight), but also a new access barrier, particularly for people in rural areas or without easy GP access.

### 4. PBS Prescribing Restrictions (2020): Reducing Volume, Creating New Gaps

From 1 June 2020, the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme introduced the most significant structural reform to opioid prescribing since the opioid treatment program:

- **Immediate-release (IR) opioids** moved from "unrestricted" to "restricted benefit" — they can now only be prescribed for specific PBS-approved indications
- **Controlled-release (CR) opioids** moved from "restricted benefit" to "authority required (streamlined)" — requiring PBS authority approval and an attested clinical indication
- **High-strength opioids** (high-dose morphine, fentanyl patches) now require additional authorisation requirements and eligibility criteria
- A **12-month secondary review** requirement: most PBS opioid listings require a second practitioner review if opioid treatment exceeds or is expected to exceed 12 months duration
- **Repeat prescriptions** fell dramatically: from 7.4% of all opioid dispensings before the June 2020 changes to **1.3% after** — a 82% reduction in repeats

The aggregate effect on prescribing volume has been substantial. A University of Melbourne/NDARC study found that **PBS opioid dispensing declined by 33%** between 2015 and 2022 — primarily driven by controlled-release formulations. Total prescription opioid use (PBS plus private market) fell approximately 21% over the same period.

However — and this is critical — the NDARC study also found that **approximately 25% of the decline in PBS dispensing was offset by a 55% jump in private market opioid use** (prescriptions filled outside the PBS, paying full market price). This "private dispensing gap" is poorly understood and poorly monitored: private prescriptions do not appear in PBS claims data, and RTPM systems (see Section 5) catch the prescription but not its payment mechanism. Some of this private-market shift likely represents patients obtaining opioids outside PBS restriction criteria — a safety concern that is not currently captured in routine surveillance.

The clinical effect of the PBS restrictions was assessed in a 2025 *MJA* study by Nielsen et al. examining Victoria 2018–2022: the two mid-2020 policies (mandatory RTPM and PBS restrictions) were associated with a **decline in opioid-related emergency department presentation rates**, but also with a **sharp but temporary increase in non-opioid substance-related presentations** — consistent with the "balloon squeeze" phenomenon, where patients unable to access opioids turned temporarily to other substances to manage pain or withdrawal.

A companion editorial by Daniels in the same issue coined the phrase "**squeezing the opioid balloon**" — arguing that supply-restricting policies move harm around rather than eliminating it, and that demand-side interventions (better pain management, addiction treatment access) are essential to achieve durable harm reduction rather than harm displacement.

### 5. Real-Time Prescription Monitoring: Significant Impact on Doctor-Shopping

The most technically innovative reform has been the national rollout of **real-time prescription monitoring (RTPM)** — systems that alert prescribers when a patient has received high-risk medicines (Schedule 8 opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants) from multiple providers within a defined period (typically four or more prescribers within 90 days).

Victoria's **SafeScript** system launched voluntarily in April 2019 and became mandatory in April 2020. Similar systems now operate in all states and territories, with national data linkage underway.

A landmark June 2026 study published in the *MJA* (Monash University, analysing 6.7 million prescriptions across 810,000 patients in 562 Victorian general practices from 2017–2023) found:

- An **immediate 15% drop** in patients seeing four or more prescribers for monitored medicines when SafeScript launched voluntarily in April 2019
- Further declines over the following year; reductions sustained after mandatory use from April 2020
- **96% of multiple-prescriber cases occurred within the same clinic** — a finding that suggests most "doctor-shopping" for opioids was concentrated within practices, not across them, challenging the common mental model of patients running between clinics
- **85% of multiple prescriber episodes involved at least one opioid prescription**
- Older patients, males, metropolitan residents, and those with a documented substance use disorder were most likely to have been seeing multiple prescribers before SafeScript

The concentration of multiple-prescribing within single clinics is a policy-relevant finding: it suggests that internal clinical governance (prescriber audit and feedback, practice-level protocols) may be as important as inter-system monitoring in addressing opioid overprescribing.

### 6. The Chronic Pain Treatment Gap: Where the Demand Side Lives

Every PBS restriction and RTPM system confronts the same structural problem: **patients in genuine chronic pain do not stop needing treatment when their opioid prescription becomes harder to obtain**. If adequate alternatives to opioid analgesics were available, supply restriction would be unambiguously beneficial. In Australia in 2026, those alternatives are often not accessible.

The gold standard for chronic non-cancer pain management is **multidisciplinary, biopsychosocial care**: integrating medical, psychological, physiotherapeutic, and social support interventions to address the full dimensions of the pain experience. The evidence base for this approach — compared to long-term opioid therapy — is robust and consistently shows better outcomes on pain, function, quality of life, and opioid use.

The economic case is also strong. The annual cost of chronic pain in Australia is conservatively estimated at **$139 billion**, including $12 billion in direct healthcare costs and $48 billion in lost productivity (National Strategic Action Plan for Pain Management, 2019). Even modest improvements in pain management outcomes from better-resourced services would deliver substantial economic returns.

But the system does not deliver this care:

- **Public hospital multidisciplinary pain clinics** have waiting times of 12–24+ months in major metropolitan areas; in rural and remote areas, such services often do not exist at all
- The **MBS does not adequately reimburse complex chronic pain consultations**. Specialist pain medicine practitioners (SPMPs) face a structural limitation: those from anaesthesia or GP backgrounds (the majority) cannot access the longer, better-reimbursed Group A4 consultant physician items (MBS Items 132/133) and are instead limited to Items 2801 and 2806, which do not reflect the complexity and time required for multidisciplinary care planning
- **Allied health under the Chronic Disease Management arrangements**: the GPCCMP (from 1 July 2025, Items 965/967) provides access to up to 5 allied health visits per year for eligible patients — wholly inadequate for complex chronic pain requiring sustained engagement with pain psychology, physiotherapy, and occupational therapy
- **Pain psychology**: not separately recognised or funded through MBS; psychologists treating chronic pain use the same Better Access items (Items 80010/80110) as those treating depression and anxiety, with the same 10-session annual cap — despite evidence that effective pain psychology requires longer-term engagement

The Faculty of Pain Medicine (ANZCA) has documented this gap to Health Minister Butler (June 2025 letter) and has advocated for revised MBS items that appropriately reflect the complexity and clinical outcomes of specialist pain medicine consultations. As of mid-2026, the review is ongoing.

### 7. Opioid-to-OAT: The Treatment Pipeline That Works When Accessed

The connection to Report 058's opioid agonist treatment system is direct: **people who develop dependence through prescribed opioids are among the primary population requiring OAT**. The AIHW's alcohol and other drug treatment data confirms this:

- In 2024–25, **approximately 40% of alcohol and other drug treatment episodes** for pharmaceutical opioids were classified as requiring treatment for opioid dependence — representing approximately 5,200 episodes nationally
- Treatment referrals for pharmaceutical opioid issues are predominantly through hospital emergency departments (a crisis presentation pathway) and self-referral — both late-stage, high-cost entry points compared to GP-initiated early intervention

The irony is that GPs who prescribe opioids and create the dependence are, in theory, also the practitioners best positioned to initiate OAT — they now have PBS access to prescribe methadone and buprenorphine since the July 2023 reform. But as Report 058 documented, only 3,357 authorised prescribers exist nationally, and the Medicare reimbursement for OAT management does not reflect the complexity of the clinical work. The system that creates dependence through inadequate chronic pain management also fails to adequately fund its correction through OAT.

### 8. The Prescription-to-Heroin Pipeline: A Policy Harm

One of the documented risks of supply-restricting opioid policies is the transition of some patients from prescribed opioids to illicit heroin or synthetic opioids when their prescriptions become unavailable. In the United States, this pathway was epidemic in scale; in Australia, the evidence for it is more limited but present.

The shift in the opioid death profile — pharmaceutical opioid deaths falling from 83% to 62% of the total between 2014 and 2024, while heroin deaths rose from 22% to 49% — is consistent with (but not proof of) some supply-side displacement. Qualitative research with people who have lived experience of opioid dependence in Australia consistently documents the pathway: a legitimate prescription for pain, growing tolerance, dose escalation, then the prescription ending, and the shift to heroin to manage withdrawal.

The 2025 *MJA* "squeezing the balloon" analysis explicitly flags this as a design problem with supply-only reform: if prescribing restrictions are implemented without commensurate expansion of OAT access, pain clinic capacity, and supported tapering programs, some proportion of patients will seek illicit alternatives. The December 2026 withdrawal of Sublocade (noted in Report 058) makes this particularly relevant: patients being transitioned off stable LAIB treatment are at elevated risk of this very pathway at a moment of maximum vulnerability.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Legitimate pain patients vs. stewardship.** Supply-restricting policies have been experienced as deeply unjust by many patients with chronic pain who were never seeking opioids for recreational or non-therapeutic purposes but who now face prescription barriers, authority requirements, and involuntary tapering. A 2024 *Drug and Alcohol Review* qualitative study (Hopkins et al.) documented the distress of this group, whose voices are often absent from policy discussions dominated by addiction medicine and harm reduction perspectives. Effective policy must hold both frames simultaneously.

**2. The private market gap.** PBS restrictions have been partially offset by a 55% rise in private-market opioid dispensing. Private prescriptions are less visible to pharmacovigilance systems, less connected to authority requirements, and less traceable through claims data. Whether RTPM systems are capturing all high-risk private prescribing — and whether the system is effectively monitoring the displaced demand — is an open empirical question.

**3. MBS structural misalignment.** The core problem — that MBS payment incentives reward throughput (short consultations and a prescription) over quality (long biopsychosocial assessment and non-pharmacological management) — has not been addressed by any of the opioid reforms. Until GP consultations are adequately reimbursed for complex pain management, and until specialist pain medicine has an MBS structure that reflects its complexity, GPs will continue to face a system that is structurally biased toward opioid prescribing.

**4. Who holds the safety net for patients cut off from opioids?** When a GP follows PBS authority guidelines and declines to continue an opioid prescription, or when a patient's prescription is flagged by RTPM and subject to clinical review, what happens to that patient? If they have genuine chronic pain and no access to a multidisciplinary pain clinic, they may experience severe unmanaged pain, or they may seek opioids elsewhere. The reforms have created exit ramps from the system without adequately providing on-ramps to better alternatives.

**5. Synthetic opioids: the next wave.** Illicit fentanyl and fentanyl analogues — the drivers of mass mortality in the US and Canada — have not yet become dominant in Australia's illicit opioid market. The RTPM and PBS surveillance systems were designed for the pharmaceutical opioid landscape. If illicit synthetic opioids enter the Australian market at scale (a risk consistently flagged by harm reduction advocates, including through the drug checking network discussed in future reports), the current architecture will be inadequate.
