# Heavy Menstrual Bleeding in Australia: A $14 Billion Burden, a Fivefold Hysterectomy Variation, and the Management Architecture Failing Half of All Women

**One-line summary:** Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) affects up to 39% of Australian women by midlife and costs the economy an estimated $14 billion per year in lost productivity, yet 55% of affected women have never discussed it with a healthcare professional — exposing a management architecture riddled with access gaps, geographic inequity, and a pharmaceutically underfunded primary care pathway.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 123 examined adenomyosis, noting that heavy menstrual bleeding is among the condition's most disabling features and that Australia's PBS architecture was not designed for adenomyosis — with the LNG-IUS accessible only through a heavy menstrual bleeding restriction, not an adenomyosis-specific pathway. Today's report examines the broader HMB management system: who gets what treatment, how it is paid for, and where the architecture fails.

---

## Background

Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) — defined clinically as menstrual blood loss sufficient to interfere with a woman's physical, social, emotional, or material quality of life, rather than by objective volume — is one of the most common reasons women present to general practitioners in Australia. It is also one of the most systematically undertreated.

Australia has had a national clinical care standard for HMB since 2017, revised in 2024 by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC). The standard exists because, despite effective first-line treatments being available, hysterectomy rates in Australia remain among the highest in the OECD — and vary by up to fivefold across geographic areas — signalling that many women are receiving the most radical intervention first, rather than the least invasive treatment appropriate to their clinical situation.

The 2024 revision and its accompanying Atlas Women's Health Focus Report set out the scale of the problem with new clarity: Australia is not managing HMB well, and the women bearing the consequences are disproportionately those in rural and regional areas, and those who are Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander.

## Key Findings

### 1. Prevalence and Burden: Larger Than the Health System Treats It

A March 2025 study in the *Medical Journal of Australia* (Guthrie et al., using the Australian Longitudinal Study on Women's Health) found that HMB prevalence rises substantially with age: 17.6% of women aged 22 reported heavy bleeding in the preceding twelve months, rising to 32.1% of women at age 48, and reaching 39.3% among women of that age who were still menstruating. The condition is not rare, nor is it primarily a midlife phenomenon — it spans reproductive life.

A 2023 commentary in *MJA InSight* noted that three in four Australian women have at some point experienced what they describe as heavy menstrual bleeding — though only a fraction have had it formally assessed. Most strikingly, **55% of Australian women who experience HMB have never discussed it with a medical professional**, and 46% of those who always experience heavy periods have not sought any treatment.

This is not a minor access gap. It represents a structural failure of the healthcare system to reach a clinical problem that, on economic cost estimates, is enormous. A study published in 2025 in the *Australian Journal of Social Issues* estimated that period pain and heavy menstrual bleeding cost the Australian economy approximately **$14 billion per year** in lost productivity — covering only paid employment among full-time and part-time workers, meaning the total burden including unpaid work, out-of-pocket costs, and healthcare utilisation is substantially higher.

HMB is also a major driver of iron deficiency — a condition with its own substantial disease burden. A Monash University study found that 34.8% of young Australian women have serum ferritin below 30 μg/L, with low ferritin strongly associated with reporting more than two days of heavy menstrual bleeding. The 2024 ACSQHC Clinical Care Standard recommends that assessment include exclusion of iron deficiency and anaemia as a first step, with full blood count recommended at initial assessment and targeted ferritin testing for those with anaemia or elevated risk.

### 2. The Treatment Pyramid and Where the Architecture Sits

HMB management in Australia follows a step-up architecture:

**First-line pharmaceutical:** Tranexamic acid (oral antifibrinolytic), mefenamic acid or other NSAIDs, and combined oral contraceptives — all available via standard GP prescription, generally under PBS general benefit restrictions for their respective indications, and at low patient cost. These are the treatments the 2024 ACSQHC Standard recommends be offered at the first presentation, without waiting for investigations to be complete.

**Second-line — LNG-IUS:** The levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (52 mg, Mirena) is PBS-listed under a restricted benefit for **"idiopathic menorrhagia"** when oral medical therapies have failed or are contraindicated. The current general patient co-payment is $25.00, making it affordable once access is achieved. However, the PBS listing was predicated on a cost-minimisation comparison against hysterectomy — meaning the PBS pathway was designed as a surgical-avoidance tool for women who had exhausted oral options, not a readily accessible first-line option for all suitable patients.

A 2021 RACGP-published analysis of GP management using BEACH data found **low prescribing and insertion rates for the LNG-IUS**, particularly among younger women (and none documented in the 12–24 age cohort in the dataset). The authors identified GP training, patient counselling capacity, and practice-level infrastructure (insertion competency, appropriate consultation length) as likely barriers.

The **2024 Clinical Care Standard** and associated RANZCOG guidance now explicitly state that the 52 mg LNG-IUS can be offered as first-line provided there is no malignancy or other significant pathology — a broadening of the clinical culture if not the PBS restriction itself.

**Endometrial ablation (surgical — uterus-preserving):** Endometrial ablation — destruction of the uterine lining — is available through several MBS-funded procedures. **MBS item 35616** covers thermal balloon or radiofrequency ablation; **item 35622** covers hysteroscopic electrosurgery or laser ablation; and **item 35623** covers ablation combined with myoma resection. These are hospital-day procedures, generally requiring specialist referral to a gynaecologist. They are not suitable for women seeking future fertility.

The Atlas Focus Report found that endometrial ablation hospitalisations **rose 10% nationally** between 2013–16 and 2019–22 — broadly consistent with the intended shift toward uterus-preserving interventions — but with a **20-fold variation** between the highest- and lowest-rate local areas. This extraordinary range suggests that access to endometrial ablation is profoundly unequal, shaped by proximity to gynaecologists who offer the procedure.

**Uterine artery embolisation (UAE):** Under **MBS item 35410**, UAE is funded for symptomatic uterine fibroids, requiring referral from a specialist gynaecologist. The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Radiologists (RANZCR) has backed removing the mandatory gynaecologist referral requirement — reflecting an interventional radiology capacity to treat women who may not have timely gynaecology access — but the 2024 Clinical Care Standard notes that UAE is now included as a recognised option, indicating growing policy recognition of the procedure's role.

**Hysterectomy (surgical — not uterus-preserving):** MBS items 35673, 35677, and 35684 cover abdominal and laparoscopic hysterectomy. This remains the definitive, curative intervention for women who have completed their families and for whom other treatments have failed or are not suitable.

### 3. The Hysterectomy Problem: Too Common, Too Variable, Too Rural

The Atlas Focus Report's central finding on hysterectomy is stark:
- Australia's age-standardised hysterectomy rate was approximately **215 per 100,000 women** in 2021–22 — compared with **126 per 100,000 in New Zealand** and **132 per 100,000 in the United Kingdom** on comparable 2019 data. Australia's rate is roughly 70% higher than these peer countries.
- National hysterectomy hospitalisations fell by approximately 20% between 2014–15 and 2021–22 — a welcome trend, partly attributable to increased LNG-IUS uptake and greater endometrial ablation availability.
- Despite this improvement, there is a **fivefold difference** between the local area with the highest and lowest hysterectomy rates. In rural NSW, communities like Bourke, Cobar, and Coonamble recorded age-standardised rates of 428 per 100,000 women in 2021–22 against a NSW average of 217 per 100,000 — double the state average in some communities.
- **First Nations women** had a hysterectomy rate approximately **9% higher** than other Australian women in 2021–22, and the rate rose by 5% over the study period rather than declining — the opposite of the national trend.

The most likely explanation for the geographic and Indigenous differential is differential access to the full treatment hierarchy. Rural and regional women who cannot readily access a GP skilled in LNG-IUS insertion, or who lack proximity to a gynaecologist offering endometrial ablation, encounter hysterectomy as the nearest definitive solution available. As Professor Kirsten Black (University of Sydney) noted in media coverage of the Atlas report: "There's a five-fold difference in hysterectomy rates across the country, and this suggests that women are being treated differently" — with access to less invasive alternatives — or more precisely, lack thereof — as the driving variable.

### 4. The Bleeding Disorders Gap

A frequently cited figure in GPeducation contexts — though not yet from a nationally representative Australian study — is that approximately one in three young women with HMB have an underlying bleeding disorder, most commonly **von Willebrand disease**, and that diagnosis of such conditions can take up to 16 years.

The 2024 Clinical Care Standard notes that testing for bleeding disorders is not routinely required at initial HMB presentation — appropriate for an undiagnosed population where other, more common causes are more probable — but it is recommended where clinical features suggest an underlying haemostatic disorder (onset at menarche, family history, dental or surgical bleeding). The standard also references iron deficiency anaemia assessment and notes that bleeding disorders, fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, and endometriosis should all be considered in diagnosis.

For many women with a bleeding disorder, management requires coordination between haematology and gynaecology — a specialist interface that is largely outside the bulk-billed primary care system and that generates substantial out-of-pocket costs for patients without comprehensive private health insurance.

### 5. The Equity Gaps the Standard Cannot Fix

The 2024 ACSQHC Clinical Care Standard and Atlas report together make a strong case that HMB is not a rare or obscure condition — it is common, burdensome, and undermanaged. But the standard is a clinical guidance document, not a funding mechanism. The access gaps it identifies require structural changes:

- **LNG-IUS insertion capacity in primary care:** GP-level insertion is feasible and endorsed, but requires procedural training, adequate consultation time (longer than a standard 15-minute MBS item), and appropriate billing. There is no dedicated MBS item for GP-level LNG-IUS insertion separate from contraception procedures, and no premium for the longer consultation associated with device counselling and insertion in the context of HMB management.
- **Rural access to endometrial ablation:** The 20-fold variation in ablation rates points to a workforce distribution problem. Telehealth cannot substitute for a surgical procedure requiring specialist skill.
- **Culturally appropriate access for First Nations women:** The rising hysterectomy rate in this population, against a declining national trend, suggests that the structural factors driving overuse of radical treatment are not being addressed by clinical guidance alone.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **PBS restriction vs clinical practice:** The LNG-IUS PBS restriction requires oral therapies to have failed. The 2024 Clinical Care Standard endorses LNG-IUS as first-line. These two frameworks are in tension for women whose clinician recommends the device immediately but who have not formally trialled oral alternatives. Some prescribers work around this through careful restriction documentation; others do not offer the device until the PBS pathway is formally met.

2. **Endometrial ablation and subsequent pathology detection:** A known clinical concern with endometrial ablation is that it can make subsequent endometrial cancer detection more difficult by obliterating the accessible mucosa and creating intrauterine scarring (haematometra). This is not a reason to avoid the procedure for appropriate patients, but it means long-term surveillance obligations exist — which are not systematically structured through any national monitoring program.

3. **Geographic access to ablation vs hysterectomy:** If endometrial ablation is only available in major-city centres, a woman in a regional area faces a choice between travelling for a uterus-preserving procedure or accepting hysterectomy locally. This is a genuine healthcare geography problem, not simply a clinical choice failure.

4. **Bleeding disorder underdiagnosis:** The long delay in diagnosing haemostatic disorders in HMB patients — across multiple GP encounters, with iron supplementation and hormonal management attempted before the underlying condition is identified — represents a systematic failure with real patient harm.
