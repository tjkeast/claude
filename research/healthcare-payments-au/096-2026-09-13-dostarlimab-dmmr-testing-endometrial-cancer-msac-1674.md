# Dostarlimab, dMMR Testing, and the Diagnostic-Therapeutic Gap in Endometrial Cancer: How Australia Is Building the Biomarker Infrastructure for Checkpoint Inhibitor Access

**Report 096 — 2026-09-13**

**Summary:** Dostarlimab (Jemperli) has been PBS-listed since 1 May 2024 for women with mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) advanced endometrial cancer, delivering extraordinary clinical outcomes including a model-projected 54% cure rate in its target population. But the diagnostic test that determines eligibility is claimed through a non-specific, catch-all MBS item never designed for the purpose. MSAC Application 1674 — seeking a dedicated dMMR testing item for endometrial cancer — reveals the structural gap between Australia's PBS therapeutic access framework and its diagnostic funding architecture. This report examines that gap, its consequences for the 200–900 Australian women annually who may need it, and the broader molecular classification revolution reshaping how endometrial cancer is treated.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 095 documented Australia's world-first broad PBS listing for checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab/ipilimumab and pembrolizumab) — listings that deliberately removed biomarker requirements. Dostarlimab presents the mirror image: a contemporaneous PBS listing for the same drug class that *retains* biomarker gating, revealing the diagnostic-therapeutic infrastructure challenge that the broad listing model sidesteps but does not solve.

---

## Background: Endometrial Cancer's Rising Burden and the Molecular Revolution

### Rising Incidence

Endometrial cancer is Australia's most common gynaecological cancer, accounting for approximately 90–95% of uterine cancer diagnoses. Incidence is rising at an alarming rate — an estimated **3,493 women** were projected to be diagnosed in 2025, a **13.8% increase** over five years (2019–2024). The primary driver is rising obesity prevalence: in 2020, overweight and obesity were attributable to **49% of all endometrioid endometrial cancers** in Australia, up from 31% in 2001. Approximately **706 women** died from uterine cancer in 2025.

The disease was historically treated surgically and managed with good outcomes at early stage, but advanced and recurrent cases had very poor prognoses until the emergence of checkpoint inhibitors. Before immunotherapy entered practice, the five-year survival rate for advanced dMMR endometrial cancer was approximately 18%.

### The Four Molecular Subtypes

A revolution in endometrial cancer management has occurred over the last decade. The TCGA molecular classification — adopted clinically as the **ProMisE** (Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for Endometrial Cancer) system — identifies four distinct molecular subtypes with distinct prognoses and treatment implications:

| Subtype | Frequency | Prognosis | Clinical implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **POLE-mutant** (POLEmut) | ~7–10% | Excellent (near 100% 5-year OS) | Potential de-escalation of adjuvant treatment |
| **Mismatch repair–deficient** (dMMR/MMRd) | ~25–28% | Intermediate-good, markedly improved with ICI | PBS-eligible for dostarlimab |
| **No specific molecular profile** (NSMP) | ~40% | Intermediate | Standard treatment |
| **p53-abnormal** (p53abn) | ~25% | Poor | Intensification with chemo + adjuvant radiotherapy |

The 2023 **FIGO staging system** for endometrial cancer incorporated molecular classification for the first time — representing a paradigm shift from purely anatomical staging to "anatomic-molecular" dual-dimension staging. Australia's clinical guidelines (through RANZCOG and ANZGOG) have adopted this framework.

## Key Findings

### 1. Dostarlimab's PBS Listing: What It Covers and Who Benefits

Dostarlimab (Jemperli®, GSK) was listed on the PBS from **1 May 2024** for:
- Women with **primary advanced (Stage III/IV) or first recurrent endometrial cancer**
- Where the tumour is classified as **mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR)**
- In combination with carboplatin and paclitaxel (chemotherapy), as **first-line treatment**
- Prescriptions are Authority Required (Streamlined)
- Approximately **200 women per year** are expected to benefit from this specific listing

The PBS listing followed a November 2023 PBAC recommendation. This was one of the most significant oncology PBS listings in recent years for a gynaecological cancer.

Cost-effectiveness analyses indicate that in the dMMR population, dostarlimab plus chemotherapy generates approximately **5.48 additional quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs)** at an incremental cost of around **$330,747** per patient, yielding an ICER of approximately **$60,349 per QALY** — well within typical PBAC cost-effectiveness thresholds when a confidential risk-sharing arrangement is factored in.

### 2. The RUBY Trial: From Efficacy to Curative Intent

The PBS listing is grounded in the **ENGOT-EN6/GOG-3031/RUBY trial** — the pivotal Phase III randomised controlled trial comparing dostarlimab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel versus chemotherapy alone in advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer.

**Four-year data presented at ASCO 2026** (June 2026) revealed results that have shifted clinical framing from "treating" the disease to potentially curing it:

- **4-year progression-free survival:** 57.9% for dostarlimab + chemo vs 15.7% for chemotherapy alone in the dMMR/MSI-H subgroup
- **Overall survival:** Hazard ratio for death of **0.32** (95% CI 0.17–0.63) in the dMMR/MSI-H population — a 68% reduction in the risk of death
- **Median OS:** Not reached in the dostarlimab arm at median 55.6 months of follow-up, vs 32.8 months in the control arm
- **Modelled cure rate:** A formal cure modelling analysis published at ASCO 2026 estimated a **54% cure rate** in dMMR/MSI-H patients receiving dostarlimab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel — the statistical concept that a plateau on the survival curve represents patients who will never experience disease recurrence
- **Historical context:** Pre-immunotherapy, five-year OS for this population was approximately 18%. Post-dostarlimab, survival in the dMMR cohort is described as "approaching that of a matched population without cancer"
- **Safety:** No new safety signals were identified with extended 2.5 years of additional follow-up

These outcomes are extraordinary in a cancer subtype previously considered incurable when advanced. They also make the accuracy and completeness of dMMR testing a question of life-and-death consequence.

### 3. The Diagnostic-Therapeutic Gap: MSAC Application 1674

The critical structural problem: dostarlimab's PBS eligibility requires a **dMMR-positive test result** — but Australia does not yet have a dedicated MBS item for this specific diagnostic purpose in endometrial cancer.

Currently, dMMR testing in endometrial cancer is claimed via:
- **MBS item 72847**: Immunohistochemistry (IHC) with 4–6 antibodies — a general, non-specific item designed for multiple pathology purposes, not tailored to MMR protein testing or to the clinical question of checkpoint inhibitor eligibility

This is the gap that **MSAC Application 1674** addresses. The application seeks a **new, dedicated MBS item** for IHC-based dMMR testing in endometrial cancer to determine eligibility for PBS-subsidised dostarlimab. Specifically:

- **Test type:** Immunohistochemistry (IHC) using antibodies directed against the four MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)
- **Patient population:** Women with advanced, recurrent, or metastatic endometrial cancer being assessed for dostarlimab eligibility
- **Current status (as of September 2026):** Under MSAC assessment — the ESC (Evaluation Sub-Committee) was considering related applications as recently as February 2026; the formal decision outcome for Application 1674 has not been publicly published in the MSAC Assessment Outcomes register

The absence of a dedicated MBS item creates several problems:

**Accountability gap:** IHC testing under item 72847 does not specify the antibodies used or the clinical purpose. There is no mechanism to audit whether the test was appropriately performed for PBS eligibility assessment or whether all four MMR proteins were tested (a critical quality requirement — loss of any one can indicate dMMR).

**No test-treatment link:** The MBS claim for the test and the PBS claim for dostarlimab are currently not linked in any administrative sense. MSAC's application, if successful, would establish a dedicated item that creates a clearer regulatory and accountability pathway.

**Potential under-testing:** Because no dedicated funding stream signals to pathologists and oncologists that dMMR testing is a standard step before prescribing, and because the test is buried within general IHC billing, some women may not receive testing before clinical decisions are made — or may receive inadequate testing.

### 4. The dMMR Prevalence and Who Is Not Being Reached

Approximately **25–27% of endometrial cancers** are dMMR. With ~3,493 new cases expected in 2025, this suggests approximately **870–940 Australian women** per year have dMMR endometrial cancer.

Only around **200 women per year** were initially projected to benefit from the PBS dostarlimab listing. The discrepancy is partly explained by stage (the listing is for advanced/recurrent disease, and many women are diagnosed at early stages), but it also reflects a system not yet fully identifying and channelling dMMR-positive women to the appropriate treatment.

Projections from a **Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific** analysis (2025) of dMMR/MSI-H biomarker prevalence across all solid cancers in Australia estimate:
- dMMR cases in advanced solid cancers will increase from **3,983 (2018)** to **5,448 (2042)**, a 37% increase
- MSI cases similarly rising from 2,484 to 3,553 over the same period

This trajectory, combined with endometrial cancer's rising incidence, makes the diagnostic infrastructure investment in MSAC 1674 increasingly urgent.

### 5. The Lynch Syndrome Dual-Purpose Dimension

dMMR testing in endometrial cancer serves a second critical clinical function: identifying patients with **Lynch syndrome** — a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6, EPCAM).

Lynch syndrome is responsible for approximately **3% of all endometrial cancers** in Australia. Universal tumour dMMR testing in endometrial cancer is the recommended first step in Lynch syndrome identification: if a tumour is dMMR-positive and somatic causes (particularly MLH1 promoter hypermethylation) are excluded, germline testing for Lynch syndrome variants should follow.

The **Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia (RCPA)** has endorsed universal dMMR screening for both colorectal and endometrial cancers in its reporting guidelines. However, a 2026 survey-based study found that:

> *"Access to tumour-based dMMR screening is neither adequate nor equitable in Australia, with variability based on jurisdiction."*

This creates a compounding equity problem: dMMR testing gaps mean women are denied not only PBS access to dostarlimab, but also the opportunity to identify a hereditary cancer syndrome that has implications for their family members' cancer risks.

The MBS cancer biomarker test utilisation analysis (Kong et al., 2026) found stark geographic disparities in testing uptake: **23.8% of incident cancers** in New South Wales received biomarker testing vs only **8.7% in the Northern Territory** — a near-threefold geographic disparity concentrated against jurisdictions with higher proportions of First Nations patients.

### 6. The Broader Molecular Classification Infrastructure Gap: MSAC 1790

The dMMR testing gap is part of a wider deficit in molecular testing infrastructure for endometrial cancer. In parallel, **MSAC Application 1790** is seeking MBS funding for **POLE genotyping** — genetic testing for POLE exonuclease domain mutations (POLE-mut) — for the molecular classification of endometrial carcinoma.

POLE-mut endometrial cancer has an extraordinary prognosis (near-100% five-year OS in some studies even at advanced stage) and may enable significant **de-escalation of adjuvant treatment** — reducing chemotherapy and radiotherapy exposure in women who don't need it, with real quality-of-life and cost benefits. MSAC 1790 was being considered at the ESC February 2026 meeting, indicating the molecular testing coverage gap extends well beyond dMMR testing.

Without MBS-funded POLE testing, clinicians cannot:
- Safely de-escalate adjuvant treatment in POLE-mut patients
- Avoid over-treating women in the most favourable molecular subgroup
- Apply the FIGO 2023 molecular staging system as intended

The coexistence of MSAC 1674 (dMMR) and MSAC 1790 (POLE) applications reveals a systemic lag: Australia has adopted molecular classification in clinical guidelines and incorporated it into international staging, but the MBS has not yet funded the diagnostic tests that implement that classification.

### 7. Cancer Biomarker Testing in Australia: The System-Wide Picture

A comprehensive analysis of MBS cancer biomarker test utilisation published in 2026 (Kong et al., *Clinical and Translational Science*) provides important context:

- Australia has **21 MBS-reimbursed cancer biomarker test items** (Group P7—Genetics in the March 2025 MBS)
- Claims grew **nearly fivefold** from 5,130 annually (2010) to 25,374 (2024)
- Cancer biomarker tests represent **6% of all MBS-funded genetic test volume** but **10% of total genetic test expenditure**
- Median reimbursement per test rose from **AU$339 (2014) to AU$667 (2024)**, reflecting greater use of multigene panel tests
- Women account for **67%** of cancer biomarker test claims (down from 88% in 2014 as BRAF, RAS, and other male-predominant cancer markers expanded)

This growth trajectory shows an expanding biomarker-guided treatment ecosystem — but the geographic and jurisdictional disparities signal that the benefits are not equitably distributed.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Who bears the cost if MSAC 1674 is delayed?** Currently, dMMR IHC testing for dostarlimab eligibility is performed under a general IHC item. The cost falls on the pathology laboratory (recouped through item 72847 at a rate that may not fully cover the MMR-specific 4-antibody panel). If testing is inadequate or inconsistent, women with dMMR cancer may not receive dostarlimab despite being eligible — or may receive it without confirmed dMMR status.

**2. Does the dostarlimab broad listing approach address or sidestep the diagnostic gap?** The broad pembrolizumab/nivolumab listings (from Report 095) removed biomarker requirements, sidestepping the need for funded testing. Dostarlimab's indication-specific listing preserves biomarker gating — arguably more clinically appropriate (the dMMR population has vastly better outcomes), but only if the gating test is reliably funded and accessible.

**3. How does reflex testing work in practice?** Guidelines recommend reflex dMMR testing at pathology diagnosis — before patients reach a medical oncologist. If testing is performed reflexively at the point of pathology reporting, its MBS claim and the subsequent PBS dostarlimab authority prescription are temporally disconnected. MSAC 1674 needs to specify whether the dedicated item covers reflex testing at diagnosis (the optimal point) or testing at the point of PBS eligibility assessment (potentially too late in the clinical pathway).

**4. Lynch syndrome cascade: who follows up?** Identifying a dMMR tumour creates clinical obligations to pursue Lynch syndrome diagnosis and to counsel and test at-risk family members. But Australia has no funded pathway for cascade genetic counselling and testing for Lynch syndrome identified through reflex tumour testing — a gap that sits at the intersection of the MBS, PBS, and state-funded genetic services.

**5. The p53-abnormal treatment gap:** While dMMR is the biomarker that unlocks PBS dostarlimab access, the p53-abnormal (p53abn) subtype — with the worst prognosis — does not yet have a specific PBS-funded targeted therapy. The molecular classification system creates winners (POLE-mut with de-escalation, dMMR with immunotherapy) and groups still awaiting matched treatment innovation.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Lynch syndrome in Australia — the hereditary cancer testing and funding gap**: Universal dMMR tumour testing is recommended to identify Lynch syndrome, but access is inequitable, germline testing pathways vary by jurisdiction, and there is no national cascade testing framework. A significant number of Australians with Lynch syndrome remain undiagnosed.

2. **The POLE-mut de-escalation question (MSAC 1790)**: POLE-mutant endometrial cancer has the best prognosis of any subtype yet most affected women still receive standard adjuvant treatment. MSAC 1790 is the mechanism for correcting this — examining what the MBS funding of POLE genotyping would mean for treatment de-escalation, quality of life, and cost savings.

3. **The molecular pathology workforce and laboratory infrastructure**: Implementing four-subtype molecular classification for endometrial cancer (dMMR, POLE, NSMP, p53abn) requires IHC, PCR, NGS, and sequencing capacity at scale. Australia's pathology laboratory funding model — including bulk-billing pathology and the MBS pathology schedule — may not be structured to incentivise this investment.

4. **Endometrial cancer and health equity — obesity, socioeconomic status, and First Nations women**: The rising incidence is disproportionately concentrated in lower-socioeconomic and rural/remote populations where obesity prevalence is highest. First Nations women have lower access to timely diagnosis and treatment. Dostarlimab, however transformative, will not reach them without deliberate equity investment.

5. **Durvalumab (Imfinzi) in endometrial cancer — the DUO-E trial and the PBS competition**: AstraZeneca's durvalumab was also evaluated in the same indication. The PBAC noted in its assessment that pembrolizumab did not demonstrate equivalence to dostarlimab or durvalumab for advanced endometrial cancer. A durvalumab PBS listing, if sought, would create the first therapeutic competition in this space.
