# Early-Onset Colorectal Cancer: Rising Rates, the Birth Cohort Effect, and Australia's NBCSP Age Expansion

**Report 078 — 2026-08-23**

**Summary:** Colorectal cancer in Australians under 50 has more than doubled in case numbers since 2000 and risen sharply in age-specific incidence rates across every young adult cohort. Australia already has the highest rates of early-onset bowel cancer in the world. This alarming trend prompted the 2024 expansion of the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP) to include 45–49 year olds on an opt-in basis — a decision backed by cost-effectiveness modelling and updated clinical guidelines. Yet the opt-in architecture of the expansion, the compound disadvantage facing First Nations young adults, and accumulating evidence on causes and birth cohort patterns are sustaining a serious debate about whether 45 is the right floor, or whether it should be lowered to 40.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 077 identified the NBCSP colonoscopy follow-up gap as a systemic equity failure at the post-positive-result stage. Thread 3 noted that the 2024 expansion to 45–49 year olds — and the evidence base behind it — had not yet been examined. Rising early-onset colorectal cancer is both the clinical driver of that age expansion and a distinct healthcare-payments challenge: a screening program that does not reach a growing at-risk younger population will encounter an even larger downstream treatment cost burden as late-stage diagnoses accumulate.

---

## Background: A Global Trend With an Australian Peak

Early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) — defined internationally as colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosed in patients under 50 — is rising across high-income countries. It is rising faster in Australia than almost anywhere else.

According to Cancer Australia's early-onset cancer statistics dashboard:

- The number of EOCRC cases diagnosed annually in Australia **more than doubled** from approximately 960 in 2000 to an estimated **2,045 cases in 2024**
- Deaths from EOCRC rose by **35% between 2000 and 2024**, despite advances in treatment, reflecting both increasing incidence and the fact that young-onset cancers are frequently diagnosed at a later, less treatable stage
- Australia has the **highest rates of early-onset bowel cancer** of any country for which comparable data exists

The demographic breakdown is notable. More females than males under 50 are diagnosed with CRC — an estimated 1,062 cases in females versus 983 in males in 2024, with females aged under 50 having a slightly higher incidence rate (12 vs 11 per 100,000 respectively). This reverses the pattern seen in older-onset CRC, where males are substantially more affected.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Age-Specific Incidence Trends

Increases in EOCRC are not uniform across age bands. Cancer Australia data shows striking heterogeneity:

| Age group | Rate per 100,000 (2000) | Rate per 100,000 (estimated 2025) | Change |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------------------------|--------|
| 20–29 years | 2.2 | 4.6 | +109% |
| 30–39 years | 6.3 | ~19 | +202% |
| 40–49 years | 25 | ~31 | +24% |

The sharpest increases are in the youngest cohorts — particularly 30–39 year olds, where age-specific incidence has tripled over 25 years. A 2025 landmark population-based time-series analysis published in *The Lancet Regional Health — Western Pacific* analysed Australian Cancer Registry data from 1990 to 2020 for all colorectal, appendiceal, and anal tumours. Key findings:

- Early-onset incidence rose **5–9% annually** across sites between 1990 and 2020
- This generated **4,072 excess cases** (1.5 per 100,000 person-years) above expected rates, with rectal cancers accounting for 48% of excess cases, colonic 28%, appendiceal 15%, and anal 9%
- The rise is predominantly in **adenocarcinoma** (non-mucinous) for colorectal cancers, with the most dramatic increase in appendiceal mucinous adenocarcinoma

A critical finding from this analysis was a **birth cohort effect**: incidence rate ratios for adenocarcinoma increased consistently with each successive birth cohort since 1960. Compared to the 1950s birth cohort, the 1990s birth cohort showed 2–3-fold higher colorectal adenocarcinoma incidence and **7-fold higher appendiceal adenocarcinoma**. This suggests that the rise is driven by exposures or environmental factors affecting Australians born from the 1960s onward — not simply by improved detection or earlier diagnosis.

Melbourne-based researcher Professor Dan Buchanan, featured in the ABC's July 2025 *Four Corners* "Generation Cancer" investigation, has shown that young-onset colorectal cancers are molecularly distinct from those diagnosed in older patients: **he can distinguish young from old tumours from DNA patterns alone**. This points to specific biological mechanisms affecting younger patients, not just earlier detection of the same disease process that affects the elderly.

### 2. Proposed Causes: An Unsettled Aetiology

The causes of rising EOCRC remain contested, but a convergent body of evidence points to environmental and dietary exposures since childhood — consistent with the birth cohort patterns seen in Australian cancer registry data:

**Ultra-processed foods (UPFs):** A 2025 Harvard/Mass General Brigham study examining over 75,000 women found that those consuming the most ultra-processed foods faced a **45% higher risk of developing young-onset colorectal adenomas** (pre-cancerous polyps) compared with the lowest consumers. Subjects in the fifth quintile of caloric intake from UPFs were significantly more likely to develop early-onset precursors. A separate analysis published in *PMC (Oncology News Australia)* linked UPF consumption directly to rising early-onset adenoma rates in Australian data.

**Microbiome disruption:** Proposed mechanisms include reduced microbial diversity (dysbiosis) associated with high-sugar, high-fat, low-fibre Western diets; altered bile acid metabolism; and reduced short-chain fatty acid production from diminished dietary fibre. The *PMC* literature consistently identifies a pattern of decreased microbial diversity as a common denominator across early-onset CRC cases.

**Dietary risk factors (Global Burden of Disease 2019):** A GBD-linked analysis across 204 countries found low milk, low whole grains, low calcium, low fibre, high red meat, and high processed meat as the primary attributable dietary risks for EOCRC — a dietary profile that maps closely onto the Australian population's dietary trajectory from the 1960s–1990s.

**Chemicals and environmental exposures:** The ABC's *Four Corners* "Generation Cancer" investigation (July 2025, Dr Norman Swan) examined emerging evidence on endocrine-disrupting chemicals and plastics as possible contributors to earlier-onset cancers, including CRC. This remains exploratory and not yet evidence-based enough for clinical guidelines, but it aligns with the unexplained residual rise in CRC risk not fully accounted for by dietary factors alone.

### 3. The 2024 NBCSP Age Expansion to 45–49 Years

In response to rising EOCRC rates and updated clinical evidence, the National Health and Medical Research Council approved revised clinical practice guidelines for colorectal cancer in 2023, recommending that biennial iFOBT screening for the asymptomatic Australian population be offered **from age 45** (extended from the prior lower bound of 50).

The Government acted on this recommendation in the 2024–25 Budget, implementing the change from **1 July 2024**:

- Australians aged **45–49** can now request a free bowel cancer screening kit from the National Cancer Screening Register or from their GP — participation is **opt-in** (unlike the 50–74 cohort, who receive kits automatically by mail every two years)
- The 50–74 cohort remains on the automatic call-recall pathway

**Early performance data (AIHW 2026 Monitoring Report):**
- **236,421 screening kits** were requested by people aged 45–49 in the period to the 2026 monitoring window
- **56.2% of kits requested were returned** — higher than the 42% overall program participation rate for the 50–74 cohort, reflecting the self-selection effect of an opt-in system (those who actively request a kit are more motivated)
- **4.7% positivity rate** among 45–49 year old returners — lower than the ~6–7% in the 50–74 cohort, consistent with expected lower background prevalence in the younger age group but still clinically meaningful

**Cost-effectiveness:**
Modelling of the age extension (Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention 2019; updated modelling 2025) found:
- Screening from 45 to 74 years remained **cost-effective** at an indicative willingness-to-pay threshold of A$50,000 per life-year saved, with an ICER of approximately **A$17,053–A$29,512 per LYS**
- Screening from 40 to 74 years also showed **health benefits in modelling** but produced a higher ICER, deemed not cost-effective at the A$50,000 threshold given current iFOBT costs, colonoscopy follow-up resource use, and yield

### 4. First Nations Australians: A Compound Early-Onset Burden

A 2025 Queensland-based study published in *Cancer Medicine (Wiley)* quantified early-onset bowel cancer among First Nations Peoples in Queensland:

- First Nations Australians had **2.6 times the odds** of being diagnosed with early-onset CRC compared with non-Indigenous counterparts
- 5-year survival was **73% for First Nations vs 77% for non-Indigenous** early-onset patients — a gap that persists even when adjusted for stage at diagnosis
- The disparity was driven by a combination of later-stage diagnosis, lower participation in NBCSP screening, and differential access to treatment

A Lancet Global Health systematic review (2026) examining global burden of early-onset bowel cancer among Indigenous populations internationally confirmed that the burden of EOCRC in Indigenous communities globally is substantially underestimated because Indigenous status is absent from most cancer registry datasets. Australia's data, though incomplete, represents some of the best available evidence of this disparity.

The NBCSP's 45–49 expansion is opt-in — meaning that the cohort most underserved by call-recall mechanisms (First Nations, rural and remote, non-English-speaking backgrounds) will be least reached by the expansion. Structural access barriers mean the opt-in design may amplify rather than reduce equity disparities in the 45–49 cohort.

### 5. The Age 40 Debate

Bowel Cancer Australia has maintained a "Screen from Age 40" campaign, arguing that:
- The epidemiological evidence now supports CRC screening beginning at 40 for general population groups
- For First Nations Australians and other high-risk groups (prior polyps, family history), the case for starting at 40 is even stronger
- The US updated its colorectal cancer screening recommendations to age 45 in 2021 (USPSTF); the American Cancer Society has recommended beginning at 45 since 2018; some researchers argue Australia's high rates warrant starting at 40

A ScienceDaily release from April 2025 summarised modelling calling for "more tailored bowel cancer surveillance" based on updated risk stratification, arguing that uniform age-based eligibility criteria are insufficient when individual risk profiles (family history, ethnic background, dietary and lifestyle factors) vary substantially across the eligible population.

The clinical guidelines authors acknowledged that starting at 40 "had health benefits" in modelling but rejected it on cost-effectiveness grounds. Critics argue this framing underestimates long-term treatment cost savings from earlier detection, particularly given rising EOCRC incidence that will make the 40–44 cohort increasingly high-risk.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Opt-In Architecture Limits Reach in the Highest-Risk 45–49 Cohort

The 56.2% return rate among 45–49 year olds who requested kits looks impressive, but the denominator is self-selected. Estimates of how many of the total 45–49 eligible population (~1.5 million Australians) have actually engaged with the program in its first two years are far lower. The program's architecture shifts responsibility from the system (automatic dispatch) to the individual (request a kit), precisely in the cohort where EOCRC risk is rising fastest and where younger Australians — who are less likely to engage with preventive health services — constitute the target group.

### Tension 2: Awareness and GP Engagement Are Critical Failure Points

The opt-in model depends on either proactive individuals requesting kits online, or GPs recommending screening at clinical consultations. But 45–49 year olds are less likely to see their GP regularly for preventive health checks, and GPs are not systematically prompted to recommend the NBCSP to 45–49 year olds in the way that automatic dispatch prompts screening participation for 50–74 year olds. There is no dedicated MBS item for a "bowel cancer screening conversation" with a 45–49 year old patient.

### Tension 3: The Cause Is Unclear, Limiting Prevention Options

Rising EOCRC in Australia is real and documented, but its aetiology remains contested. Ultra-processed food consumption is the most evidenced modifiable risk factor, but neither the NBCSP's design nor the broader healthcare system has a clear lever for dietary reform. The programme can detect cancer earlier; it cannot prevent the exposures that appear to be causing it. A prevention-oriented response would require cross-portfolio action (food regulation, health promotion, UPF taxation) that sits well outside health department jurisdiction.

### Tension 4: The 40-Year-Old Who Gets CRC Today Had No Programme When They Were 45

The NBCSP only began operating for 45–49 year olds in July 2024. A person who is 45 today — who is precisely in the age group with the fastest rising incidence — is only now eligible for the first time. The cohort currently aged 40–44 remains outside the program unless they have risk factors placing them in a surveillance pathway. As the birth cohort effect matures, the 40–44 group will carry a substantially higher EOCRC burden than 40–44 year olds a decade ago.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Colorectal cancer treatment costs and the stage-at-diagnosis funding equation.** The clinical urgency of catching CRC early is inseparable from the economic logic: stage I CRC costs approximately $14,000 to treat; stage IV costs $150,000+, with ongoing chemotherapy. The NBCSP's age expansion changes the stage distribution of detected cancers — how large is that downstream saving, and how does it compare to the screening programme's cost?

2. **MBS items for colonoscopy 32090 and 32093: fee structure, gap payments, and reform options.** A closer examination of what Medicare actually pays for diagnostic and surveillance colonoscopy, the gap between schedule fees and actual charges, and whether targeted MBS reform could improve access equity for uninsured patients with a positive NBCSP result in the 45–49 cohort.

3. **Surveillance colonoscopy overuse and the guideline compliance gap.** As flagged in Report 077, 30–40% of repeat surveillance colonoscopies in Australia occur at intervals shorter than evidence-based guidelines recommend. The ACSQHC's September 2025 Atlas found this overuse diverting capacity away from post-NBCSP follow-up patients. What would a targeted MBS reform of surveillance interval compliance look like?

4. **Bowel Cancer Australia's "Screen from Age 40" campaign: the economic case for going further.** The modelling that justified the 45-year threshold used assumptions about test cost, colonoscopy follow-up rates, and EOCRC incidence that are now outdated. With incidence rising, does the ICER for starting at 40 still clear $50,000/LYS? An examination of what an updated MSAC evaluation would likely conclude.

5. **BreastScreen dense-breast notification and personalised screening: a model for risk-stratified NBCSP?** Several screening programs globally are moving from population-level age eligibility to risk-stratified eligibility, incorporating family history, genetic risk, lifestyle factors, and imaging findings. Could the NBCSP adopt a risk-stratified approach to identify 40–44 year olds who should be offered screening early?
