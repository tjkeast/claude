# Maternity Services and the Antenatal Care Payment Architecture: Australia's Three-Tier Birth System, the Private Obstetrics Crisis, and the $42 GP Shared Care Problem

**One-line summary:** Australia's maternity payment system funds a high-quality public hospital sector that most women use while leaving private obstetrics in structural crisis — driven by outdated MBS rebates, PHI gold-tier-only access, and at least 14 private hospital maternity unit closures since 2018.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Yesterday's report on iron deficiency identified IV iron administration in pregnancy as a critical care gap with no MBS administration item; today's report examines the broader payment architecture governing all antenatal and maternity services in Australia and the structural tensions that produce such gaps.

---

## Background

Australia records approximately 305,000–315,000 births per year (the rate has been declining, with total fertility at 1.50 in 2023, a record low). These births are spread across a three-tier system — public hospitals, private hospitals, and a small but growing community/home-birth sector — each funded through different payment mechanisms and offering substantially different out-of-pocket costs, care continuity, and model choice.

The payment architecture for Australian maternity care sits at the intersection of four funding streams:

1. **Medicare (Commonwealth):** MBS rebates for GP antenatal shared care, specialist obstetrician attendances, and midwife-led care
2. **Public hospital funding (Commonwealth–State):** Activity-based funding via IHACPA's National Efficient Price, flowing through the National Hospital Funding Agreement
3. **Private health insurance:** Gold-tier hospital cover for private obstetric admission
4. **Out-of-pocket payments:** The gap between what MBS and PHI reimburse and what specialists and hospitals charge

The system has delivered broadly good clinical outcomes — Australia's perinatal mortality rate of 11.0 per 1,000 births (2023, AIHW) is among the lowest in the world, and maternal mortality is rare. But the payment architecture is increasingly dysfunctional: a GP shared care item frozen in design since 1991, a private obstetrics sector in financial crisis, a midwifery workforce only recently freed from a decade-long administrative barrier, and caesarean rates at 41% — twice the 1990s level — driven partly by incentives embedded in how the private system is paid.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Three-Tier Birth System: Free, Expensive, or Unavailable

**Public hospitals** are the dominant birth setting (~70% of births). For women holding a Medicare card, a vaginal delivery in a public hospital carries out-of-pocket costs approaching zero for the birth itself — though ancillary costs (parking, private room upgrades, some scans) can total up to ~$1,000. Antenatal shared care visits through a GP are Medicare-rebated, and most diagnostic tests (blood tests, glucose tolerance) attract bulk billing. Public patients accept a hospital's rostered staff model: continuity with a single named midwife or obstetrician is rare and depends on what the hospital offers.

**Private hospitals** handle around 25–30% of births (declining). A private birth even with appropriate private health insurance typically costs $3,000–$10,000+ out-of-pocket for the full episode (antenatal + birth + postnatal), and in major cities, obstetrician gap fees can exceed $10,000 on their own for complex cases. Private Healthcare Australia's CEO has noted first consultation fees of up to $650 for a private obstetrician — before accounting for ongoing management fees. The appeal is continuity of care with a named specialist and greater control over birth setting preferences.

**Homebirth and birth centre models** serve a small but growing proportion of low-risk births. Some birth centres are co-located with hospitals (public, low or no cost); standalone homebirth with a registered midwife involves MBS-rebatable midwife items but substantial out-of-pocket gaps.

### 2. The MBS Antenatal Architecture — Outdated by Design

Australia's MBS antenatal payment framework rests on a handful of items, several of which have not had their descriptors meaningfully updated since 1991.

**Routine antenatal attendance (Item 16500):** The core item used by both GPs and specialist obstetricians for routine visits. The schedule fee is approximately $42–$43 (post July 2025 indexation). RACGP has noted that the item descriptor itself has not been substantively reviewed since 1991 — it does not reflect current evidence-based care requirements (e.g., depression screening, domestic violence inquiry, VBAC counselling) that GPs are expected to provide in each visit. At ~$42 per visit, a GP seeing 10–12 antenatal patients per month earns only ~$420–$504/month from antenatal items for that cohort, even before accounting for non-billable time.

**GP shared care management (Item 16591):** A once-per-pregnancy item for a GP who manages antenatal care after 28 weeks but will not deliver the baby (the patient delivers as a public patient). This item was historically claimable from 20 weeks but the 2021 MBS Review Obstetrics Clinical Committee recommended restricting to 28 weeks to reduce inappropriate claiming. The fee is a modest add-on amount paid once. A 2023 Australian Doctor report headlined it as "The $41.70 Medicare item for GP antenatal shared care 'failing women'" — the figure varies slightly with indexation but the substance is unchanged.

**Private obstetric management (Item 16590):** The equivalent item for a provider who will deliver a private patient, claimable once per pregnancy after 28 weeks. Items 16590 and 16591 cannot both be claimed for the same pregnancy.

**Total GP shared care economics:** A GP providing all routine antenatal visits for a shared-care patient receives item 16500 per visit plus item 16591 once. With ~6–8 GP visits in a shared-care model, total Medicare revenue for the GP is perhaps $300–$380 for the entire antenatal episode. The 2021 MBS Review taskforce recommended a 25% fee increase for item 16590, and the RACGP has consistently argued for a new tiered antenatal care plan item covering up to 10 visits. As of mid-2026, neither reform has been implemented.

### 3. The March 2025 Midwifery Reform: A Decade-Long Barrier Removed

The most significant MBS maternity reform in years took effect on **1 March 2025**, backed by $56.5 million in the 2024-25 Budget. The headline change was the **removal of the Collaborative Arrangement requirement** for participating midwives.

Prior to March 2025, midwives providing Medicare-rebatable care were required to have a formal collaborative arrangement with a specified medical practitioner — a requirement the Australian College of Midwives described as "a decade-long barrier" that prevented many midwives from practising independently and blocked women in rural and remote areas from accessing midwifery-led continuity of care.

New and amended items from 1 March 2025 include:
- **Item 82100** (amended): Minimum 60-minute antenatal attendance by a participating midwife
- **Item 82102** (new): Long antenatal attendance of at least 90 minutes
- **Item 82103** (new): Complex antenatal care up to three times per pregnancy — for situations leading to hospital admission
- **Item 82115**: Midwifery care plan (once per pregnancy only)
- Telehealth item **91219**: Routine antenatal phone attendance by a midwife, ≥40 minutes, schedule fee $89.00
- **Postnatal care extension**: From 6 weeks to 8 weeks post-birth; a new postnatal item includes mental health and domestic violence screening and a birth debrief

These changes are designed to enable genuine midwifery continuity of care (MLCC) — a model where one or a small team of midwives provides care throughout pregnancy, birth and the postnatal period. Evidence consistently shows MLCC reduces interventions, improves satisfaction and reduces preterm birth rates. Yet pre-reform, fewer than 10% of Australian women could access MLCC despite strong demand. Post-reform uptake data are not yet available, but structural barriers to private-practice midwifery remain.

### 4. The Private Obstetrics Crisis: 14 Unit Closures and a Gender Bias Argument

RANZCOG has documented **at least 14 private hospital maternity unit closures since 2018**. The Health Workforce Division's April 2026 O&G Compendium confirms that between 2015-16 and 2022-23, nine private hospitals closed their maternity wards, with two hospitals ceasing maternity services entirely. A declining birth rate, high fixed costs, expensive medical indemnity, and the cost-of-living pressure on PHI uptake are all contributory — but RANZCOG and the National Association of Specialist Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (NASOG) argue that the structural problem is **gender bias in private health insurance architecture**.

The argument runs as follows:
- Obstetric and gynaecological services require **Gold tier** private health insurance — the most expensive tier
- Despite this, O&G services are **excluded from the PHI risk equalisation pool**, which means insurers do not receive the same cross-subsidy for high-cost obstetric claims that they do for other high-cost procedures
- The MBS rebates for obstetric services are lower relative to other specialties of comparable complexity — RANZCOG argues this reflects historical gender devaluation of women's health
- Obstetricians face the highest medical indemnity premiums in Australia, partly because of lifelong liability for birth-related injuries; they pass these costs through to patients as gap fees
- The result is an affordability trap: women needing private O&G care pay the highest premiums but get the least insurance value

At RANZCOG's November 2025 Private Practice Roundtable, six reform options were identified, including: adding O&G to the PHI risk equalisation pool; reforming all MBS obstetric rebates to reflect true cost; and piloting team-based and collaborative private care models. As of mid-2026, none has been implemented.

### 5. The Caesarean Section Rate and Payment System Incentives

Australia's caesarean section (CS) rate reached **41% in 2023** (AIHW), up from ~20% in the early 1990s and well above the WHO's recommended maximum of 10–15% for clinical necessity. The OECD average in 2017 was 28%; Australia is above that and rising.

The private sector drives this disparity. Private hospital CS rates consistently run 10–15 percentage points higher than public hospital rates for comparable patient populations. Payment system incentives are widely cited:
- Obstetricians are paid a higher MBS fee for operative delivery (item 16511, ~$1,400+ schedule fee) than for a managed vaginal delivery
- PHI funds the hospital costs of an elective CS equivalently to a complicated vaginal birth from an insurer's perspective
- Scheduled CS eliminates uncertainty in obstetrician time management for a private practice
- Medical indemnity risk calculations may favour elective CS for certain maternal presentations

The 2021 MBS Review Obstetrics Clinical Committee identified these incentive misalignments but did not recommend fee restructuring to address them. No bundled payment model for maternity care — which could neutralise procedure-specific incentives — has been adopted in Australia, despite IHACPA having evaluated the concept in 2017 and ongoing advocacy for it from the ACM and QNMU.

### 6. Hospital Funding: ABF and the Maternity DRG Problem

Public hospitals are funded through Activity Based Funding using IHACPA's **National Efficient Price (NEP)**. For maternity care, this means hospitals are paid based on Diagnosis Related Groups (DRGs) assigned to birth episodes. The main maternity DRGs cover vaginal delivery, caesarean section, and related complications — each with a different ABF price weight.

Several problems arise:
- **Midwifery Continuity of Care is undervalued in the DRG system:** An MLCC birth costs more in midwife time but generates no DRG premium; a CS generates a higher DRG weight. Hospitals face a financial incentive toward higher-intervention models
- **Community-based antenatal care is largely invisible to ABF:** Public antenatal clinics in hospitals are funded through block grants or state health system budgets, not activity-based pricing
- **Social complexity is underweighted:** Women who are homeless, experiencing family violence, or with substance use disorders require substantially more antenatal care coordination than the DRG price reflects

The IHACPA evaluated a **bundled payment model for maternity care** in 2017 — a single price covering all care from the antenatal booking visit through to postnatal discharge. The evaluation found it technically feasible but identified risks around price adequacy and the challenges of attributing costs across state/Commonwealth funding boundaries. The proposal was not pursued.

### 7. Equity Dimensions

The maternity payment architecture produces documented equity gaps:

**First Nations women:** Face a maternal mortality rate approximately four times the non-Indigenous rate. The National Maternity Workforce Strategy 2026–2036 and the Birthing on Country roadmap (2026–2036, CDU/Molly Institute) aim to redesign services — but neither comes with major new Commonwealth funding. The MLCC model and ACCHO-based midwifery are critical for culturally safe care.

**Rural and remote women:** Access to specialist obstetrics is increasingly dependent on FIFO or FAFO (fly-in-fly-out/drive-in-drive-out) practitioners. RANZCOG's FROGS rural training pathway (launching 2025) and the OGET program embed trainees in peripheral sites, but retention post-training remains the systemic challenge.

**Low-income women:** The caesarean access equity gradient runs backwards: CS rates are *lower* in the most disadvantaged areas (35% vs 38% least disadvantaged, 2023 AIHW data) — not because low-SES women are choosing vaginal birth but because they largely access public hospitals where clinical decision-making differs from private. The private obstetrics premium is simply inaccessible.

**Women with complexity:** The MBS item structure funds episodic antenatal visits poorly; there is no MBS item for care coordination, case conference, or joint management between GP, obstetrician and midwife for high-risk pregnancies — analogous to the chronic disease care plan items under MyMedicare.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Can the GP antenatal shared care model survive?** With Item 16500 at ~$42 per visit and Item 16591 a one-off management fee, the GP economics of antenatal care are marginal. If GPs divert to higher-MBS-value consultations (MHCP, CDM plans), the shared care model fragments.

2. **Will the March 2025 midwifery reforms actually shift uptake?** Removing collaborative arrangements is necessary but not sufficient. Private practice midwives still face high indemnity costs (~$7,000–$12,000/year), PHI integration gaps, and hospital credentialling barriers. Whether the MBS item changes translate into material uptake expansion remains unknown.

3. **PHI risk equalisation reform: When?** RANZCOG and NASOG's April 2025 parliamentary push for O&G risk equalisation has not produced legislative action. If private maternity unit closures accelerate, the pressure point moves from advocacy to emergency.

4. **CS rate: Is this a problem, and who owns it?** The CS rate involves clinical, cultural, and financial factors. The payment system contribution is real but rarely named in government reporting. IHACPA could study it through ABF price reform; the government hasn't asked.

5. **National Maternity Workforce Strategy vs funding reform:** The 2026–2036 strategy explicitly excludes industrial matters and models of care. It is a workforce headcount document, not a payment reform document. The underlying MBS architecture — unchanged in design since 1991 — is not in scope.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Peripartum haemorrhage and Patient Blood Management:** Australia loses over 1,500 women/year to severe postpartum haemorrhage; Patient Blood Management is a proven, underfunded system response. Directly connects to yesterday's iron deficiency report.

2. **Birthing on Country and First Nations maternity services:** The ACCHO-based model, the National Roadmap for Birthing on Country 2026–2036, and the gap between workforce strategy and payment architecture for culturally safe maternity care.

3. **Medical indemnity and the maternity liability tail:** Obstetricians face lifelong liability for birth-related brain injuries (cerebral palsy, HIE); the cost of this tail is borne via MDA/MDO premiums; the National Injury Insurance Scheme (NIIS) does not yet cover birth-related injury in all states, creating a gap that feeds through to patient costs.

4. **Private health insurance reform for women's health:** The broader case for adding O&G to the PHI risk equalisation pool and reclassifying basic obstetric services below Gold tier — a reform with implications well beyond maternity.

5. **GP incentives and the preventive antenatal care gap:** Whether a new antenatal care plan item (analogous to MHCPs or CDM plans) could fund the GP time required for evidence-based antenatal care, reducing reliance on the $42 routine item.
