# The TGA-to-PBS Gap: 726 Days, Stalled Negotiations, and the Pre-Listing Access Patchwork

**One-line summary:** The average gap between TGA regulatory approval and PBS listing has blown out to 726 days — almost two years — driven by bottlenecked price negotiations, repeated PBAC submissions, and a systemic failure to implement HTA reform; patients bridging this gap rely on a patchwork of special access schemes, company-run programs, and private out-of-pocket spending that is neither equitable nor sustainable.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 109 examined how PBS managed entry agreements and deed-of-agreement structures are used to list medicines that fail standard cost-effectiveness thresholds — but reaching a deed requires first completing a price negotiation. The TGA-to-PBS gap is what happens when that negotiation fails, stalls, or when multiple PBAC submission rounds are required before any listing can begin.

---

## Background

Every medicine dispensed on the PBS has travelled two separate regulatory highways. The first — TGA registration on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) — determines whether a product is safe and effective enough to be supplied in Australia. The second — PBAC recommendation and PBS listing — determines whether the Commonwealth will subsidise it and at what price.

These processes are formally independent. A medicine can receive TGA approval and sit without PBS listing for years. During that interval, Australian patients may have no subsidised pathway to access a medicine that their oncologist can see in the ARTG and their NHS-equivalent counterpart in the UK can access on the NHS.

The gap between those two outcomes — TGA approval and PBS listing — is the **patient access gap**, and it has grown to crisis proportions.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Gap Is Now 726 Days and Growing

The Australian Patient Access Gap Tracker, maintained by pharmaceutical company Amgen and tracking new drug-indication pairs, recorded an average TGA-to-PBS access gap of **726 days** in 2025 — almost exactly two years. This figure represents an increase of 135 days from the same tracker's 2024 reading of 591 days, and roughly 260 days worse than estimates from earlier in the decade (466 days).

For context:
- **Germany**: ~136 days (immediate listing under AMNOG unless HTA finds no added benefit)
- **Japan**: ~102 days (parallel review with accelerated pricing)
- **United Kingdom**: ~156 days (NICE appraisal + NHS commercial framework)
- **Sweden**: ~325 days
- **Australia**: ~726 days (2025)

Australia has ranked near the bottom of comparable OECD countries for medicine access for more than a decade. Medicines Australia's historical COMPARE analysis placed Australia 18th of 20 OECD comparators; more recent IQVIA analysis found that while 60% of new medicines are reimbursed within 12 months in Australia, the comparable OECD benchmark is closer to 60% within six months — Australia is behind both on timing and on depth of access.

The 726-day figure covers only medicines that eventually reach the PBS. It does not count medicines for which negotiations are abandoned without a PBS listing — situations that the tucatinib case (see below) illustrates.

### 2. Why the Gap Exists: The Two-Stage Bottleneck

The access gap is not produced by a single cause. It accumulates across two sequential obstacles:

**Stage 1 — PBAC recommendation.** A medicine must first receive a positive PBAC recommendation. This requires the sponsor to lodge a major submission — typically a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the medicine against the current best alternative. Research examining 634 PBAC submissions between 2005 and 2018 found that the median time from first submission to a positive recommendation was 16 months. More starkly, 70% of medicine-population pairings that had at least one cost-effectiveness-based submission between 2021 and 2025 required multiple PBAC submissions before receiving a positive recommendation. Each failed submission delays access by a further six months (the PBAC meets three times per year, with an additional intracycle meeting since 2025).

**Stage 2 — Price negotiation.** A positive PBAC recommendation opens price negotiations between the sponsor and the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing. But it does not guarantee a listing. A formal listing occurs only when a "price offer package" is agreed. Research shows that 71% of individual negotiation rounds end without a deal. A recommendation becomes "inactive" if no acceptable price offer is received within six months; if no active negotiation is occurring six months after lodging a price offer, the Department may cease providing pricing services.

These stages compound: a medicine that requires two PBAC submissions before a positive recommendation, followed by two rounds of negotiation before a price agreement, has easily consumed two to three years before a single PBS prescription is dispensed.

### 3. Medicines That Never Make It: The Tucatinib Case

The access gap measure captures only medicines that eventually reach the PBS. An equally serious problem — less visible in aggregate statistics — is medicines for which negotiations fail entirely and sponsors withdraw.

Tucatinib (Tukysa), a HER2-targeted oral therapy developed by Seagen and commercialised by Pfizer for metastatic breast cancer with brain metastases, illustrates this failure mode. The PBAC gave a positive recommendation at its November 2025 meeting, explicitly recognising high unmet clinical need for patients with HER2-positive brain metastases who have limited or no alternatives. NICE recommended tucatinib for NHS use in the UK in 2022.

Despite the positive PBAC recommendation, Pfizer walked away from PBS pricing negotiations without an agreed deal. As of mid-2026, approximately 300 Australian women with metastatic breast cancer cannot access the medicine at a subsidised price. Private access costs approximately $8,000 per treatment cycle, with a total out-of-pocket cap of around $64,000. For a medicine approved in the US and listed in the UK years earlier, this outcome is a direct product of the negotiation dysfunction at the heart of Australia's PBS listing process.

Tucatinib is not an isolated case. A pattern of sponsor disengagement has emerged as negotiations become more protracted and the Department's price offers — constrained by tight budget envelopes and PBAC's minimum effective price conditions — fall further below sponsor expectations. Pfizer's decision to pause broader engagement with the Department of Health's Drugs and Advanced Therapies Group was reported in 2025, reflecting not just one medicine but a systemic commercial relationship breakdown.

### 4. Pre-PBS Access Pathways: What Exists in the Gap

For patients who cannot wait — patients with advanced cancers, progressive neurological conditions, or rare diseases with no alternative treatment — several pre-PBS access mechanisms exist. None is a satisfactory substitute for PBS listing.

**Special Access Scheme (SAS) — Category B:** The most commonly used TGA pathway for pre-PBS access. A prescribing doctor applies to the TGA for approval to prescribe an unapproved or unlisted medicine for a single named patient. TGA approval typically takes 2–5 working days. "Unapproved" covers both medicines not registered on the ARTG at all, and ARTG-registered medicines used for indications not yet on their registered label. SAS Category B approvals have grown dramatically: the TGA received more than 264,000 SAS submissions in 2023, compared to fewer than 60,000 a decade earlier. A substantial portion of this growth reflects the explosion of medicinal cannabis prescribing, but TGA-approved, PBS-unlisted medicines in the access gap account for a meaningful share.

SAS Category B has critical limitations. The TGA approves the prescribing; it does not fund the medicine. The patient pays full cost privately unless the company provides the medicine free of charge through a compassionate access program, or a state health system formulary covers it in a hospital. The administrative burden on prescribers — filling individual TGA applications per patient per medicine — is real, and the documentation required discourages access, particularly in community settings.

**SAS Category A:** For medicines immediately required to save a life or prevent serious permanent injury. Category A requires no prior TGA approval; the doctor notifies the TGA within 28 days. The threshold is deliberately high and is not a general pre-PBS access pathway.

**Authorised Prescriber (AP) Scheme:** A prescribing doctor can apply to become an Authorised Prescriber for a medicine for an entire class of patients (e.g., "adults with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer"). AP status requires either Human Research Ethics Committee endorsement or relevant specialist college endorsement, and carries ongoing obligations to report patient numbers to the TGA every six months. The AP scheme reduces per-patient administrative burden relative to SAS Category B but is only available where the medicine is well-established and the prescribing class is clearly defined.

**Company-sponsored Managed Access Programs (MAPs):** Pharmaceutical companies often operate named-patient programs or expanded access programs to provide their medicines free or at reduced cost to patients in the TGA-to-PBS gap. These programs vary widely: some are structured as formal clinical observation programs with data collection; others are straightforward compassionate supply. They are voluntary — companies are under no obligation to run them — and they create a perverse incentive structure: a company that provides its medicine for free pre-PBS is effectively reducing the PBAC's budget concern, since patients already receiving the medicine create pressure to list.

The Medicines Access Portal (MAP), run by Rare Cancers Australia, provides a single online gateway listing compassionate access and special access programs that pharmaceutical companies make available in Australia. Its existence reflects how fragmented and non-standardised the landscape is.

### 5. The Financial Architecture of the Gap: Who Bears the Cost?

During the TGA-to-PBS gap, the cost of medicines is either borne by patients out-of-pocket, written off as charitable supply by companies, or shifted to state and territory hospital formularies.

**Patient out-of-pocket:** For uninsured patients or those lacking access to a company program, the cost of a TGA-registered but PBS-unlisted medicine is paid entirely out-of-pocket. For oncology medicines — the largest category affected — this can reach tens of thousands of dollars per year. Private health insurance covers some in-hospital treatments but generally does not cover outpatient chemotherapy or oral cancer medicines prescribed in clinic.

**State formularies:** Public hospitals operate their own drugs and therapeutics committees, which can approve hospital formulary listing for medicines not yet on the PBS. Hospital formulary listing covers in-patient or in-hospital outpatient use, often at specialty cancer centres. This has become an ad hoc national patchwork: a medicine may be available free at a public hospital in NSW but not in Queensland; a patient who is clinically equivalent may pay $0 or $40,000 depending on their geography and the hospital they access.

**Company compassionate supply:** Pharmaceutical companies operating in Australia increasingly regard pre-PBS compassionate supply as a necessary cost of maintaining clinical relationships and generating registry data that will support the eventual PBAC submission. This creates a dynamic in which the company, not the Commonwealth, bears the cost of access during the gap — but the company's interest in continuing to supply free product is time-limited, creating a supply-termination risk for patients.

### 6. The Reform Agenda and Its Implementation Gap

The HTA Policy and Methods Review, finalised in September 2024, identified the TGA-to-PBS access gap as a critical structural failure and made specific recommendations:

**Bridging Fund (Recommendation 20):** A Commonwealth-funded mechanism to provide temporary PBS-equivalent subsidised access for medicines with high added therapeutic value addressing high unmet clinical need, between TGA registration and PBS listing. The Bridging Fund would operate under defined eligibility criteria (high unmet need, strong efficacy evidence, time-critical disease) and would function as a conditional bridge, not as a permanent access pathway, with normal PBS listing still required. Medicines Australia, the Bridging Funding Coalition (a consumer-industry coalition), and NeuroEndocrine Cancer Australia have publicly advocated for the Bridging Fund's implementation.

**Accelerated timelines (Recommendation 9):** The Review set a target that 90% of medicines demonstrating superiority should be listed within 6–12 months of ARTG registration — a target that would require halving or better of current average timelines.

**TGA-PBAC parallel processing:** The review noted that TGA and PBAC assessments are largely sequential in practice. Genuine parallel processing — running TGA regulatory and PBAC health technology assessment simultaneously — could reduce the pre-PBAC portion of the gap by up to 95 weeks, the review estimated.

**Implementation status (September 2026):** The Implementation Advisory Group (IAG), established in November 2024, issued an Interim Report in September 2025 identifying access and timelines as a priority area. The IAG's final roadmap was due to the government in January 2026 but has not been publicly released. The Minister for Health announced "immediate steps" on HTA reform at PharmAus 2025, but the 2026–27 federal budget did not include a funded Bridging Fund or a concrete plan to achieve the 6–12 month listing timeline target. Australia's approach to HTA reform remains at the consultation and scoping stage, with no funded implementation pathway in view.

### 7. US Trade Pressure as a Complicating Factor

Report 18 in this series examined the impact of US trade pressure on PBS drug pricing (the MFN pricing dispute and tariff threats). That pressure has not eased. In 2026, US pharmaceutical industry lobbying continues to frame Australia's PBS price negotiations as a market access barrier — arguing that the combination of PBAC cost-effectiveness requirements, aggressive price negotiation, and long listing timelines effectively amounts to trade discrimination against US innovators.

This creates a political tension: HTA reform aimed at faster access (shorter timelines, parallel processing, a Bridging Fund) could be read by industry as evidence that Australia's current system is as restrictive as US negotiators have claimed, potentially strengthening the case for US demands. Conversely, the case that Australia's PBS delivers value for taxpayers depends in part on maintaining a rigorous PBAC assessment and real price negotiation — which is exactly what produces the access gap. The political economy of reform is therefore constrained from both ends.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**What is the access gap actually measuring?** The 726-day figure is calculated from TGA registration to PBS listing date. It includes time spent at PBAC (which may require multiple submissions to address genuine clinical evidence gaps) and time in price negotiation (which may reflect genuine disagreement about value). Compressing this gap requires either accepting medicines at higher prices (budget impact), lowering the evidence bar (risk of listing ineffective medicines), or administrative efficiency improvements. The political debate conflates these three very different sources of delay.

**Bridging Fund moral hazard.** If the Commonwealth funds a Bridging Fund for high-value, high-unmet-need medicines while standard PBS listing negotiations continue, companies have a reduced incentive to agree rapidly to a PBS listing — the government is already paying. Designing Bridging Fund eligibility, duration limits, and PBS listing transition mechanisms to prevent indefinite bridging access is technically and politically difficult.

**State-federal cost shifting.** Hospital formulary listings function as an informal (and accidental) bridging mechanism. States bear the cost of medicines that should ultimately be listed on the PBS. No data-linkage system tracks the total cost of this unquantified cross-subsidisation, but it is material for states like NSW and Victoria that operate comprehensive specialist cancer centre networks.

**Equity of the pre-PBS patchwork.** The patients most able to navigate the access gap are those with well-resourced oncologists, private health insurance, geography near major cancer centres, and literacy to advocate within complex systems. The patients who cannot navigate it — rural, First Nations, low-income, with less specialised care — are systematically less likely to access pre-PBS medicines. The access gap is not just a delay; it is a structural equity failure.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHARMAC and rare disease access — the no-managed-entry model:** New Zealand's PHARMAC explicitly cannot accommodate gene therapies and managed entry agreements within its fixed-budget architecture. The contrast with Australia's growing ATMP pathway — itself built on deed infrastructure — is sharp and illustrative of the trade-offs in different funding models.
2. **Hospital drug and therapeutics committees as patchwork PBS:** State-based hospital formulary committees have become a de facto national pre-PBS subsidy system — covering some of the access gap, inconsistently, with no national coordination, funding or transparency. Understanding this layer is essential to understanding the true cost distribution.
3. **The Medicines Australia Strategic Agreement 2022–2027 and the 2027 renegotiation:** Australia's price negotiation architecture is shaped by a five-year agreement with Medicines Australia governing price disclosure, managed entry, and company obligations. The 2027 renegotiation will be shaped by everything the access gap crisis has revealed.
4. **Parallel TGA-PBAC processing: evidence from existing users:** Some cancer medicines have used parallel TGA-PBAC processing. What has the actual time saving been, and what are the practical barriers to expanding its use?
5. **The private medicines market and out-of-pocket spending on PBS-unlisted medicines:** IQVIA has documented a growing private market in Australia for PBS-unlisted medicines — patients and families paying full cost. Characterising the size, composition, and equity profile of this market would clarify the true scale of the access gap's impact.
