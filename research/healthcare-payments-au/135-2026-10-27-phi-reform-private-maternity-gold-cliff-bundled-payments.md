# PHI Reform for Private Maternity: The Gold Cliff, the Bundled Payment Debate, and Whether Insurance Restructuring Can Reverse Private Maternity's Collapse

**Summary:** Private maternity services in Australia are in structural decline — more than 18 units have closed since 2018, private births have fallen ~22% since 2012, and the largest private hospital operator fell into receivership in May 2025. The federal government's Private Health Sector Reform Consultation Paper 1 (2 July 2026) identified maternity care as one of the two most acute viability pressures in the private health system. This report examines what reform is on the table: expanding maternity coverage beyond Gold-tier PHI products, a contested bundled payment model proposed by Private Healthcare Australia, and the narrower question of newborn and SCN coverage. It finds that the measures are unlikely to reverse the structural forces driving private maternity's decline.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Reports 133 and 134 documented how anaesthetist and paediatrician/neonatologist workforce shortages are the proximate cause of individual private maternity unit closures. This report examines the upstream financing architecture — private health insurance design — that determines whether private maternity remains economically viable at all, and what the government proposes to do about it.

---

## Background: The Structural Crisis in Private Maternity

Private maternity care in Australia requires simultaneous commercial viability across several interlocking markets: an insured patient base willing to pay Gold-tier premiums; hospitals that can recruit three specialist rosters (obstetricians, anaesthetists, and paediatricians) around the clock; and insurer contract rates sufficient to cover fixed costs at declining birth volumes.

Each of these requirements is under pressure simultaneously.

**The birth volume problem.** ABS birth registration data shows a 3% year-on-year decline in births in 2022 versus 2021. Private birth rates have declined further: roughly 22% fewer births in private hospitals in 2024 compared to 2012. Higher socio-economic groups — those most likely to hold Gold PHI — are showing the steepest birth rate declines. When fewer births are spread across the same number of private units, the fixed cost of 24/7 specialist rosters becomes progressively harder to sustain. A unit doing 400 births per year carries nearly the same roster overhead as one doing 1,200, leaving a much smaller revenue surplus to cover operational costs.

**The Gold tier decline.** Australia's four-tier PHI product architecture — Basic, Bronze, Silver, Gold — concentrates maternity coverage exclusively in Gold policies, which currently mandate cover for "Pregnancy and birth" and "Assisted reproductive services." Gold's share of all hospital policyholders has fallen sharply: from approximately 39% in 2020 to ~28–30% by early 2026, driven by premium increases running well ahead of CPI. Bupa's analysis quantifies this as a loss of 360,000 Gold policyholders since the start of COVID-19, against an overall market growth of 640,000 hospital policies. The dynamic is classic adverse selection: people upgrade to Gold when planning a pregnancy and downgrade afterwards, concentrating claims while dispersing premiums.

Finity modelling cited in the DoHDA consultation process found that Gold premiums increased 7.7% in the April 2026 round, against a market average of 4.41% — a spread that reflects Gold's worsening claims experience and accelerates the exodus. As Gold shrinks, the pool of privately insured pregnant women shrinks with it, making maternity services less viable for private hospitals regardless of what else changes.

**Healthscope receivership.** The largest private hospital operator, Healthscope (37 hospitals in every state and territory), entered receivership in late May 2025 with McGrathNicol appointed as receiver over the parent entity. The operating business was not placed in receivership, and hospitals continued to operate. But Healthscope had already announced the closure of maternity services at Darwin Private Hospital (June 2025) and Hobart Private Hospital (August 2025) before the receivership event, citing declining birth volumes, difficulty recruiting paediatricians and obstetricians, and declining antenatal client numbers. As at October 2026, the banks (CBA and Westpac) have provided ~$100M in financing to support operations, and a sale process is underway — but the structural deterioration that forced the maternity closures remains.

**The closure count.** Catholic Health Australia, RANZCOG, and the AMA have each published analyses documenting the acceleration of private maternity unit closures: more than 18 closures since 2018, with at least 13 in the three years to October 2026. The Nous Group's 2026 analysis characterised private maternity as "sending a warning signal."

*(Sources: Healthscope announcement; AMA media release February 2025; RANZCOG news; Bupa PHI structural stress analysis; Finity premium modelling; Nous Group 2026)*

## Key Findings

### 1. The Government's Consultation: What's on the Table

The Department of Health, Disability and Ageing released Consultation Paper 1 on 2 July 2026 as the first in a series of proposed PHI reform tranches. The paper identified maternity care and mental health as the two primary viability pressures requiring structural intervention, following a Financial Health Check of the private hospital system.

The consultation closed 20 August 2026 with no government response published as at October 2026. The relevant product changes are targeted at the 2027 premium round, with industry submissions due November 2026 — meaning any reform will not flow through to PHI products until 2027 at the earliest.

The paper's maternity section identified three reform areas:

1. **Coverage tier expansion:** Whether maternity and birth should be mandated for products below Gold — specifically Silver. The January 2025 predecessor consultation had floated Bronze inclusion; the July 2026 paper focused on Silver.

2. **Bundled payment model:** Whether insurers and government should jointly fund a fixed per-pregnancy payment to a lead clinician coordinating all pregnancy and birth care.

3. **Newborn and SCN coverage:** Whether private health insurance should be required or incentivised to cover specialist and nurse practitioner services for newborns in the first months after birth, beyond the current trigger of "admitted to SCN."

*(Sources: DoHDA Consultation Paper 1, 2 July 2026; NRHA response; PHA submission 13 August 2026)*

### 2. The Silver Expansion Proposal: Industry Opposes on Affordability Grounds

The proposal to require Silver-tier policies to cover pregnancy and birth was the most structurally significant measure in the consultation, and drew the sharpest industry pushback.

**Private Healthcare Australia's position:** PHA's submission (13 August 2026) explicitly opposed expanding maternity coverage to Silver or Bronze, citing its own modelling that found:
- Adding maternity to Silver would increase premiums by approximately **3.5%** for Silver policyholders
- Adding maternity to Bronze would increase premiums by approximately **13.3%** for Bronze policyholders
- Approximately **580,000 Australians** would leave private health cover in response to the premium increases

PHA's core argument is that spreading maternity across tiers addresses affordability only in theory: in practice, the adverse selection dynamic means that people seeking maternity coverage would still upgrade, while non-pregnant people on lower tiers would face premium increases for coverage they do not use.

**Members Health Fund Alliance:** The mutual health funds broadly supported the maternity care reform direction while criticising the consultation paper for providing no government modelling of participation, affordability, or adverse selection impacts. Members Health argued that without modelling, it is impossible to assess whether expanding coverage tiers would actually improve access or simply redistribute costs.

**The NRHA position:** The National Rural Health Alliance supported the maternity reforms in principle but asked for explicit safeguards to ensure rural, regional, and remote Australians benefit equally — noting that PHI coverage in outer regional and remote Australia is significantly below metropolitan rates, and that any reform driven primarily by urban PHI economics may not reach the communities with the worst access deficits.

*(Sources: PHA submission August 2026; Members Health submission August 2026; NRHA response)*

### 3. The Bundled Payment Model: A 30% Saving — or a Safety Risk?

The most contentious proposal in the reform debate is PHA's bundled payment model, which predates the July 2026 consultation paper but is central to it.

**What PHA proposes:** Under the bundled model, a lead clinician — an obstetrician, GP, or midwife — would coordinate all pregnancy and birth care and be responsible for distributing payments to other providers. The woman would pay one fixed price covering all antenatal, birth, and immediate postnatal care rather than receiving separate bills from each provider. PHA's modelling estimates this could reduce the cost of private pregnancy and birth by approximately **30%**. PHA proposes that private insurers and the federal government each contribute a minimum of **$3,000** to the lead clinician for each pregnancy.

The proposal is influenced by the St John of God (Subiaco) "no surprises" model, which has demonstrated that coordinated, pre-agreed pricing with obstetricians can reduce out-of-pocket costs. SJoG's model achieved measurable reduction in OOP costs for participating patients.

**AMA opposition:** The AMA rejects bundled models as "impractical and administratively unworkable." Its January 2026 position statement on key principles for private maternity funding argues that bundled payments:
- Do not accommodate obstetric emergencies or clinical complexity that arise unpredictably
- Create perverse incentives against escalating care when it is clinically warranted
- Cannot accommodate the approximately **17% of low-risk pregnancies** that experience complications requiring unplanned procedures

The AMA prefers reforms targeting: MBS rebate adequacy (specifically addressing what RANZCOG calls "systemic gender bias" in MBS item values for obstetric procedures compared with equivalent male-dominated specialties); improved insurer-hospital contract rates; and direct Commonwealth investment in private hospital viability.

**RANZCOG opposition:** RANZCOG's college news explicitly opposed PHA's bundled model, arguing it would enable midwife- and GP-led births without specialist obstetrician involvement, and that bundling "will not improve affordability." RANZCOG's six reform priorities (developed at its November 2025 Private Practice Roundtable) include: removing gender bias from MBS obstetric rebates and Extended Medicare Safety Net caps; improving PHI affordability for maternity; multidisciplinary team models with appropriate specialist oversight; and sustainable private hospital funding.

**APHA position:** The Australian Private Hospitals Association argues the fundamental problem is that insurer payments to hospitals are too low to cover the fixed cost of maintaining 24/7 specialist rosters at current and projected birth volumes. APHA backs making maternity coverage more accessible but argues that bundled payments shift costs rather than reduce them: "private health reform risks moving costs around rather than cutting them."

*(Sources: PHA submission; AMA position statement January 2026; RANZCOG news; APHA analysis; Insurance Business Australia; UTS/The Conversation August 2025)*

### 4. Newborn and SCN Coverage: The Specific Gap PHA Flagged

The most targeted and practically achievable reform identified in the consultation is the extension of PHI coverage for newborns beyond the current "admitted to SCN" trigger.

**The current architecture:** Under existing PHI rules, a newborn is covered as a private patient — and charges flow to the insurer — only if the baby is admitted to a special care nursery or NICU for treatment. Routine newborn care (observation, uncomplicated jaundice management, feeding support, first-day paediatric assessment) is bundled into the mother's admission and does not generate a separate PHI claim for the baby.

**The consequence for SCN economics:** As documented in Report 134, the SCN is the clinical safety infrastructure that allows a private hospital to operate a maternity unit at all (Level 4 neonatal capability). But the PHI admission trigger is narrow — only sick babies generate SCN revenue — meaning many babies who require low-acuity specialist attention do not trigger PHI billing. This creates a revenue gap for the paediatric roster that backs the SCN.

**PHA's ask:** PHA's August 2026 submission explicitly asked for PHI to be required or incentivised to cover "specialist and nurse practitioner services for newborns in the first three months after birth, regardless of whether care is delivered in hospital or in a community setting." This would:
- Allow private hospitals to bill PHI for paediatric assessment of well newborns before discharge
- Create a revenue stream for community-based newborn follow-up under private paediatric care
- Partially offset the fixed cost of maintaining the paediatric roster

This proposal is relatively narrow in scope but could meaningfully change the economics of SCN provision. The government did not publish a response to this specific ask in the consultation period.

*(Sources: PHA submission August 2026; Report 134 analysis of SCN economics)*

### 5. Government Response and Timeline

As at October 2026, no government response to the consultation has been published. The DoHDA indicated that the July 2026 paper is "Tranche 1" of a multi-tranche reform process, with subsequent papers covering other elements of the private health system.

The immediate policy lever is the **2027 premium round** — the annual process by which insurers apply for premium increases and the government approves (or rejects) them. Any structural change to what products must cover, or how bundled payments are structured, would ideally be legislated or mandated before the November 2026 industry submissions for the 2027 round. This timeline is extremely tight, and industry analysts have noted the government's history of consultation without implementation on private maternity: the Australian Private Hospitals Association stated publicly that the government's own earlier deadline for short-term maternity solutions had "lapsed over a year ago with no action."

*(Sources: DoHDA consultation page; APHA media statement; Insurance Business Australia)*

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Does tier expansion fix adverse selection — or worsen it?** PHA's argument is that adding maternity to Silver will not fix adverse selection because people will still upgrade when pregnant. But the counter-argument — that keeping maternity exclusively in a shrinking Gold tier condemns private maternity to a death spiral as Gold volume falls — is also credible. The honest answer is that without government modelling (which Members Health notes is absent from the consultation paper), neither position can be definitively established.

**Is bundled payment the wrong solution to the right problem?** The right problem is that current MBS rebates and insurer-hospital rates are structurally inadequate for the cost of providing safe private maternity care. Bundled payments could reduce administrative fragmentation and out-of-pocket bills, but they do not increase the total funding envelope. If the aggregate bundle payment is set below the cost of care (including appropriate specialist involvement), the reform shifts who bears the loss, not whether a loss occurs.

**The SCN newborn coverage gap is tractable — but the timeline is unclear.** Of all the proposals, PHA's ask for broader newborn PHI coverage is the narrowest, most technically straightforward, and most directly linked to the SCN economics that are driving closures. It does not require restructuring the tier system or resolving the bundled payment dispute. Its absence from a clear government response is notable.

**Private-to-public transfer is the implicit alternative.** The federal government's response to Darwin and Hobart closures — $10M and $6M respectively for public system expansion — signals that Treasury has implicitly accepted private-to-public birth transfer as manageable. If that is the effective policy, then PHI reform for private maternity is a rearranging exercise, and the question becomes whether the public system can absorb the volume at acceptable quality and without crowding out other care — a question for which no published analysis exists.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The MBS gender bias claim in obstetrics**: RANZCOG's central reform demand is that MBS obstetric item values reflect systemic gender bias — that procedures of equivalent complexity in male-dominated specialties attract higher rebates than obstetric procedures. Examining the evidence for this claim and the history of MBS reviews of obstetric items.
2. **International models for funding neonatal on-call coverage**: How NHS England, Canada, and New Zealand fund neonatal and paediatric on-call cover in birthing units, and whether a Commonwealth direct payment for 24/7 neonatal cover outside the MBS is feasible.
3. **PHI risk equalisation reform**: The consultation paper includes risk equalisation alongside maternity. The mechanism by which risk is shared across insurers, and whether current risk equalisation subsidises products and populations that compound Gold tier's adverse selection problem.
4. **Birthing on Country and First Nations neonatal outcomes**: The intersection of the private maternity collapse with First Nations communities' access to geographically local birth services, and whether the federal Birthing on Country program is funded and designed to fill the resulting gap.
5. **The no-gap / known-gap contract architecture in private maternity**: How insurer contracts with obstetricians and anaesthetists work in practice, and why the gap-payment problem in private maternity (OOP averaging $5,000–$7,000+ per birth) persists despite PHI coverage.
