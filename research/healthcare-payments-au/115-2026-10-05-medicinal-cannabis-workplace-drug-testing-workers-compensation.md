# Medicinal Cannabis at Work: The Drug-Testing Gap, Workers' Compensation Claims, and the Payment-System Collision

**One-line summary:** Australia has legalised medicinal cannabis prescribing through the SAS-B pathway for over 177,000 patients per year, but the workplace testing, employment law, and Workers' Compensation frameworks have not kept pace — creating a three-way collision in which the same patient may have their cannabis funded by a workers' compensation insurer, lose their job for testing positive to it, and face road transport charges for driving while legally prescribed.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 114 documented the $1 billion SAS-B prescription economy for medicinal cannabis. One of the most significant downstream consequences of prescribing at that scale — and the one most directly connected to payment flows — is what happens when those prescriptions intersect with workplace drug testing policies, Workers' Compensation schemes, and employment law.

---

## Background

Australia legalised medicinal cannabis prescription from February 2016. By 2024, the SAS-B pathway was processing 177,762 approvals per year — and the total Australian user base had reached approximately one million. Yet the workplace legal and safety infrastructure was built for a world in which the only people with detectable THC in their system were using cannabis illicitly.

The result is a structural mismatch across three distinct payment-system domains:

1. **Workers' Compensation**: Can injured workers require their insurer to fund medicinal cannabis as "reasonably necessary" treatment?
2. **Employment law**: Can an employer dismiss someone for testing positive to a legally prescribed medicine?
3. **Road transport**: Can a medicinal cannabis patient drive, and who bears the cost of enforcement, lost licences, and associated medical-legal disputes?

None of these questions has been fully resolved in Australian law as of October 2026. The gaps are actively generating litigation, inconsistent outcomes, and payment-system costs.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Testing Architecture: Presence, Not Impairment

The foundational problem is technical: there is no scientifically validated method to test for cannabis impairment at a single point in time.

Australian workplace drug testing operates under two Australian Standards:
- **AS/NZS 4308:2023** (urine testing, applicable to all industries) — laboratories must transition to this updated standard by December 2026
- **AS/NZS 4760:2019** (oral fluid testing, used in safety-critical industries including mining, transport, and construction)

Both standards set **presence-based cut-off thresholds**, not impairment thresholds. For THC:
- Urine: 50 µg/L (screening); 15 µg/L (confirmatory)
- Oral fluid: 15 ng/mL (screening)

Oral fluid testing detects recent use — typically within 12–48 hours — making it a better proxy for recency of use than urine testing, which can detect THC metabolites weeks after consumption. But neither standard links a positive result to a finding of impairment.

**This means**: A patient who takes a CBD-dominant product at night, experiences no next-day cognitive or psychomotor effects, and presents for a morning saliva test in a safety-critical industry will test positive under AS/NZS 4760:2019. A patient who takes a high-THC oral oil daily for chronic pain will produce positive urine results days to weeks after the last dose.

The AS/NZS 4308:2023 update made no changes to THC cut-off levels. The impairment measurement gap has not been addressed in the standard revision.

The Fair Work Commission has noted this problem explicitly. In *Adam Mills v Glamorgan Spring Bay Council* [2025 FWC 116], Deputy President William Clancy stated there is "no current globally accepted definition of impairment or agreement as to how to accurately measure it" for cannabis. This observation — by the tribunal that decides employment dismissals — reflects the state of the field.

### 2. Employment Law: A Contested and Evolving Picture

The Fair Work Commission's 2025 caselaw illustrates the legal uncertainty for both employers and employees.

**Mills v Glamorgan Spring Bay Council [2025 FWC 116]**

Adam Mills was a Works Officer who tested positive for THC after a workplace drug test. He had been prescribed medicinal cannabis for chronic pain through the SAS-B pathway. The council dismissed him for testing positive under its Drug and Alcohol Policy.

Key findings:
- The Commission found a **valid reason** for dismissal: Mills had not disclosed his prescription to his employer as required by the policy.
- However, it found the dismissal was **unfair**: the council failed to consider alternative disciplinary measures available under its own policy before proceeding to dismissal.
- **Reinstatement** was ordered.

The Full Bench **rejected the council's appeal** against reinstatement. The outcome sends a qualified message: non-disclosure of a prescription can constitute a valid reason for disciplinary action, but dismissal without considering alternatives will be found harsh.

**The non-disclosure dilemma**

This creates a structural trap: an employee who discloses their SAS-B prescription to their employer exposes themselves to job loss through policy restrictions or employer fear; an employee who does not disclose exposes themselves to a valid reason for dismissal if they test positive. Neither option is safe for the patient.

Advocates including *Legalise Cannabis Victoria* and the *Greens* senators have called for amendment of the Equal Opportunity Act 2010 and anti-discrimination frameworks to explicitly protect access to prescribed medication. As of October 2026, no federal legislation explicitly prohibits employment discrimination against SAS-B medicinal cannabis patients on the basis of prescription status.

Some employers — particularly in white-collar industries — have updated their Drug and Alcohol policies to distinguish between presence and impairment, and to create notification and accommodation pathways for prescribed medicines. But industries with safety-critical roles under WHS law — mining, construction, transport — face a more difficult regulatory position.

**Work Health and Safety law**: The Model WHS Act (adopted across most jurisdictions) requires employers to ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, that workers are not exposed to health and safety risks. WHS regulators have been consistent: the legal prescription of medicinal cannabis does not change an employer's WHS duty, or an employee's duty not to attend work impaired. WorkSafe WA issued updated guidance in January 2025 explicitly noting this principle.

Victorian WorkSafe guidance on drug and alcohol in the workplace had not been substantively updated in 17 years as at mid-2026 — predating the entire medicinal cannabis legal framework.

### 3. Workers' Compensation: The Insurer Funding Debate

A parallel and financially significant question has emerged in Workers' Compensation jurisdictions: can an injured worker require their insurer to fund medicinal cannabis as a form of treatment for a work-related injury?

In **NSW** — by far the largest state Workers' Compensation scheme — the test under section 60 of the *Workers Compensation Act 1987* is whether treatment is **"reasonably necessary"** as a result of the work injury.

A series of Personal Injury Commission decisions has progressively developed this threshold:

- **Clark v Department of Transport [2020]**: The Commission held the applicant was entitled to have the respondent meet the costs of medicinal cannabis treatment.
- **French v [Employer] [2023]**: The Commission accepted that medicinal cannabis was reasonably necessary where treating specialists identified "substantial benefit and utility" and the patient had achieved functional improvement and reduction in other medications.
- Multiple subsequent decisions established that **"pain relief is a sufficient basis"** for treatment to be reasonably necessary in the absence of evidence of direct harm.

However, the Commission has also found **against** claimants in cases where:
- The cost was disproportionate ($5,000/month was found not "reasonable" in one case)
- No evidence of failure of conventional treatments was presented
- No treating specialist assessment supported the claim

The **practical payment position** in NSW Workers' Compensation as of 2026:
- Medicinal cannabis *can* be covered as reasonably necessary treatment
- It requires a valid SAS-B prescription, documented failure of conventional treatment, specialist support, and demonstrated functional benefit
- Monthly costs of $150–$600 are more likely to be approved than premium products exceeding $1,000/month
- There is no blanket exclusion — nor blanket inclusion — in the scheme

This has generated a legal services market: specialist compensation solicitors now advertise specifically for Workers' Compensation medicinal cannabis claims. At least three NSW law firms have dedicated information pages on the question. The *Lawyers Weekly* noted this as "an active and growing litigation category" in a 2025 analysis.

The **payment flow implications** are significant:
- If a patient's work injury is covered by Workers' Compensation, the scheme potentially bears the $150–$600/month ongoing cannabis cost
- If the patient is then dismissed for testing positive to that same prescribed cannabis, they lose their income — potentially returning to the Workers' Compensation scheme for wage replacement
- The dual cost to insurers (treatment funding + potential wage replacement) has not been comprehensively modelled in any public actuarial analysis

**Interstate variation**: Workers' Compensation is state-based, and outcomes vary. Victoria, Queensland, South Australia, and Western Australia each have separate statutory schemes and their own case-law on medicinal cannabis. There is no national harmonisation on whether or how cannabis treatment is covered.

### 4. The Road Transport Problem and the NSW Reform

The drug-driving legal framework adds a third dimension. Under most Australian state road transport laws, a "prescribed illicit drug" offence is a **per se** offence — a driver commits it by having a detectable level of the drug in their oral fluid, regardless of impairment and regardless of prescription status. THC is a prescribed illicit drug in all jurisdictions.

Prior to 2026, a medicinal cannabis patient in NSW could lawfully take their prescribed medicine, then face a criminal road transport charge — a fine, licence disqualification, and potential job loss — for driving the following morning.

**NSW Reform**: In a significant policy shift, NSW Premier Chris Minns announced legislation to reform this framework specifically for medicinal cannabis patients. Under the proposed legislation:

- Eligible patients would **register with Transport for NSW**, providing evidence of a valid SAS-B prescription
- They must complete an online education program about cannabis and driving safety
- Registered drivers would **not be penalised** for having up to **50 ng/mL of THC** in their saliva at roadside testing (threshold subject to legislative finalisation)
- Drivers would still be removed from the road for **24 hours** after a positive roadside test
- Drivers remain subject to **full penalties** if found to be impaired, regardless of THC level

NSW has an estimated 300,000+ medicinal cannabis users, making this a substantial reform with direct equity implications — particularly for regional and outer-suburban patients for whom driving is essential.

As of September 2026, the legislation was before the NSW Parliament. Queensland responded by doubling drug driving fines — explicitly not providing a medicinal cannabis exemption — reflecting divergent state-level approaches to the same federal SAS-B prescribing framework.

The ACT government responded to an ePetition on drug-driving law reform for medicinal cannabis patients in 2025, acknowledging the problem but not yet legislating a solution.

### 5. The Payment-System Tensions in Summary

The three-way collision creates identifiable payment-system costs that currently fall unevenly:

| Domain | Who bears the cost |
|--------|-------------------|
| Drug test administration | Employer (testing regimes in safety-critical industries) |
| Medicinal cannabis treatment | Patient (out-of-pocket, $150–$600/month) or Workers' Comp insurer (where "reasonably necessary") |
| Employment dispute litigation | Employee (legal costs), employer (FWC legal costs + reinstatement costs) |
| Workers' Comp wage replacement on dismissal | Workers' Comp scheme |
| Drug-driving prosecution | Patient (fines, licence loss, legal fees) |
| Impairment incidents/WorkCover claims | Workers' Comp scheme, employer |

No national framework systematically manages the interaction between these cost streams. A patient with a work injury, a SAS-B cannabis prescription, a workplace drug test, and a drug-driving charge faces simultaneous litigation in the Personal Injury Commission, Fair Work Commission, and a state transport court — each with different standards, no cross-scheme information sharing, and entirely different evidentiary frameworks.

### 6. The Equity Dimension

As noted in Report 114, the medicinal cannabis patient population is concentrated in indications with high rates in lower-SES and regional populations: chronic pain (~42% of SAS-B approvals), anxiety (~33%), and sleep disorders (~14%).

The employment and road-transport consequences of positive drug tests fall disproportionately on:
- **Manual workers** in safety-critical industries — the workers most likely to have work-related injuries and chronic pain conditions, and least likely to work in industries with flexible drug and alcohol policies
- **Truck drivers, mine workers, and construction workers** — for whom loss of a safety-critical licence is job-ending
- **Regional patients** — most dependent on driving for employment and healthcare access, who benefit most from medicinal cannabis telehealth and are most exposed to drug-driving enforcement

White-collar workers in non-safety-critical roles effectively face no enforcement risk from the same prescription. The equity inversion documented at the access end of the medicinal cannabis market (Report 112–114) replicates itself at the consequences end.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Presence vs impairment**: Without a validated impairment test, any reform requires a policy choice about what level of detected THC should be permissible in which circumstances. The NSW road-transport model (50 ng/mL oral fluid with registration) represents one approach; a more comprehensive framework would need to address workplace testing across all industries.

2. **WHS law vs anti-discrimination**: Employers simultaneously have a WHS duty to prevent impairment hazards and a potential anti-discrimination obligation not to dismiss employees for taking prescribed medicine. These obligations are not reconciled in any current federal or state legislative framework.

3. **Workers' Compensation cost modelling**: The willingness of NSW Personal Injury Commission decisions to find medicinal cannabis "reasonably necessary" implies a growing cost exposure for scheme insurers. No public actuarial study has modelled this exposure at scale.

4. **The disclosure trap**: Current law incentivises non-disclosure of SAS-B prescriptions, which in turn creates the conditions for a valid reason for dismissal if a test is positive. A safe-harbour disclosure framework — allowing employees to notify their employer of a prescription without triggering automatic WHS exclusion — does not currently exist nationally.

5. **Legislative fragmentation**: Workers' Compensation, drug-driving law, and employment anti-discrimination are all state-based. With NSW reforming its drug-driving law and Queensland hardening enforcement, the same SAS-B prescription issued by the same TGA-approved pathway will have different legal consequences depending on the state in which the patient works and drives.
