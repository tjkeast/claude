# The NSW Birth Trauma Inquiry and the Maternity Payment Reform Agenda: 43 Recommendations, $83 Million, and a System Still Catching Up

**One-line summary:** Australia's first parliamentary inquiry into birth trauma — the NSW Select Committee's 2024 report — exposed how the fragmented funding architecture of Australian maternity care directly causes birth trauma, and the government's $83 million response is a state-level start on a problem that requires a national payment overhaul.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Yesterday's report on New Zealand's ACC examined how no-fault treatment injury coverage eliminates obstetric liability for NZ practitioners — one structural solution to the private maternity closures crisis documented in Report 128. The NSW Birth Trauma Inquiry is the parallel Australian accountability exercise: a state parliamentary committee that interrogated the clinical, cultural, and payment conditions producing poor birth outcomes in Australia, generating the most concrete reform agenda yet for how maternity care is delivered and funded.

---

## Background

On 29 May 2024, the NSW Legislative Council Select Committee on Birth Trauma tabled its final report — described by international health law scholars as the first parliamentary inquiry into birth trauma anywhere in the world. The committee, chaired by Emma Hurst MLC, had received over 4,000 submissions from women, clinicians, researchers, advocacy organisations, and health services across New South Wales. Public hearings ran from September 2023 through early 2024.

The inquiry had its immediate origins in a cluster of complaints: approximately 30 women had reported traumatic maternity care at Wagga Wagga Base Hospital, triggering an investigation by the NSW Health Care Complaints Commission (HCCC) and, ultimately, the parliamentary inquiry. But the pattern of evidence that emerged was not a single hospital's failure — it was a system-wide picture of fragmented care, understaffed wards, inadequate consent processes, and a maternity workforce in structural crisis.

The report produced 5 formal findings and 43 recommendations. The NSW Government accepted 42 of 43 in its August 2024 response, and the June 2025 state budget allocated $83 million to begin implementation. The Commonwealth, in parallel, expanded Medicare rebates for endorsed midwives from March 2025. Together, these moves represent the largest coordinated response to Australia's maternity care crisis in more than a decade — though advocates argue they fall well short of what the evidence demands.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Problem: Birth Trauma in Australia

The most cited figure from inquiry submissions: approximately **one in three Australian women** experience a traumatic birthing event — roughly **30,000 women in NSW per year** and more than **100,000 nationally** every year.

The Australasian Birth Trauma Association (ABTA), in a 2024 pre-publication self-selected survey, found that 31% of respondents reported thoughts of ending their life as a result of their birth trauma experience. This is a self-selected sample and substantially skews toward women severely affected, so it is not a population prevalence figure — but it indicates the severity of impact at the end of the distribution. Mental health consequences, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), postnatal depression, and birth-related tokophobia (fear of future births), are well-established sequelae.

The most comprehensive economic estimate available comes from a 2024 *Cost of Birth Trauma in Australia* report commissioned by Birth Trauma Australia: an annual cost of **$17.5 billion**, comprising:
- $8.8 billion in lost labour market participation
- $6.5 billion in wellbeing and burden-of-disease impacts
- $1.4 billion in direct health system costs
- $792 million in household financial costs

These figures remain the first systematic economic estimate of birth trauma costs in Australia; they have not been independently verified by AIHW or the Productivity Commission.

### 2. The Inquiry's Architecture: 43 Recommendations Across Seven Domains

The committee's 43 recommendations covered seven broad areas. Not all have equal payment or funding implications, but several directly address how maternity care is paid for.

**A. Continuity of carer (Recommendations 7–11)**

The committee's primary recommendation — designated in its report as the overarching priority — was that all women should have access to **continuity of carer models** with a known provider. "Midwifery Group Practice" (MGP), in which a small team of midwives provides care across antenatal, labour, and postnatal periods, was designated the "gold standard" model, supported by a strong evidence base including randomised trial data showing reduced rates of intervention, improved neonatal outcomes, and substantially better patient experience.

The critical caveat: MGP currently reaches only **11% of maternity models in NSW**. The committee did not recommend *mandating* universal access to midwifery-led continuity specifically (a limitation that generated a formal dissenting statement from Chair Hurst), leaving the door open for GP-led and other continuity models to satisfy the recommendation. That ambiguity matters for payment design: GP continuity of care payments flow through the MBS; midwifery continuity in the public system requires state-funded workforce allocation; privately practising midwife MBS billing requires endorsement, credentialing, and indemnity — a different and more complex architecture.

**B. Workforce and staffing (Recommendations 1–6, 22–25)**

The committee recommended:
- Investment in the midwifery workforce pipeline to address severe understaffing
- **Safe staffing ratios in postnatal wards** — one of the most concrete structural changes sought
- Establishment of a **standalone Chief Midwifery Officer** within NSW Health
- Mandatory debriefing and psychological support protocols for maternity clinicians experiencing traumatic births

These are primarily workforce funding and regulation recommendations — they require state budget allocation and award or contract negotiation, not changes to the MBS or PBS.

**C. Mental health and psychological support (Recommendations 12–18)**

The committee made the report's most direct Medicare intervention: a recommendation to the Commonwealth to **increase the Medicare rebate for psychological support for new parents from 20 to 40 sessions per year** and to reduce gap fees for post-birth psychological care. This directly maps onto the Better Access program (MBS Items 80000–80020, reviewed in Report 011 of this series) and the general critique of the 20-session cap as inadequate for conditions — such as birth-related PTSD — requiring longer-term treatment.

Additional recommendations in this domain:
- Fund postnatal physiotherapy within the six weeks after birth via the public system
- Dedicate maternity ward spaces for parents experiencing pregnancy loss
- Increase funding for psychological support in stillbirth and miscarriage settings

The cost implications of extending Better Access to 40 sessions specifically for new parents are non-trivial. Report 011 estimated the full Better Access program costs approximately $640 million per year; a targeted extension for postnatal PTSD would be a smaller sub-program but would require MSAC and cabinet consideration of clinical need and cost-effectiveness.

**D. Consent and legal accountability (Recommendations 26–32)**

The committee recommended:
- Funding for mandatory informed consent training for maternity practitioners
- Review of NSW Health consent policies
- **Research into incorporating "birth trauma" and "obstetric violence" into NSW law**

The most controversial area: the committee stopped short of recommending specific legislative change to allow women to seek legal redress where informed consent was not obtained — a recommendation that Chair Hurst argued in her dissent was the "most fundamental omission" of the report. The government accepted this more cautious position, declining to recommend specific legislation.

**E–G. Antenatal education, research, and culturally safe care (Recommendations 33–43)**

Remaining recommendations covered: comprehensive antenatal education delivered as a health system responsibility; dedicated government funding for birth trauma research; and trauma-informed, culturally safe care models for First Nations women and women from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds.

### 3. The NSW Government Response: Accepted, But Underfunded

The NSW Government accepted 42 of 43 recommendations in its August 2024 response, with Health Minister Ryan Park offering a public apology to women who had not received the care standard they were entitled to. The rejected recommendation (Recommendation 43) concerned the committee chair's role — a procedural matter.

The government identified five "accelerated initiatives" for delivery within 12 months:
1. Increasing access to maternity continuity of care models
2. Embedding trauma-informed maternity care across NSW Health services
3. Improving information provision to pregnant women
4. Improving consent processes
5. Supporting women who experience pregnancy complications

Critics — notably local independent MP Dr Joe McGirr, and the Australian College of Midwives — noted that these five initiatives lacked funding commitments and timetables, and that the government had not adopted the committee's recommendation for universal access to a *named* care provider. Rural and regional areas, where the Wagga Wagga case originated, faced the sharpest gap between aspiration and capacity.

### 4. The $83 Million NSW Budget 2025–26: What Was Actually Funded

The June 2025 NSW Budget provided the first concrete investment. The $83 million package included:

- **$44.8 million** for expanding Midwifery Group Practice, funding **53 additional FTE midwife roles** in regional and remote NSW — the single largest expansion of MGP workforce in the state's history
- **$26.8 million** to maintain seven family care centres, five mobile maternity services, and one residential perinatal unit in regional and rural NSW
- **$5.7 million** for free whooping cough and influenza vaccination for pregnant women
- Remainder: trauma-informed care training and enhanced antenatal education

Implementation milestones by October 2026:
- **Chief Midwifery Officer** role established August 2025; **Donna Garland** (former Director, Royal Hospital for Women, Randwick) appointed inaugural NSW Chief Midwife in March 2026
- NSW midwifery workforce has grown **10% since June 2023**, reaching 3,400+ employed midwives statewide
- **Safe staffing ratios** in postnatal wards began rolling out from March 2026

The $83 million over four years is substantial for a state-level maternity investment. But it covers only public hospital services. The private maternity sector — where 35–40% of Australian births occur, and where the medical indemnity–driven closures documented in Report 128 are occurring — is not directly addressed by this package.

### 5. The Commonwealth Response: MBS Items, Not Structural Reform

The Commonwealth's parallel response has been incremental rather than transformative. The 2024–25 Federal Budget included **$56.5 million** in new and upgraded MBS items for endorsed midwives, taking effect **1 March 2025**:

- Extended postnatal care to **8 weeks post-birth** (from 6 weeks)
- New item: **90-minute long antenatal appointment** for complex cases
- New item: final postnatal appointment including mental health screening, domestic violence screening, and birth debrief
- New item: complex antenatal care management leading to hospital admission
- Homebirth: 100% MBS coverage for privately practising midwives providing low-risk homebirth and intrapartum care outside hospital

These items were drawn from the 2020 Participating Midwife Reference Group recommendations, accelerated in the context of the birth trauma debate. There are currently **1,355 endorsed midwives** eligible to bill these items nationally — a workforce of 1,355 providing care for approximately 310,000 annual Australian births represents extraordinarily limited reach.

The structural barrier to midwifery continuity of care at scale — identified in the 2020 Reference Group and not yet resolved — is the **MBS item architecture itself**: current items are episode-based, not bundled, making them poorly suited to the continuous relationship model that MGP requires. The Reference Group's recommendation for a **bundled payment model** — in which a midwife or team is paid a capitated amount to provide all antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care — has not been adopted. Endorsed midwives remain largely unable to obtain hospital credentialing for intrapartum care, meaning their MBS billing is confined to antenatal and postnatal consultations, not the full continuity model.

### 6. The Private Maternity Sector: Unaddressed by the Inquiry

The NSW inquiry's remit was public hospital maternity care. It did not directly address:
- Medical indemnity premium costs ($65,000–$100,000+/year for full-scope obstetricians)
- The closure of 18 private maternity units nationally since 2018 (Avant data)
- The declining pool of PHI-covered maternity patients due to policy downgrades
- The APRA-observed hospital and medical inflation rate of 6.7% in 2024

These dynamics — explored in Reports 128 and 129 — create a private maternity sector under acute pressure. The AMA (Queensland) noted in April 2025 the case of Gladstone, where closure of the private maternity unit triggered closure of the public service as well — a cascading failure that underlines how the private-public maternity boundary is not as clean as policy design assumes.

No Commonwealth review has yet integrated the birth trauma findings with the medical indemnity reform agenda. The 2019 national *Woman-Centred Care: Strategic Directions for Australian Maternity Services* remains the operative federal framework — advocates argue it is wholly inadequate to the current crisis.

### 7. The National Picture: NSW as Prototype, Australia as Laggard

The national response to the NSW inquiry has been piecemeal. Key developments as at October 2026:
- **ANMF** has called on all states and territories to adopt the 43 recommendations — no other state has tabled an equivalent inquiry
- **RANZCOG** convened its first national birth trauma roundtable in **July 2026**, calling for better postnatal care, lower cost barriers, and recognition of long-term effects including pelvic floor dysfunction and sexual health impacts
- **Birth Trauma Australia** submitted to the 2025 federal budget a proposal for a national birth injury care and treatment plan — no commitment has been made
- The Commonwealth has not commissioned an equivalent national inquiry into birth trauma, leaving the 2024 NSW report as the de facto national reference document for reform

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Is the MBS structurally capable of funding continuity of care?** The 2020 Participating Midwife Reference Group concluded it is not, without a bundled payment model. The episode-based MBS suits episodic care; midwifery continuity is relational. Without this architectural change, expanding the MBS item count may simply expand rebates for the existing fragmented model rather than incentivising continuity.

2. **Does the 43-recommendation acceptance mean anything in rural NSW?** Workforce shortages in regional and remote NSW mean that even fully funded continuity models cannot be delivered without practitioners. Recruiting 53 FTE midwives to regional areas addresses a fraction of the gap; the structural barriers (wages, rural incentives, scope of practice, housing) are not solved by the inquiry's recommendations.

3. **Who governs the private maternity sector?** The NSW inquiry addressed public hospital care. The private maternity crisis — indemnity costs, unit closures, PHI policy downgrades — is a Commonwealth-level problem sitting at the intersection of APRA regulation, MBS design, private health insurance legislation, and medical indemnity architecture. No Commonwealth inquiry has yet been established.

4. **What happens to the 40-session MBS recommendation?** The committee's recommendation to extend Better Access from 20 to 40 sessions for new parents was made to the Commonwealth, not NSW. As of October 2026 the Commonwealth has not responded. Given the experience of the 2021–2022 Better Access expansion and subsequent COVID-era retreat (Report 011), resistance to session cap expansion is predictable. The birth-specific framing — extending only for postnatal PTSD — may provide political viability that a general extension did not.

5. **Is the $17.5 billion cost estimate robust enough to drive federal investment?** The Birth Trauma Australia economic study is a first-in-kind analysis, but it has not been independently verified. Without AIHW or Productivity Commission-validated estimates, the cost of inaction remains contested, making sustained investment politically harder.
