# Interval Cancers in the NBCSP: The Quality Failure Beneath the Capacity Crisis

**Report 082 — 2026-08-27**

**Summary:** Australia's National Bowel Cancer Screening Program generates an interval cancer rate of 6.3 bowel cancers per 10,000 people who receive a negative iFOBT — cancers that emerge precisely because the screening system failed to catch them. A 2026 *Medical Journal of Australia* study of Western Australian participants found that 49 of 51 interval cancers were diagnosed at stage II or higher, carrying all-cause mortality of 41 per 1,000 person-years. The quality metrics most capable of explaining why — endoscopist adenoma detection rates and cancer stage at diagnosis — are not reported in the NBCSP's national monitoring framework due to incomplete histopathology data. Australia knows it has an interval cancer problem; it cannot yet systematically identify who is causing it.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 081 documented that the Australian nurse endoscopist debate is fundamentally a workforce capacity argument — can the system perform enough colonoscopies? This report examines the quality dimension that debate sidesteps: not merely whether colonoscopies are performed, but whether they find the cancers and pre-cancers they are supposed to find. Interval cancers are the measure of that failure.

---

## Background: Defining the Interval Cancer Problem

In the context of the NBCSP, an **interval cancer** is a bowel cancer diagnosed after a participant receives a negative iFOBT result and before their next scheduled biennial screen or within 24 months — whichever comes first. The NBCSP uses this as Performance Indicator 7 (PI 7).

There are two distinct pathways through which an interval cancer can arise:

1. **iFOBT false negative**: The tumour or polyp was present at the time of screening but did not bleed sufficiently, or consistently enough, to trigger a positive result. iFOBT sensitivity for detecting pre-cancerous advanced adenomas ranges from 16% to 64% in the literature, and is substantially lower for right-sided, sessile, and flat lesions that may not bleed.

2. **Post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer (PCCRC)**: The participant had a positive iFOBT and underwent colonoscopy — but the cancer or precursor lesion was missed or incompletely removed during the procedure. This is a direct quality failure attributable to the colonoscopy itself.

Both mechanisms are at work in the NBCSP, but the program's monitoring framework is not designed to distinguish them reliably. PI 7 counts the interval cancer rate from negative iFOBT — capturing pathway one directly. It does not separately report post-colonoscopy interval cancers in iFOBT-positive participants who then received (imperfect) colonoscopy, which is where endoscopist performance is most directly implicated.

## Key Findings

### 1. The National Interval Cancer Rate: 6.3 per 10,000

The AIHW's *National Bowel Cancer Screening Program Monitoring Report 2026* reports Australia's interval cancer rate at **6.3 bowel cancers per 10,000 participants** who had a negative screen in 2018, based on matched national cancer diagnosis data followed to 31 December 2020.

This figure carries significant caveats. Matched national cancer diagnosis data are not available for those who screened after 2019, meaning the 2026 report's headline interval cancer rate still rests on 2018 screening cohort data — now eight years old. The program has grown substantially since then, and updated figures using more recent screening cohorts are not yet reportable.

Geographic variation is marked:

| Jurisdiction | Interval Cancer Rate (per 10,000) |
|---|---|
| Northern Territory | 8.4 |
| Queensland | ~6.8 |
| NSW/ACT | ~6.5 |
| Victoria | ~6.0 |
| South Australia | ~5.8 |
| WA | ~5.5 |
| Tasmania | 2.8 |

The NT's rate of 8.4 per 10,000 — nearly three times Tasmania's — almost certainly reflects a combination of the program's participation patterns, the age and health profile of participants in each jurisdiction, remoteness-related delays in colonoscopy follow-up after positive iFOBTs, and First Nations population characteristics, but the monitoring data cannot decompose these causes.

### 2. The WA 2026 Study: Who Gets Interval Cancer and What Happens to Them

The most granular Australian evidence on interval cancer characteristics comes from a retrospective observational cohort study published in the *Medical Journal of Australia* in early 2026 (Smith et al., MJA 2026; 224:e70138). The study examined WA NBCSP participants aged 50–74 who had a negative iFOBT during the 2018 screening round, following them for interval CRC diagnoses to 31 December 2020 and for mortality to 30 September 2022.

The study identified **51 interval colorectal cancers** among WA 2018 negative screeners.

**Key findings:**

- **Only 13 of 51 interval cancers (25%) were stage I** at diagnosis — the stage at which curative treatment success rates are highest. The remaining 38 (75%) were stage II or higher.

- **25 of 51 (49%) were right-sided** (proximal colon). Right-sided cancers are disproportionately over-represented in interval cancer cohorts internationally because: (a) iFOBT has lower sensitivity for right-sided lesions, which tend to be flatter and less haemorrhagic; and (b) sessile serrated lesions — the precursor most commonly missed at colonoscopy — are predominantly proximal.

- **19 of 51 (37%) were diagnosed in the 19–24 month window** after the negative iFOBT — late in the 2-year inter-screening interval. This suggests some tumours were either fast-growing or present at screening but generating insufficient blood to trigger the iFOBT threshold.

- **34 of 51 (67%) were adenocarcinomas** (the remainder were mucinous or other histological subtypes).

- **Men had a substantially higher adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR) than women**: aIRR 5 (95% CI 3–11). This is consistent with the biological observation that men tend to have higher polyp burden and faster adenoma-to-carcinoma progression, as well as men's lower NBCSP participation rates meaning those who do participate may have higher baseline risk.

- **Age elevated risk**: People aged 70–74 had an aIRR of 3 (95% CI 1–6) compared with those aged 50–59, consistent with accumulating adenoma burden over time.

**Mortality outcomes were severe:**

- All-cause mortality rate: **41 per 1,000 person-years** (95% CI 18–92) over a median follow-up of 33 months
- CRC-specific mortality: **35 per 1,000 person-years** (95% CI 14–83)

These are the mortality signatures of late-stage cancer diagnoses in an older cohort. For comparison, the five-year survival rate for stage I CRC is approximately 90%; for stage IV it is around 15%. Catching these cancers at stage I rather than stage III–IV would transform survival outcomes at the individual level.

### 3. The Surveillance Framework Cannot See What Matters Most

Australia's NBCSP tracks interval cancer rates (PI 7) nationally. It does not — and currently cannot — routinely report three performance indicators that would most directly explain why interval cancers occur:

- **PI 5a: Adenoma Detection Rate (ADR)** — the proportion of colonoscopies in which at least one adenoma is found. Not reported in 2025 or 2026 monitoring reports due to incomplete histopathology data return to the National Cancer Screening Register (NCSR).

- **PI 5b: Positive Predictive Value of diagnostic assessment for detecting adenoma** — similarly unreported.

- **PI 8: Cancer clinico-pathological stage distribution** — the staging breakdown of screen-detected cancers. Not reported due to incomplete histopathology data return.

The structural problem is straightforward: when a colonoscopy is performed under the NBCSP, the endoscopist is required to submit colonoscopy outcome data (including histopathology results for any removed polyps) to the NCSR. But this requirement is **not mandated** under a penalty or withholding mechanism — it relies on compliance. Reporting rates are incomplete, and the histopathology link between what was removed and what was found is broken in enough cases to make population-level ADR reporting unreliable.

This is not a minor data quality issue. The ADR is the single most validated quality predictor of interval cancer risk in colonoscopy. Evidence published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (Kaminski et al., 2010) and in large European cohort studies established that **patients undergoing colonoscopy by endoscopists in the highest ADR quartile (34–52%) face a four-fold lower interval cancer risk** compared with patients of endoscopists in the lowest ADR quartile (12–21%). The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy sets a minimum ADR standard of 25% for colonoscopy programmes.

Australia has the same 25% ADR standard embedded in CCRTGE credentialling and the Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard. But without population-level ADR reporting through the NBCSP, there is no way to identify which individual endoscopists or health service organisations are routinely falling below standard — and therefore no mechanism for systematically preventing the interval cancers those endoscopists are generating.

### 4. The Sessile Serrated Lesion Problem: A Whole New Quality Frontier

The colonoscopy quality framework has historically centred on ADR. But international evidence increasingly identifies **sessile serrated lesions (SSLs)** — also called sessile serrated adenomas (SSAs) — as a distinct and under-recognised precursor pathway to interval cancer, particularly in the proximal colon.

SSLs are characterised by:
- Flat, sessile morphology with indistinct borders
- A mucus cap that makes them easy to overlook
- Predominantly right-sided (proximal colon) location
- A serrated pathway to malignancy that can progress faster than the conventional adenoma-carcinoma sequence

The proximal serrated polyp detection rate (PSPDR) is now recognised as an independent quality indicator that is strongly inversely associated with PCCRC risk, independent of ADR. A 2025 review (*Colonoscopy Quality Indicators in Transition*, published in *Diagnostics*) documented that ADR alone is increasingly insufficient as a quality measure — particularly for interval cancers arising from the serrated pathway.

The WA 2026 study's finding that 49% of interval cancers were right-sided is consistent with the SSL hypothesis: right-sided, flat, sessile precursors were missed at colonoscopy (or insufficient iFOBT bleeding made a preceding positive test unlikely), progressed to cancer, and were diagnosed in the 2-year window.

The ACSQHC's 2020 revision of the Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard added a fourth indicator for sessile serrated adenoma detection — the first formal acknowledgement at an Australian policy level that ADR alone was not sufficient. The **September 2025 update to the Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard**, launched at the Australian Gastroenterology Week conference in Melbourne, built further on this quality framework. It emphasises nine quality statements, mandatory sharing of colonoscopy reports with patients, GPs, and the NCSR, and aims to strengthen the information loop that currently breaks down when histopathology results are not returned.

### 5. International Comparison: Is 6.3 per 10,000 Good or Bad?

The Dutch FIT-based colorectal cancer screening programme — a useful comparator given its similar biennial FIT methodology — reports interval CRC incidence rates of **10.4 per 10,000 participants** after the first screening round and **9.6 per 10,000** after the second round.

Australia's 6.3 per 10,000 appears better on the surface. But direct comparison is complicated by:
- Different data linkage methodologies and cancer registry completeness
- The Dutch programme includes follow-up colonoscopy within its linked data, while the Australian figure is based on iFOBT-negative participants only
- Australia's 2018 screening cohort data may not represent current program performance given the NBCSP's substantial expansion since 2018

The UK's NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme, which historically used guaiac-FOB testing (less sensitive than FIT), reported 749 interval colorectal cancers registered within 2 years among 628,976 women screened between 2006 and 2012 — approximately 11.9 per 10,000 women. The transition to FIT across most BCSP programmes has generally been associated with reduced interval cancer rates due to higher sensitivity.

Whether Australia's rate is genuinely among the lowest in comparable FIT-based programmes, or merely appears so due to less complete cancer registry linkage, cannot be definitively established from current data.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The ADR Accountability Gap Is the Structural Problem, Not the Rate Itself

Reporting that Australia's interval cancer rate is 6.3 per 10,000 tells the program nothing about what is causing it. Without mandatory, linked, endoscopist-level ADR and PSPDR reporting through the NCSR, the program cannot identify poor performers, cannot target quality improvement interventions, and cannot demonstrate whether improvements in quality are driving reductions in interval cancer rates over time. The 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard strengthens reporting expectations — but the histopathology data return compliance problem predates the standard and has not been resolved by previous iterations.

### Tension 2: Capacity vs Quality Tradeoffs in a Stretched System

The colonoscopy debate (Reports 080–081) focuses on how to perform more colonoscopies faster. But evidence from quality improvement programmes internationally shows that throughput pressure is associated with shorter withdrawal times, lower ADR, and more missed lesions. If Australia expands colonoscopy capacity by reducing per-procedure time, relaxing quality standards, or deploying less experienced operators, it risks increasing interval cancer rates at the same time as it reduces wait times. These goals can be in tension.

### Tension 3: The iFOBT Itself Has Inherent Sensitivity Limits

Some interval cancers are not caused by colonoscopy failures — they arise because the iFOBT did not detect sufficient haemoglobin in the stool to trigger a positive result. For right-sided, sessile lesions in particular, the iFOBT may simply not be sensitive enough to catch precursors before they progress. This is an argument for alternative or adjunct screening modalities (stool DNA testing, CT colonography, or earlier colonoscopy for high-risk individuals) rather than exclusively a quality improvement argument. But Australia has not yet made a policy determination about whether to supplement the iFOBT-only NBCSP with additional modalities, even for high-risk subgroups.

### Tension 4: The Stage Data Gap Compounds the Problem

PI 8 — cancer clinico-pathological stage distribution — is also unreported in the 2025 and 2026 NBCSP monitoring reports. This means Australia cannot systematically compare the stage profile of screen-detected cancers (where early detection should produce a stage shift towards stage I–II) against interval cancers. Without this comparison, the program cannot measure the degree of harm caused by interval cancers relative to the benefit of screen detection. The WA 2026 study provides a partial answer for one state in one year — but it is not a national, ongoing measurement.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Stool DNA testing and multi-target approaches: should Australia's NBCSP add a second screening modality?** The iFOBT has inherent sensitivity limits, particularly for right-sided and serrated lesions. Stool DNA testing (e.g., Cologuard in the US) and combined FIT/DNA approaches detect significantly more proximal lesions but generate more false positives and cost more. The case for a high-risk supplemental pathway in Australia.

2. **CT colonography as a colonoscopy alternative: the MBS funding gap and the colon imaging debate.** CT colonography (virtual colonoscopy) detects lesions ≥6mm with sensitivity approaching optical colonoscopy, can be performed without sedation, and could absorb some of the colonoscopy capacity shortfall. But its MBS funding is limited to specific indications — it is not funded for routine NBCSP follow-up. A funding reform analysis.

3. **The NCSR data governance problem: why Australia can't report ADR nationally.** The National Cancer Screening Register is the central infrastructure for NBCSP quality surveillance. Understanding why histopathology data return rates are insufficient — whether legal, technical, financial, or governance barriers — and what reform would be needed to enable endoscopist-level quality reporting.

4. **First Nations bowel cancer screening: interval cancer and colonoscopy quality in remote settings.** The NT has the highest interval cancer rate in Australia (8.4 per 10,000). First Nations Australians face lower NBCSP participation and lower colonoscopy follow-up rates despite higher CRC risk in some settings. The equity dimension of interval cancer is the least-examined aspect of the quality debate.

5. **The economics of colonoscopy quality improvement: what does reducing the ADR gap actually cost?** Quality improvement programmes — split-dose bowel preparation protocols, endoscopist feedback and education, water-exchange techniques — have demonstrated ADR improvements of 5–10 percentage points in international trials. The cost of implementing these at a system level, and the downstream savings from reduced interval cancer treatment costs, have not been modelled for Australia.
