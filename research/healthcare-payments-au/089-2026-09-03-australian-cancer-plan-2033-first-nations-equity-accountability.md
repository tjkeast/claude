# The Australian Cancer Plan 2023–2033: Strategic Ambition, Accountability Gaps, and the First Nations Equity Test

**Report 089 — 2026-09-03**

**Summary:** Australia launched its first-ever national cancer plan in November 2023 — a decade-long framework backed by over $500 million in budget commitments and a clear strategic objective of achieving equity for First Nations Australians, who are 14% more likely to be diagnosed with cancer and 45% more likely to die from it. The plan is comprehensive in scope and genuine in its equity focus. But its accountability architecture — built on shared responsibility, voluntary participation, non-binding partnerships, and sector "buy-in" — stands in striking contrast to the explicit, funded, and time-limited elimination target that has defined Australia's more successful response to cervical cancer. Whether the Plan's 2-year and 5-year evaluations will drive the accountability its First Nations equity objective requires is the defining question of the next 18 months.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 088 showed that Australia's cervical screening equity response — the National Cervical Cancer Elimination Strategy with its explicit 2035 target, $48.2 million dedicated investment, and community-driven self-collection redesign — has materially improved First Nations participation, yet still projects elimination 12 years behind the national target. The Australian Cancer Plan is the overarching framework within which both cervical and bowel cancer screening equity sit — and its accountability model is far less specific than the cervical elimination strategy.

---

## Background

Australia has one of the world's highest cancer incidence rates by absolute numbers. In 2025, approximately 175,000 Australians will be diagnosed with cancer and around 50,000 will die from it. Yet despite cancer being the leading cause of death nationally, Australia did not have a comprehensive national cancer plan until November 2023 — when Cancer Australia launched the **Australian Cancer Plan 2023–2033**.

The plan is significant in several respects. It is Australia's first attempt to coordinate the full cancer control system — prevention, screening, treatment, survivorship, palliative care, research, and workforce — under a single strategic framework. It explicitly identifies equity as a central organising principle. And it is accompanied by the largest single investment in First Nations cancer equity the Australian Government has made: $238.5 million announced in the May 2023 Budget.

Understanding what this plan commits to, how it will be held to account, and whether its equity architecture is adequate requires engaging seriously with both its design and its limitations.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Disease Burden: First Nations Cancer Mortality Is an Avoidable Crisis

The gap in cancer outcomes between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians and the non-Indigenous population is large, persistent, and to a significant degree avoidable.

The AIHW's July 2026 release — the most comprehensive national picture of First Nations cancer outcomes yet published — documents:

- **Incidence rate (2025 estimate):** 315 cases per 100,000 First Nations people, down from 342 in 2011 — a decline of ~8%
- **Mortality rate (2025 estimate):** 105 deaths per 100,000 First Nations people, down from 148 in 2011 — a decline of ~29%
- **Overall scale (2025):** approximately 3,600 cancer cases and 1,200 cancer deaths among First Nations people per year
- **Relative risk:** First Nations people remain 14% more likely to be diagnosed with cancer, and 45% more likely to die from it, than non-Indigenous Australians

These improvements are real — the mortality decline is larger than for the non-Indigenous population, suggesting some gap closure. But they are not sufficient. Cancer and neoplasms account for **18% of the total gap in death rates** between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Australians, making cancer the second leading contributor to that gap.

A landmark analysis published in *BMC Cancer* (PMC 2022) quantified the preventable dimension: approximately **21% of all First Nations cancer deaths within 5 years of diagnosis** are potentially avoidable — they would not have occurred if First Nations cancer survival equalled non-Indigenous survival. This translates to approximately 133 deaths per average annual cohort, or 947 potentially avoidable deaths across the 2005–2016 study period. These are not deaths from rare or untreatable cancers: they span breast, bowel, lung, and cervical cancer — all of which have established treatment pathways and, in several cases, effective screening programs.

The reasons are structural: late-stage diagnosis (because of lower participation in screening programs and less access to primary care where symptoms might be detected earlier), reduced access to treatment (especially for those in rural and very remote communities), and lower rates of culturally safe care.

### 2. The Australian Cancer Plan: What It Says

The Australian Cancer Plan was developed by Cancer Australia through an extensive consultation process: more than 700 submissions, over 400 organisations, more than 300 individuals, and extensive engagement with First Nations communities, ACCHOs, clinicians, researchers, and peak bodies. The plan runs to 2033 with a tiered goal structure:

- **10-year Ambition Statements:** aspirational vision for each strategic objective
- **5-year Goals:** measurable outcomes the sector aims to reach by 2028
- **2-year Goals:** near-term milestones to track early implementation (due 2025–26)

The plan has six strategic objectives:

1. Achieving equity in cancer outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people
2. Achieving equity in cancer outcomes for all people
3. Optimal and equitable access to person-centred cancer care
4. Strong and dynamic cancer research, data, and evidence
5. Skilled, compassionate, and sustainable cancer workforce
6. Strong and dynamic foundations

**Strategic Objective 1 — First Nations equity** — is identified as the plan's primary equity priority. The ambition statement describes a future where "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people have equitable cancer outcomes and experiences of care, supported by self-determination, culturally safe services, and Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations."

The 2-year goal (by late 2025) calls for: increased access to culturally safe cancer services; expanded ACCHO-delivered cancer prevention and early detection; and initial investment in First Nations cancer research capacity. The 5-year goal (by 2028) is more ambitious: measurable reduction in the cancer incidence and mortality gap; increased ACCHO capacity to deliver care across the cancer journey; and a growing body of First Nations-led research evidence.

Notably, the Plan does not set a specific quantified target — no stated year by which the mortality gap will be eliminated or halved, no numerical threshold for ACCHO-delivered care uptake, no minimum participation target for First Nations cancer screening. The strategic objective is directional; the goals are process-oriented.

### 3. Companion Document: The NACCHO Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Cancer Plan

Launched at the NACCHO Members' Conference in October 2023 — one month before the national plan — the **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Cancer Plan** is a companion document developed by and for the ACCHO sector. It represents the First Nations-controlled health sector's own strategic response to the cancer equity crisis, designed to complement but not be dependent on the national plan.

NACCHO's plan reflects ACCHO sector consultations and centres self-determination, calling for:

- **Sustainable funding:** long-term, flexible funding for ACCHO-delivered cancer services beyond project-based grants
- **Accessible services:** primary prevention, screening, and early detection delivered through ACCHOs and community-controlled services
- **Culturally safe mainstream cancer centres:** structural reform of metropolitan and regional cancer centres so that First Nations people can safely access them when specialist care is required
- **Wrap-around support:** transport, accommodation, interpreter services, and social support for First Nations cancer patients navigating the mainstream system

The NACCHO plan is more explicit than the national plan about what structural change looks like. But it faces the same funding constraint: the $238.5 million announced in the 2023–24 Budget — while substantial — is distributed across a decade, serving a very large sector with deep structural needs.

### 4. The $238.5 Million Investment: Adequate or Insufficient?

The **Improving First Nations Cancer Outcomes** budget measure announced in May 2023 allocates $238.5 million over the life of the Cancer Plan, with funding channelled through:

- **Cancer Australia:** $38.6 million for culturally safe mainstream services and First Nations cancer research capacity building. This includes the Partnerships for Cancer Research program, which funds First Nations-led and co-designed research.
- **NACCHO:** Investment to improve access to cancer services for First Nations women (linking to the cervical cancer elimination strategy as well as broader cancer care)
- **ACCN grant programs:** including the 2025 Networking for Cancer Equity Grant Program, which funds connections between Comprehensive Cancer Centres and ACCHOs, regional hospitals, and community health services

At $238.5 million over ten years, this represents approximately $23.85 million per year — or roughly $6.60 per Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander person per year (in a population of approximately 3.6 million). For context, the National Cervical Screening Program's dedicated Indigenous equity investment ($12.7 million over four years) is smaller in nominal terms but explicitly tied to a time-limited, measurable elimination target.

Whether $238.5 million over a decade is sufficient to close a mortality gap that currently kills 1,200 First Nations Australians per year is a question the plan does not directly address. The AIHW's avoidable deaths analysis suggests the cost of inaction — in lives lost — is already very high.

### 5. The Accountability Architecture: Where the Weakness Lies

The Australian Cancer Plan's implementation model rests on what Cancer Australia calls "shared responsibility" — a coordinated national effort by governments, NGOs, the health sector, and researchers, governed by:

- **Non-binding partnership agreements:** Cancer Australia signs non-financial, non-binding partnership agreements with interested stakeholders who commit to implementing actions and reporting progress. There is no regulatory consequence for non-participation.
- **Voluntary monitoring and reporting:** Cancer Australia will collect information on activities being implemented across the sector, but reporting is voluntary and depends on sector engagement.
- **2-year and 5-year evaluations:** Cancer Australia will lead formal evaluations at these timepoints, the first of which is due in late 2025.

This model contrasts with the accountability mechanisms in Australia's more successful cancer control programs:

| Program | Target | Accountability mechanism | Consequence for non-achievement |
|---------|--------|--------------------------|----------------------------------|
| NCSP cervical cancer elimination | Elimination by 2035 | National Strategy + monitoring report + annual AIHW data | Public reporting; national strategy triggers action |
| Australian Cancer Plan (First Nations) | Equitable cancer outcomes by 2033 | Shared responsibility; voluntary reporting | No formal consequence; 2-year review |
| NBCSP colonoscopy follow-up | 90% of positive screens followed up (target not formally set) | NCSR data (voluntary histopathology return) | No formal consequence |

The parallel with the NBCSP accountability gap (documented in Report 083) is striking. The cancer data governance gap — where voluntary reporting means the system cannot measure what it is trying to change — appears in multiple places across Australia's cancer control architecture.

The ACP's monitoring and evaluation framework was published in 2024. It describes a logic model, a set of indicators, and a data collection approach. What it does not contain is a mechanism for escalation — for what happens when the 2-year review finds that early milestones are not being met.

### 6. The ACCN: A Structural Innovation with Equity Promise and Voluntary Participation Risk

The **Australian Comprehensive Cancer Network (ACCN)** — launched in May 2024 — is the plan's flagship structural reform. It links Comprehensive Cancer Centres in major cities (e.g. Peter MacCallum, Chris O'Brien Lifehouse, Princess Alexandra) with regional hospitals, community health services, and Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services through:

- A set of standards focusing on comprehensive cancer care, equitable access, research excellence, data collection, workforce development, and network connectivity
- Virtual connectivity to extend specialist expertise across geographic distances
- The 2025 Networking for Cancer Equity Grant Program to fund specific connections

The ACCN's equity logic is sound: if First Nations patients and patients in rural/remote areas can access the expertise of metropolitan cancer centres without relocating, the geographic distance that currently drives late-stage diagnosis and lower survival rates can be partially offset.

But the ACCN operates on **voluntary participation** — cancer services "commit" to its standards rather than being required to meet them. In a health system where metropolitan comprehensive cancer centres already have high patient demand, the incentive to extend capacity to rural and remote networks is not self-evident. The grant program addresses this partly, but grant funding is time-limited; structural integration is not.

### 7. Progress Data: Some Encouraging Signs, But Structural Gaps Remain

The AIHW's July 2026 data release — described as "the most detailed national picture yet" of First Nations cancer outcomes — provides the first clear mid-implementation signal:

- **Incidence decline:** 342 → 315/100,000 (2011–2025) — a meaningful reduction, partly reflecting tobacco control (lung cancer), cervical screening (HPV vaccination and 2017 program renewal), and expanded bowel screening
- **Mortality decline:** 148 → 105/100,000 — a 29% decline over 14 years, larger than the non-Indigenous decline

But this progress pre-dates the Australian Cancer Plan (which launched in 2023) and largely reflects long-term policy trends rather than Plan-specific actions. The critical question for the 2-year evaluation is whether the Plan has added momentum, particularly through the $238.5 million investment in ACCHO capacity and culturally safe mainstream services.

The structural barriers that remain are not primarily about funding alone. First Nations cancer patients are still more likely to be diagnosed at late stage (because screening participation remains below target in most programs). They are still more likely to forgo treatment or have it disrupted (because of geographic distance, cultural unsafety in mainstream services, and lack of transport and accommodation support). And they are still systematically under-identified in cancer registry data — meaning that the gap may be even larger than the data shows.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Strategic Objective vs. Elimination-Style Target
The National Cervical Cancer Elimination Strategy is built around an explicit, time-bounded, quantified target: elimination by 2035, defined as incidence below 4/100,000. The Australian Cancer Plan's equity objective for First Nations is expressed as an ambition — "equitable cancer outcomes" — without a comparable numerical or temporal commitment. The policy literature on health equity consistently shows that directional goals without quantified targets are less effective at driving accountable action. The 2-year review (due late 2025 / early 2026) is the first real test of whether "shared responsibility" can deliver measurable progress in the absence of binding targets.

### Tension 2: Voluntary Architecture in a Fragmented System
Cancer care in Australia is delivered across Commonwealth, state, and territory governments; public and private hospitals; NGOs; research institutions; and community health services. No single actor controls the system. The "shared responsibility" model acknowledges this reality. But voluntary coordination in a complex, resource-constrained system tends to produce uneven implementation: organisations with existing capacity and motivation participate; those in greatest need of support may not. Whether the ACCN and the ACCHO capacity investment overcome this structural bias will be the central equity test of the Plan's first five years.

### Tension 3: The Data Gap Problem
Australia cannot fully measure its First Nations cancer outcomes because Indigenous identification in cancer registry data is incomplete. The AIHW's innovative methods for the July 2026 data release have partially addressed this, but the fundamental problem — that cancer registry data quality varies significantly by jurisdiction — has not been solved. A plan that cannot accurately measure the baseline cannot confidently track progress. The National Cancer Data Framework (currently in development) will need to mandate Indigenous identification to close this gap.

### Tension 4: Funding Adequacy Over a Decade of Need
$238.5 million over ten years is the largest First Nations cancer investment Australia has made. It is also substantially less than the scale of the problem — 1,200 deaths per year, a 45% mortality gap, and deep structural deficits in ACCHO cancer capacity that predate the plan by decades. Without indexation, a back-loaded funding profile, or a commitment to sustained funding beyond 2033, the investment risks being absorbed by existing structural deficits rather than creating the new capacity the plan requires.

### Tension 5: The NBCSP Equity Deficit
The Australian Cancer Plan encompasses all cancer screening programs — including the NBCSP. But as documented in Reports 083–088, the NBCSP has no dedicated equity strategy for First Nations participants comparable to the National Cervical Cancer Elimination Strategy. Bowel cancer causes more male First Nations cancer deaths than any other cancer type; yet the NBCSP has no First Nations participation target, no mandated follow-up accountability, and no alternative delivery model with sufficient funding to scale. The Australian Cancer Plan's Strategic Objective 1 theoretically covers this gap — but the Plan's voluntary architecture provides no lever to compel reform of the NBCSP's equity infrastructure.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **HPV vaccination coverage among Indigenous youth: the upstream prevention pipeline.** The Lancet 2026 modelling (from Report 088) identified vaccination as one of three binding constraints on cervical cancer elimination in First Nations communities. What is current vaccination coverage among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adolescents, how is it delivered, and is it on a trajectory to sustain the prevention pipeline?

2. **First Nations cancer data and Indigenous identification in cancer registries.** The AIHW's July 2026 data release used innovative methods to address longstanding data gaps. But the fundamental problem — incomplete Indigenous identification in cancer registry data across jurisdictions — remains. How does Australia's cancer data governance compare with international best practice, and what would it take to mandate adequate data collection?

3. **Medical oncology gap payments and PBS cancer medicine access: the treatment equity gap for First Nations patients.** High cancer mortality in First Nations communities is not only a screening problem — it is a treatment access problem. What does the out-of-pocket cost structure of cancer treatment (specialist fees, chemotherapy co-payments, radiotherapy access) look like for First Nations patients, particularly those in rural and remote areas?

4. **The 2-year Australian Cancer Plan review (due late 2025).** Cancer Australia committed to a formal evaluation at the 2-year mark. Has this review been published? What did it find? Does it trigger any escalation mechanism? This would be the first systematic assessment of whether the Plan's shared-responsibility model is working.

5. **The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025 and quality governance.** Flagged in Report 088 as an unresolved thread: what does the 2025 update to the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care's colonoscopy standard say about equity, follow-up, and accountability — and does it provide any lever that the NBCSP itself lacks?
