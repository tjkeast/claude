# Nurse Endoscopists and the Colonoscopy Workforce: Australia's Contested Solution to a 50,000-Procedure Shortfall

**Report 081 — 2026-08-26**

**Summary:** Australia's colonoscopy workforce is ageing, geographically concentrated, and heading for a structural shortfall precisely as NBCSP demand accelerates. Advanced practice nurse endoscopy — deployed at scale in the UK and trialled in Victoria since 2013 — could meaningfully expand capacity. But the Australian model is constrained by a Medicare Benefits Schedule that restricts colonoscopy billing to medical practitioners, a gastroenterology establishment with strong incentives to resist scope-of-practice expansion, and training infrastructure that has never moved beyond a handful of metropolitan public hospitals.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 080 documented that Australia's National Bowel Cancer Screening Program generates approximately 100,000 positive iFOBT results per year but that only 14.6% of those people receive a colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day window, largely due to capacity constraints. It noted that NSW's Direct Access Colonoscopy model uses nurse-led pre-procedure assessment — but stops short of nurse-performed colonoscopy — and that the scope-of-practice debate in Australian endoscopy remains contested. This report examines that debate directly.

---

## Background: Why Colonoscopy Demand Is Outpacing Supply

Australia's MBS-funded colonoscopy use grew from approximately 284,676 in 2001 to 663,213 in 2019 — a 133% increase in 18 years driven by an ageing population, growing NBCSP participation, and broadening clinical indications for surveillance. Modelling from Cancer Australia and the Daffodil Centre projects annual MBS colonoscopy demand will exceed 780,000 by 2030.

The July 2024 expansion of the NBCSP to include 45–49-year-olds — adding approximately 1.2 million Australians to the eligible cohort — materially tightened an already strained system. Workforce modelling published in *BMC Health Services Research* (2021) estimated the expanded NBCSP would require an additional 50,000–60,000 colonoscopies per year above the pre-expansion baseline.

Against that demand trajectory sits a workforce under acute pressure. As of March 2021, the Conjoint Committee for the Recognition of Training in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (CCRTGE) listed 2,698 medical practitioners recognised for adult colonoscopy, of whom 2,123 held active specialist registration with AHPRA. Of those, a workforce survey found that **38% were considering giving up colonoscopy within the next two to five years**, with retirement (58% of responses) and pension considerations (23%) as the dominant reasons. This attrition profile means Australia's colonoscopy workforce is not just insufficient today — it is shrinking faster than it is being replenished.

The geographic distribution of that workforce compounds the problem. In capital city Local Government Areas, there is roughly one endoscopist per 0.33 km² to 62 km². In rural LGAs, that figure stretches to one per 23,382 km² to 267,780 km² — a distribution that makes a scheduled rural colonoscopy, for many patients, contingent on driving hundreds of kilometres to a regional hub.

## Key Findings

### 1. The APNE Program: Victoria's Decade-Long Experiment

Australia's most developed model for nurse-led endoscopy is Victoria's **Advanced Practice Nurse Endoscopy (APNE)** program, established in 2013–14 when the Victorian Government funded the State Endoscopy Training Centre (SETC) at Austin Health.

The APNE pathway requires gastroenterology nurses with substantial existing endoscopy experience to undertake **a minimum of 12 months of advanced skills training** under direct supervision of credentialled gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons. The training encompasses colonoscopy from caecal intubation, polypectomy, and the clinical decision-making required for safe independent practice.

The program operates through a **multidisciplinary team model**: APNE-trained nurses perform colonoscopies within a structured governance framework, with medical oversight and credentialling maintained by the SETC. Austin Health developed companion documents — organisational readiness checklists, trainee position descriptions, and clinical competency frameworks — intended to be replicated by other Victorian health services.

In practice, the program has remained concentrated in a small number of metropolitan public hospitals. Questions about the scalability of the training pipeline — which requires not just willing nurses but willing gastroenterologist supervisors, simulator access, and procedural volume for trainees — have limited uptake. There is no equivalent program in any other Australian state or territory that has reached comparable maturity.

### 2. The MBS Barrier: Colonoscopy Items Are Medical Practitioner-Only

The structural reason APNE programs are confined to the public system is straightforward: **colonoscopy MBS items 32222–32228 require a medical practitioner to bill Medicare**. A nurse endoscopist — even one fully credentialled through the Victorian APNE pathway — cannot independently claim a Medicare rebate for performing a colonoscopy.

This has two consequences:

**In public hospitals**, colonoscopies are funded through global hospital budgets (Commonwealth-State block grants, the activity-based funding (ABF) model), not through MBS billing. An APNE nurse in a public hospital performs colonoscopy within the hospital's funded service envelope. The patient pays nothing; the cost is absorbed by the state health budget. This makes nurse endoscopy financially viable in the public system but invisible to the MBS data that is the primary accountability mechanism for the colonoscopy workforce.

**In private practice**, there is no equivalent funding pathway. A private practice gastroenterology group cannot employ a nurse endoscopist to perform colonoscopies and bill the MBS. The only way a patient in the private system has a colonoscopy performed by a credentialled endoscopist is via a medical practitioner. Nurse endoscopy is therefore structurally excluded from the private colonoscopy market, which accounts for a substantial share of total colonoscopy volume in Australia.

This MBS restriction is not a mere administrative detail: it means that even comprehensive evidence on APNE safety and quality would not, on its own, make nurse endoscopy economically viable outside the public hospital sector without a legislative change to the Health Insurance Act 1973 and an MBS item descriptor amendment.

### 3. Quality and Safety Evidence: What the Research Shows

The international evidence on non-physician endoscopists — mainly from the UK's 30-year nurse endoscopy programme — is broadly reassuring about safety and quality equivalence for defined low-complexity procedures.

The most-cited quality data from the UK comes from Leeds, where nurse endoscopists achieved a **caecal intubation rate of 92%** — identical to the consultant gastroenterologist rate at the same centre. The average polyp detection index was virtually identical (34.5 for nurse endoscopists versus 33.5 for consultants). In Leeds, seven nurse endoscopists undertake approximately 22% of gastroscopies, 27% of colonoscopies, and all flexible sigmoidoscopies at their centre.

UK national data from the National Endoscopy Database (published in *Gastrointestinal Endoscopy* in 2025) showed an overall colonoscopy caecal intubation rate of 93.5% nationally — an improvement from 2011 levels — though with significant variation by endoscopist procedure volumes, with almost 40% of endoscopists still falling short of minimum quality standards.

A systematic review of non-physician endoscopists (*World Journal of Gastroenterology*, 2015) concluded that **most studies found nurse endoscopists safely perform procedures** after appropriate training, but also noted significant shortcomings in study design, selection bias, and generalisability. Critically, the review found that nurse endoscopy is **not consistently demonstrated to be more cost-effective** than standard physician-led models — partly because training costs, ongoing supervision requirements, and indemnity arrangements absorb much of the labour cost differential.

The Australian evidence is more limited. An APNE-specific outcomes study from the Austin Health programme in the mid-2010s demonstrated comparable caecal intubation rates and procedural complication rates to physician benchmarks in the Victorian context, but this was a single-centre study with a small cohort. No multi-centre, prospective Australian trial of APNE colonoscopy quality has been published.

### 4. The Gastroenterology Establishment's Response

Professional opposition to nurse endoscopy in Australia has been consistent, if not always publicly prominent. The Australian Medical Association of Queensland (AMAQ) made an explicit public statement in 2016 arguing that the solution to colonoscopy capacity was to train more gastroenterologists rather than extend scope to nurses.

GESA (the Gastroenterological Society of Australia) has been more measured in public statements, but its standard position has been that colonoscopy should be performed by practitioners with comprehensive gastroenterology training — a standard that, in practice, describes specialist medical training. The 2025 Joint Position Statement on Quality Upper Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (from GESA and the Gastroenterological Society of New Zealand) emphasised objective quality metrics and endoscopist volume thresholds — standards that, if applied strictly, could be used to restrict nurse endoscopy rather than enable it, depending on who controls the credentialling process.

The CCRTGE — the body that controls recognition of training for colonoscopy — is predominantly composed of representatives from medical specialist colleges. It has no formal nurse endoscopist credentialling pathway at a national level. The Victorian APNE programme operates through state-level governance at the SETC, outside the CCRTGE framework, meaning APNE-credentialled nurses have no nationally portable credential.

This is a significant structural gap: a nurse trained at Austin Health to perform colonoscopy under the APNE pathway holds a Victorian-specific credential, not a national one. A move to a different state or hospital resets the process.

### 5. SaferCare Victoria's 2025 Recommendations

In late 2024, SaferCare Victoria published its *Promoting Best Practice Colonoscopy Recommendations Report* (March 2025 release), which addressed quality standards for colonoscopy across Victorian public hospitals. The report endorsed the use of **objective quality metrics and objective assessment tools** for certifying extended scope of practice — a recommendation that implicitly supports structured APNE pathways if tied to measurable quality benchmarks.

The report recommended colonoscopy audits to ensure minimum competency thresholds (caecal intubation rate, adenoma detection rate, withdrawal time) across all endoscopists, including those in extended scope roles. Critically, it did not propose restricting APNE practice — rather, it sought to ensure quality measurement applied equally.

This represents a modest but meaningful policy signal: a Victorian safety authority endorsing the quality framework within which nurse endoscopy operates, rather than questioning whether it should operate at all.

### 6. The DAC Model: How Far Nurse-Led Endoscopy Goes Without Performing Colonoscopy

NSW's Direct Access Colonoscopy (DAC) model — described in Report 080 — illustrates the limits of the current Australian consensus. DAC is explicitly nurse-led in pre-procedure assessment: nurses conduct the clinical suitability assessment, manage the Refer4Scope protocol, coordinate the booking, and lead patient preparation. This substantially reduces the workload on gastroenterologists and eliminates the specialist consultation step that previously added weeks to the pathway.

But the colonoscopy itself is performed by a credentialled medical endoscopist. DAC nurses do not perform the procedure. The model is designed to make better use of limited gastroenterologist time, not to replace gastroenterologists in the procedural role.

DAC is the limit of what is currently politically and professionally possible in Australia without a formal scope-of-practice reform — and even this model has been contested by some gastroenterologists who believe GP-referral to colonoscopy without a prior specialist consultation increases risk of missing clinical contraindications.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Training Pipeline Is a Real Constraint, Not Just a Political One

Nurse endoscopy advocates sometimes present the MBS restriction and professional opposition as the only barriers to scale. But the training pipeline has its own bottlenecks. A 12-month supervised training pathway requires a gastroenterologist willing to supervise, simulator access, sufficient procedural volume for the trainee (typically 200–300 supervised colonoscopies to achieve competency), and an institutional governance framework. These conditions exist at Austin Health. They do not exist in most of rural Victoria, let alone remote Australia — the places where colonoscopy capacity is most needed. Scaling APNE to regional settings requires more than lifting the MBS restriction.

### Tension 2: Scope-of-Practice Expansion Has Opponents on Both Sides

Opposition to nurse endoscopy comes from medical societies. But there is also a question within nursing: the GENCA (Gastroenterological Nurses College of Australasia) represents endoscopy nurses, and while it supports extended scope, the practical burden of training, maintaining competency, and carrying medicolegal exposure for an independent proceduralist role is substantial. Not all experienced endoscopy nurses want to perform colonoscopies; some prefer to maintain their current role assisting medical endoscopists.

### Tension 3: Cost-Effectiveness Is Not Proven

The systematic review evidence suggests that nurse endoscopy is not consistently more cost-effective than physician-led models. Training costs, ongoing supervision, quality assurance, and indemnity arrangements reduce the labour cost savings significantly. In the Australian public hospital context, the relevant comparison is not nurse versus gastroenterologist cost per procedure (both are salary-funded) but throughput per available session. If APNE nurses can perform more colonoscopies per session day than the current model allows, there is a capacity benefit even without a pure cost saving.

### Tension 4: The MBS Reform Required Is Legally and Administratively Complex

Amending MBS colonoscopy item descriptors to allow nurse endoscopists to bill would require legislative change (the Health Insurance Act 1973), not merely administrative action by the Department of Health. It would also require establishment of a national credentialling framework, an indemnity solution, and a regulatory pathway through AHPRA or an equivalent body. This is a multi-year reform process, not a ministerial announcement.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Interval cancers in the NBCSP: the quality measure that colonoscopy debates avoid.** Interval cancers — colorectal cancers diagnosed in the interval between a negative screening test and the next scheduled screen — are the most important quality failure metric in a screening programme. A 2026 *Medical Journal of Australia* study examined interval cancer characteristics among WA NBCSP participants. This is the quality argument that makes the nurse endoscopist debate more than just a workforce numbers question.

2. **The CCRTGE and credentialling architecture: who controls the right to perform colonoscopy in Australia?** The Conjoint Committee for the Recognition of Training in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy is a multi-college body that determines which practitioners are recognised as competent to perform colonoscopy. Understanding its governance, membership, and decision-making is essential to understanding why scope-of-practice reform is difficult.

3. **Colonoscopy simulation training: how VR technology is changing the competency acquisition curve.** High-fidelity colonoscopy simulators (including the EndoVR system used at some Australian training centres) can reduce the number of supervised live procedures needed to achieve competency, potentially making nurse endoscopy training faster and more accessible. The evidence on simulation-based training and its role in workforce expansion.

4. **MBS indexation failure across surgical procedures: colonoscopy as a case study.** The 2020 MBS colonoscopy reform (items 32222–32228) improved clinical targeting but did not address the fundamental gap between MBS schedule fees (~$340 for the primary NBCSP follow-up item) and actual procedure costs (~$1,300 specialist fee). A broader examination of MBS fee indexation and how the erosion of real schedule fees is driving gap payments across procedural medicine.

5. **First Nations bowel screening and mobile colonoscopy: a purpose-built access model.** First Nations Australians face lower NBCSP participation and colonoscopy follow-up rates despite higher CRC risk in some regions. Mobile colonoscopy units have been piloted in Queensland. The evidence on whether outreach endoscopy can close the access gap, and what it costs.
