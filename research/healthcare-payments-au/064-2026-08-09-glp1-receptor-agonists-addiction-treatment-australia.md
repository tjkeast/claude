# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Addiction: When a Diabetes Drug Becomes the Most Promising Addiction Medicine in a Generation

**Report 064 — 2026-08-09**

**Summary:** Drugs originally approved for type 2 diabetes and obesity — semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and other GLP-1 receptor agonists — are producing some of the most striking addiction medicine signals in decades: a 600,000-person BMJ cohort study found GLP-1 use associated with 14–25% lower risk of substance use disorders across every major addictive substance; a 2026 Lancet RCT showed semaglutide reduced heavy drinking days in people with alcohol use disorder by 41% versus 26% for placebo; and Australia's Kirby Institute has launched the world's first semaglutide trial specifically for methamphetamine use disorder. But Australia's PBS system — which lists semaglutide only for type 2 diabetes and, now, specific cardiovascular risk — has no pathway for addiction indications, and the half of Australians accessing GLP-1s privately are disproportionately wealthy and urban, the inverse of who bears the heaviest substance use burden.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 063 documented that Australia's methamphetamine crisis has no approved pharmacotherapy anywhere in the world, that Australia's research infrastructure (NCCRED, Kirby Institute) is among the most active globally in the search for one, and that the Burnet Institute was investigating semaglutide as an emerging candidate. This report examines what is now known about GLP-1 agonists as addiction treatments, the quality of the evidence, the Australian research response, and the policy and payment barriers to translating promising signals into accessible treatment.

---

## Background

The GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonist drug class began as an incretin-based diabetes treatment and became a global cultural phenomenon through its effects on body weight. But the biological story of these drugs is stranger and more interesting than their marketing suggests. GLP-1 receptors are not confined to the pancreas and gut — they are expressed throughout the brain, including in the **ventral tegmental area (VTA), nucleus accumbens, and prefrontal cortex**: the core structures of the mesocorticolimbic dopamine system that addiction medicine has spent four decades trying to modulate pharmacologically.

This anatomical fact, documented in preclinical work from the late 2010s onwards, generated an obvious question: if GLP-1 agonists modulate the brain's reward circuitry — reducing the dopamine signal that makes food hyper-rewarding — might they do the same for drugs? The preclinical answer, now confirmed across dozens of animal studies and multiple species, has been yes: GLP-1 agonists including exendin-4, liraglutide, and semaglutide consistently reduce self-administration of alcohol, cocaine, heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone, methamphetamine, and nicotine in preclinical models, and suppress drug-seeking and cue-induced relapse-like behaviour. The 2025 *Science* paper from the WashU group on hedonic eating — showing that dopamine neurons specifically oppose GLP-1R satiety signals — added a mechanistic framework that explains a class-wide anti-craving effect beyond appetite suppression.

Whether this translates cleanly to human addiction is the question 2025 and 2026 have substantially advanced.

## Key Findings

### 1. The BMJ Cohort Study: 600,000 Veterans, 14–25% Lower Substance Use Disorder Risk

The largest population-level evidence base for GLP-1s and addiction published in **The BMJ in March 2026**: an analysis of more than **600,000 US veterans with type 2 diabetes**, comparing those prescribed a GLP-1 receptor agonist with those on other diabetes medications.

GLP-1 use was associated with a **14% lower risk of developing any substance use disorder**, with substance-specific reductions of:

- **18% lower risk for alcohol use disorder**
- **14% lower risk for cannabis use disorder**
- **20% lower risk for cocaine use disorder**
- **20% lower risk for nicotine use disorder**
- **25% lower risk for opioid use disorder**

In people who *already had* a substance use disorder, GLP-1 use was associated with reduced overdoses, fewer drug-related emergency department visits, and lower drug-related mortality.

Limitations are significant: this is an observational cohort, veterans are a specific demographic, and confounding by indication (GLP-1 users may be systematically different from comparison groups in ways unmeasured) cannot be excluded. But the consistency across all major substance classes — pointing to a class-wide, not drug-specific, effect — is a distinctive signal that is difficult to explain by confounding alone.

### 2. The Lancet RCT: Semaglutide Outperforms Placebo for Alcohol Use Disorder

The most rigorous evidence to date arrived in **The Lancet in May 2026**: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled 26-week trial of once-weekly semaglutide in **108 adults with moderate-to-severe alcohol use disorder and co-occurring obesity** (54 per arm).

**Key results:**
- Semaglutide group: **–41.1 percentage points** reduction in heavy drinking days from baseline
- Placebo group: **–26.4 percentage points** reduction in heavy drinking days from baseline
- **Net treatment effect: ~15 percentage points** fewer heavy drinking days for semaglutide
- Secondary outcomes: significant reductions in total alcohol consumption, drinks per drinking day, and self-reported craving scores
- Safety: adverse events were predominantly transient, mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal effects (nausea, constipation), more frequent in the semaglutide group

This is the first Phase 3-equivalent RCT of a GLP-1 agonist for a substance use disorder to publish in a top-tier general medical journal. Earlier, a 2025 **JAMA Psychiatry** randomised trial of low-dose semaglutide over nine weeks had found significantly lower alcohol cravings and fewer drinks per drinking day in people with alcohol use disorder — the Lancet trial was longer, larger, and more definitive.

The US Department of Veterans Affairs has now launched a full **Phase 3 trial** of semaglutide for alcohol use disorder in veterans (estimated start July 2026; primary completion April 2028), the largest trial in this space globally.

### 3. The Mechanism: Reward Salience Modulation, Not Just Appetite

A **2025 Frontiers in Pharmacology systematic review** (published July 2025) synthesised evidence from 33 registered clinical trials and extensive preclinical literature, identifying the core mechanism:

GLP-1 receptors in the **VTA and nucleus accumbens** modulate mesolimbic dopamine signalling — reducing the dopamine response to drug cues and drug administration, and suppressing the reward salience that drives compulsive use. GLP-1R activation engages circuits involving the VTA, nucleus accumbens, lateral septum, laterodorsal tegmentum, and nucleus tractus solitarius. The effect is not sedation or aversion — it is a reduction in the motivational pull of the drug, which maps onto the clinical experience of GLP-1 patients who report that alcohol, cigarettes, or food simply become less compelling.

This mechanism suggests a *class-wide* anti-addiction effect, not drug-specific properties of semaglutide — which means the entire GLP-1/GIP dual-agonist class (tirzepatide and successors) may carry the same potential.

### 4. Australia's Response: The SHIFT Study and the NHMRC Investment

Australia has responded with characteristic academic speed but policy lag. The **SHIFT Study** (Semaglutide for Treatment of People With Methamphetamine Use Disorder; NCT07509112) is the world's first clinical trial of semaglutide specifically for methamphetamine use disorder. It is:

- Led by **Scientia Professor Greg Dore** at the **Kirby Institute, UNSW Sydney**
- Funded by a **$3,035,320 NHMRC Investigator Grant** awarded in July 2026
- Estimated start: **May 2026**; primary completion: **January 2027**
- A pilot/feasibility study to assess safety and early efficacy signals in people with methamphetamine use disorder

This is a directly practical trial: methamphetamine is Australia's most hospitalisation-intensive drug (29% of all drug-related admissions in 2023-24), has no approved pharmacotherapy anywhere in the world, and — as Report 063 documented — the LiMA and Tina trial results for lisdexamfetamine and mirtazapine are promising but years from TGA registration. If the SHIFT pilot generates a positive signal, it would strengthen the case for a larger Phase 3 trial, with the DAP redesign process providing a potential commissioning pathway.

A second Australian methamphetamine-related GLP-1 trial is also registered: **"Glutide for Ending Methamphetamine"** (NCT07204249), suggesting multiple research groups are converging on this question simultaneously.

### 5. Australia's GLP-1 Market: Half a Million Users, Half Excluded From PBS

A **July 2026 UNSW study** (preprint; now mainstream reporting) documented the scale of Australia's GLP-1 uptake: **approximately 500,000 Australians are regularly using GLP-1 receptor agonists**. Total GLP-1 sales increased **almost 10-fold since May 2020**. By April 2025, **280,000 units/month** were dispensed on the PBS (for type 2 diabetes); a further **180,000–240,000 units/month** were accessed through the private market.

The private market is where the equity story fractures. Private GLP-1 prescriptions — primarily semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) for obesity without type 2 diabetes — cost **$250–$450 per month** without subsidy. This is not accessible to people in financial hardship, and the distribution of private-market GLP-1 users skews strongly toward higher-income, urban, non-Indigenous Australians.

The PBAC addressed this in November 2025 by recommending semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) for PBS listing — but with narrow eligibility: **adults with established cardiovascular disease (prior heart attack, stroke, or symptomatic peripheral arterial disease) and obesity**. A BMI ≥35 criterion has also been mooted. The listing has not yet been finalised as at August 2026.

Critically, **substance use disorder is not an approved indication for semaglutide under the TGA** in any formulation. Using it for addiction would be off-label prescribing, which is legal in Australia but creates PBS access barriers (no subsidised listing), professional indemnity questions for prescribers, and monitoring challenges.

### 6. The PBS-to-Addiction Pathway: Structural Misalignment

The journey from "GLP-1 works for addiction" to "person with methamphetamine use disorder can access semaglutide on the PBS" is long and structurally blocked at every stage:

1. **TGA registration** for an addiction indication requires a completed Phase 3 RCT with a registered drug (or off-label recommendation); the SHIFT study is a Phase 1/2 pilot
2. **PBAC assessment** then requires a full cost-effectiveness submission for the new indication — at Australian semaglutide prices ($380–$450/month private), cost-effectiveness against a comparator of "no treatment" may be favourable, but the process takes years
3. **Off-label prescribing** is the only near-term pathway, but GPs prescribing Ozempic off-label for alcohol use disorder or methamphetamine dependence would do so without PBS subsidy, without clinical guidelines, and with limited specialist support
4. **The populations with highest methamphetamine and alcohol use disorder burden** — First Nations Australians, people in very remote communities, people in financial hardship — are precisely those least able to afford $350–$450/month for private-market Wegovy

The PBAC's own equitable access review, requested by Health Minister Butler in March 2025, has acknowledged that the private-market GLP-1 boom is generating health inequity. But the PBAC's November 2025 recommendation addressed obesity/CVD equity, not the addiction indication that doesn't yet exist.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Signal vs. causation.** The BMJ cohort study and other observational data are striking but cannot establish causation. People prescribed GLP-1s may differ systematically from comparators in ways that reduce substance use risk independently of the drug (better metabolic health, more GP contact, higher socioeconomic status). Only RCTs can resolve this, and the Lancet alcohol trial — while positive — was small (108 people) and short (26 weeks). Phase 3 replication is essential.

**2. The methamphetamine mechanism gap.** GLP-1 agonists reduce *dopamine-mediated reward salience*. Methamphetamine produces dopamine surges of a magnitude that qualitatively outstrips natural rewards or alcohol — it is plausible but not certain that GLP-1 receptor modulation can meaningfully damp a methamphetamine-grade dopamine flood. The SHIFT study is the test; NCCRED researchers have noted this mechanistic uncertainty explicitly.

**3. Off-label prescribing and the GP burden.** If GLP-1s are effective for addiction, Australian GPs will face the same prescribing pressure they face with every evidence-shifting moment: patients and advocates will request the drug before guidelines exist, before PBS subsidies are available, and before specialist oversight frameworks are in place. This is not hypothetical — it is already occurring in alcohol use disorder clinics in major cities, according to addiction medicine specialists.

**4. The PBS pathway for addiction indications.** Australia has no precedent for a blockbuster metabolic drug being added to the PBS for a substance use disorder indication. The cost-effectiveness framework would need to weigh semaglutide's cost against the full health-system cost of untreated alcohol use disorder or methamphetamine use disorder — a calculation that would likely be favourable, but which has never been done for this drug class in this indication.

**5. Supply.** Ozempic experienced global supply shortages in 2022–2024 driven by off-label weight loss demand. Adding addiction to the demand would create pressure on a supply chain that Novo Nordisk has since scaled. But it is not costless — expanded PBS indications translate directly to government expenditure.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Alcohol use disorder treatment in Australia — the MBS gap** — Australia's treatment for AUD is fragmented, underfunded, and almost entirely outside MBS coverage. The Lancet semaglutide trial highlights this: AUD affects 800,000 Australians meeting diagnostic criteria, yet PBS has no pharmacotherapy equivalent to OAT, and only three MBS-listed medications (naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram) treat AUD, all with limited uptake due to prescribing complexity and poor GP training.

2. **The National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — the current strategy expires at the end of 2026. The consultation underway is the primary policy vehicle for deciding whether GLP-1s, contingency management, and new pharmacotherapy pathways receive structural commitment or remain research curiosities.

3. **First Nations communities and GLP-1 access** — GLP-1s are particularly relevant to First Nations Australians, who face disproportionate burdens of both metabolic disease (type 2 diabetes, obesity) and substance use disorders, but who have the least access to private-market GLP-1s. The PBAC equity review acknowledged this gap for obesity; the addiction dimension has not been considered.

4. **The pharmaceutical cost-effectiveness case for treating addiction** — a formal economic analysis of what subsidising semaglutide for alcohol or methamphetamine use disorder would cost vs. save in hospitalisation, emergency presentations, policing, and productivity terms. Australia's HTA framework has never been applied to this question; if the numbers work, it could change the PBAC's cost-effectiveness calculus dramatically.

5. **Tobacco cessation and GLP-1s** — nicotine showed the largest and most consistent risk reduction in the BMJ cohort (20%), and smoking cessation is already an MBS-supported activity. A GLP-1-assisted smoking cessation pathway would be the fastest route to an addiction indication with existing MBS infrastructure.
