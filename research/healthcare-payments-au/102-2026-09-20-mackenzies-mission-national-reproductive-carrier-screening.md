# Mackenzie's Mission and the Case for Universal Reproductive Carrier Screening at Scale

**Report 102 — 2026-09-20**

**Summary:** Australia's landmark Mackenzie's Mission research program — published in the New England Journal of Medicine in November 2024 — screened 9,107 couples for more than 1,280 genes and found approximately 1 in 50 couples had a previously unknown increased risk of having a child with a severe genetic condition. Critically, 80% of those at-risk couples were carriers for conditions outside the three currently Medicare-funded ones (cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, fragile X syndrome). Cost-effectiveness modelling published in 2025 finds that expanded screening covering 569 conditions is cost-saving from a health-service perspective at 50% uptake. MSAC declined to fund an expanded panel in 2022, recommending resubmission and a rethink of implementation infrastructure. A June 2025 Australian Genomics report lays out the architecture for a full national program — but the genetic counselling workforce gap documented in Report 101 remains its binding constraint.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 101 documented that Australia has only ~346 FTE genetic counsellors in clinical practice, that there are no standalone MBS items for their services, and that every hereditary disease program — including reproductive carrier screening — depends on a workforce the Medicare system does not fund. This report examines the specific program that now most urgently requires that workforce at scale.

---

## Background: The Story Behind the Program

Mackenzie Casella was born on 10 June 2017 and died seven months later from spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) — a severe autosomal recessive condition for which her parents, Rachael and Jonathan Casella, were unknowing carriers. Neither parent had any family history of SMA. Their story catalysed a national conversation about whether all Australian couples planning a family should have the opportunity to know whether they carry genes for severe childhood conditions before making reproductive decisions.

In 2018, the Australian Government announced $20 million in funding for a national research project named after Mackenzie. The Australian Reproductive Genetic Carrier Screening Project — "Mackenzie's Mission" — was designed to answer a specific research question: can population-scale reproductive carrier screening (RCS) be delivered in Australia in a way that is feasible, acceptable, and cost-effective, and what would it take to make it permanent?

The program ran from 2019 to 2023, recruiting participants through GPs, obstetricians, fertility clinics, and online channels. It screened for more than **1,280 genes** associated with approximately **750 severe, childhood-onset, autosomal recessive and X-linked recessive conditions** — far beyond the three-condition panel that Medicare now funds.

## Key Findings

### 1. The NEJM Primary Paper (November 2024): 1 in 50 Couples at Risk

The primary findings from Mackenzie's Mission were published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* on 21 November 2024 (Archibald et al., NEJM 2024):

- **10,038 couples enrolled; 9,107 (90.7%) completed screening** — a completion rate far higher than many anticipated, demonstrating feasibility of a population program
- **175 couples (1.9% — approximately 1 in 50) were newly identified** as having an increased chance of having a child with a genetic condition included in the screen
- The 175 at-risk couples carried pathogenic variants in **90 different genes**
- **74.3% of identified conditions were autosomal recessive; 25.7% were X-linked**
- Among the 175 at-risk couples who received an increased-chance result, **three-quarters used that information to inform their reproductive decisions** — including using IVF with preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) or prenatal diagnosis (PND)
- One funded cycle of IVF with PGT-M was made available through the study for eligible couples; the majority of affected couples who pursued this pathway were able to avoid having an affected child

The NEJM editorial accompanying the paper called for a "Global Mission" — arguing that Australia's study provided the first large-scale real-world evidence that nationwide couple-based carrier screening is achievable and produces outcomes that couples value.

### 2. The 80% Problem: Why Three Genes Are Not Enough

The most consequential finding for Australian health policy is this: **80% of couples identified as being at increased risk were carriers for conditions that are not covered by the Medicare-funded three-gene screen** (CF, SMA, fragile X).

This means that the MBS items 73451 and 73452 — introduced on 1 November 2023, providing a once-per-lifetime rebate of approximately $340 for CF/SMA/fragile X screening — capture only about **20% of the preventable reproductive risk** that Mackenzie's Mission identified at the population level.

The remaining 80% of at-risk couples — carrying variants in conditions ranging from Duchenne muscular dystrophy to Pompe disease, phenylketonuria, congenital disorder of glycosylation, and hundreds of rare lysosomal storage disorders — currently face two options:
- **User-pays expanded panel testing** through private laboratories (typically $300–$600 for a broader panel, with no Medicare rebate)
- **No testing at all**, because they do not know their risk and cannot afford or access private options

This creates an explicit inequity: couples who can afford private testing can access information about their reproductive risk for the full range of conditions; couples who cannot afford it receive a screen that catches, at best, one in five of the pregnancies at elevated risk.

### 3. Current Medicare Coverage: The Three-Condition Baseline

The MBS items introduced in November 2023 cover:
- **Item 73451**: Initial screening of the female partner (pregnant or planning pregnancy) for CF, SMA, and fragile X syndrome
- **Item 73452**: Subsequent screening of the male partner when a pathogenic variant is found in the female for CF or SMA
- The benefit is a **once-per-lifetime benefit** — approximately $340 per episode — intended to fully offset the test cost

Additionally, items 73453–73455 provide expanded screening (nine conditions) for individuals identifying as **Ashkenazi Jewish** — reflecting a targeted approach for higher-prevalence variants in this population.

Despite this progress, GPs — who are the frontline delivery point — consistently report that:
- They receive **limited time and Medicare funding** for the pre-test counselling and consent processes that responsible carrier screening requires
- The MBS items fund the test, not the consultation work involved in explaining what a carrier result means or what happens when a couple receives an increased-chance result
- Access to **genetic counsellor support** is essential but largely unavailable outside major urban teaching hospitals

### 4. MSAC Application 1637: The Expanded Panel That Did Not Get Funded

In parallel with the Mackenzie's Mission research program, a formal MSAC application (Application 1637) was lodged in March 2020, seeking Medicare funding for **expanded reproductive carrier testing** covering the broader panel of autosomal recessive and X-linked conditions. This application went through the PICO Advisory Sub-Committee in August 2021, an Evaluation Sub-Committee meeting in June 2022, and was considered by the full MSAC in July 2022.

**MSAC's July 2022 decision:** MSAC did **not** recommend funding under the standard Medicare model. The Public Summary Document, however, made several important acknowledgements:
- It recognised **significant unmet need** and inequities in access
- It noted that the infrastructure and coordination requirements of this program were "more conventionally associated with formal population screening programs" (i.e. nationally organised programs like BreastScreen, the NBCSP) than with individual MBS items billed by GPs
- It **encouraged resubmission** addressing the concerns raised
- It recommended the Department of Health consider the "most appropriate implementation methodology" — implicitly acknowledging that a population screening program model, rather than an MBS billing model, may be more appropriate

As of mid-2026, no resubmission to MSAC is confirmed. The question of which implementation model is "most appropriate" remains formally unanswered.

### 5. Cost-Effectiveness: From Cost-Effective to Cost-Saving

The economic evidence for expanded reproductive carrier screening has strengthened considerably since the MSAC 2022 decision.

**2023 Economic Evaluation (Genetics in Medicine):**  
The first major cost-effectiveness analysis of a population-based expanded RCS program in Australia found an **incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of AUD $6,287 per QALY gained** for expanded screening versus three-condition screening. Against Australia's implicit willingness-to-pay threshold of $50,000/QALY, this makes expanded screening highly cost-effective — among the most cost-effective preventive health investments modelled in the Australian genomics literature.

**2025 Microsimulation Analysis (European Journal of Human Genetics / PMC):**  
A more sophisticated microsimulation model — "PreconMOD" — evaluated population-based expanded RCS for **569 recessive conditions** (reflecting a clinically realistic expanded panel). Key findings at **50% population uptake**:
- Expanded RCS is **cost-saving** (both higher QALYs and lower total costs) compared with three-condition screening, from both health-service and societal perspectives
- QALY gain: **78,974** from expanded screening vs **69,536** from three-condition screening (difference: ~9,400 QALYs)
- The cost savings arise primarily from preventing the lifetime healthcare costs associated with children born with severe genetic conditions — costs that, for many conditions, run to hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars per affected individual

The 2025 finding that expanded RCS is cost-saving (not merely cost-effective) substantially strengthens the policy case. A program that produces better health outcomes and saves the health system money has a rare economic profile that directly addresses Treasury concerns.

### 6. The Australian Genomics June 2025 Report: Architecture for a National Program

In June 2025, Australian Genomics published *Expanding the Scope of Reproductive Genetic Carrier Screening: Towards a National Screening Program* — a comprehensive analysis of what a universal national program would require.

The report's central recommendations:
1. **Expand the Medicare-funded panel** from three conditions to a broader clinically-curated set, moving toward coverage of the conditions in the Mackenzie's Mission gene panel
2. **Establish a nationally governed program infrastructure** — including a dedicated program office, a standing gene panel advisory committee, standardised laboratory reporting requirements, and a national outcomes registry
3. **Fund genetic counselling as an integral component**, not an optional add-on — the report identifies the genetic counselling workforce (Report 101's subject) as the primary constraint on safe and equitable delivery
4. **Invest in GP education and primary care support** to enable GPs to offer pre-test counselling within standard consultations
5. **Develop culturally appropriate resources** for First Nations communities and other populations with specific genetic risk profiles

The report estimates that a national program covering the expanded panel would require **significant Commonwealth investment in program infrastructure** beyond the test cost alone — particularly in genetic counselling, laboratory capacity, and data governance.

### 7. Access and Equity: Who Can Currently Access Expanded Testing?

Under the current arrangement, access to expanded carrier screening beyond the three Medicare-funded conditions is primarily determined by ability to pay:

- **Private laboratories** (e.g. Sonic Genetics, Lumos Genetics, Eugene Labs, Repromed) offer expanded panels ranging from ~50 conditions to 750+ conditions at prices of $300–$600 per couple or per individual, without Medicare rebate
- **Fertility clinic patients** pursuing IVF are more likely to be offered expanded carrier screening as part of their clinical workup, creating a de facto advantage for higher-socioeconomic couples already engaged with private fertility services
- **Rural and remote populations** face geographic and structural barriers to GP-delivered testing; those in regional areas report that their GPs are less likely to offer or discuss carrier screening
- **First Nations populations** — who have unique genetic risk profiles for some conditions and existing inequities in access to genomics services (documented across Reports 87, 97, 98, 101) — are not systematically reached by the current model

A BMC Medical Genomics paper published in 2026 on GPs' experience offering carrier screening in Australian primary care found that GPs' confidence in offering RCS was "contingent on the availability of expert genetic counselling and clinical support" — and where that support was not available (as it frequently is not), GPs often deferred or did not offer screening.

### 8. The IVF and Downstream Access Question

Identifying a couple as being at increased reproductive risk only generates value if they can access something useful with that information. The options are:

1. **Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Monogenic Disorders (PGT-M)** via IVF — the most definitive option for avoiding an affected pregnancy
2. **Prenatal diagnosis** (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis during pregnancy) followed by decision-making about the pregnancy
3. **Gamete donation** using an unaffected donor
4. **Accepting risk** with informed awareness

PGT-M is only accessible through IVF, which is expensive (a single cycle costs $6,000–$12,000 privately before Medicare and PHI rebates), emotionally demanding, and not universally successful. The question of whether at-risk couples identified through a national carrier screening program can actually access funded IVF cycles is one that Australian Genomics identified as requiring explicit policy attention.

Mackenzie's Mission provided one funded IVF cycle for eligible couples within the research program. A permanent national program would need to address whether — and under what criteria — the Commonwealth or states fund IVF access for carrier couples, or whether the program's equity benefits are undermined by a downstream access gap.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Which model — MBS items or population program?** MSAC's 2022 signal that a "population screening program" model may be more appropriate than MBS billing is significant but unresolved. Australia's national cancer screening programs (BreastScreen, NBCSP, cervical, bowel) operate through Commonwealth-funded program infrastructure, not MBS billing. Carrier screening may need similar architecture — but this requires explicit Cabinet-level decision-making, not just MSAC approval.

**2. What goes on the panel?** Gene selection is deeply contested. The Mackenzie's Mission panel screened for 1,280+ genes; the 2025 PreconMOD analysis evaluated 569 conditions; MSAC's PICO criteria apply strict thresholds (condition severity, clinical actionability, test analytic validity). A national program needs standing governance for panel decisions as genomic science evolves — who decides which conditions qualify, and how do they manage public pressure to add conditions with lower severity or weaker evidence?

**3. Can the workforce deliver it?** The June 2025 Australian Genomics report is explicit: the genetic counselling workforce cannot safely absorb a universal expanded screening program under current conditions. This is the binding constraint identified in Report 101. Without MBS items for genetic counsellors and a rapid expansion of the workforce, a national carrier screening program risks overwhelming already-scarce genetic counselling services.

**4. Does Medicare fund the test, the counselling, or both?** The current MBS items fund only the laboratory test, not the clinical consultation work around it. GPs cannot use a standard Level B or C consultation item to deliver pre- and post-test genetic counselling — the MBS does not recognise the genetic dimension as adding clinical value above a standard consult. This needs to be fixed regardless of whether the panel is expanded.

**5. The downstream equity gap: IVF access.** A carrier screening program that identifies risk but does not fund the reproductive options for acting on that information has limited equity value for low-income couples who cannot afford IVF. This is a known gap that requires explicit policy resolution.

**6. The timing of the ask.** Budget 2026-27 has passed without a major announcement on reproductive carrier screening expansion. The question is whether the June 2025 Australian Genomics report, the 2024 NEJM publication, and the 2025 cost-saving economics together provide sufficient evidence for an MYEFO or Budget 2027-28 decision.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) and IVF funding policy** — For carrier couples identified through screening, PGT-M is the primary clinical option for avoiding an affected pregnancy. What does Medicare currently fund for IVF, what are the out-of-pocket costs, and what would a national carrier screening program require in terms of accompanying IVF funding reform?

2. **Population-scale newborn screening expansion** — The NBSM (National Bloodspot Screening Panel) covers ~25 conditions at birth. MSAC and the Department of Health have active work on expansion to more conditions. The logic parallels reproductive carrier screening: the question is whether to catch conditions prenatally (carrier screening) or postnatally (newborn screening), and which catches more value.

3. **Spinal muscular atrophy: from carrier screening to neonatal treatment** — SMA was the condition that triggered Mackenzie's Mission. Australia now has gene therapy (onasemnogene abeparvovec, Zolgensma) and risdiplam on the PBS for SMA. The interplay between neonatal identification, treatment eligibility windows, and what carrier screening vs newborn screening detects illustrates the broader logic of the genomic medicine pipeline.

4. **Direct-to-consumer genetic testing and reproductive risk: the unregulated parallel market** — While policy debates proceed slowly, hundreds of thousands of Australians have used commercial DTC tests (AncestryDNA, 23andMe). Some discover carrier status for severe conditions from consumer-grade reports. What is the regulatory framework, and what happens when GPs are asked to manage the downstream implications?

5. **The gene panel governance problem: who decides what goes on a national screening program?** — Australia lacks a standing multi-condition gene panel governance committee for reproductive screening, unlike the UK (UK NSC) or US (ACMG). Establishing one is a prerequisite for any national program and would have implications across newborn, cancer, and cardiovascular genomic screening.
