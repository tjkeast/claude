# Colorectal Cancer Treatment Costs and the Stage-at-Diagnosis Funding Equation: How $42 Million in Screening Offsets $1.85 Billion in Treatment

**Report 079 — 2026-08-24**

**Summary:** Bowel cancer is Australia's most expensive cancer to treat in dollar terms, consuming $1.85 billion in health system spending in 2023–24 — 44 times the $42.1 million spent running the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program that year. The economic logic of the NBCSP rests on a single mechanism: catching cancer at stage I instead of stage IV fundamentally transforms both the survival odds and the cost equation. Screen-detected cancers are diagnosed at stage I in approximately 40% of cases; symptom-detected cancers reach stage IV in 15–20% of cases. Closing that gap is projected to reduce cumulative bowel cancer expenditure by up to $2.1 billion between 2030 and 2040. Yet Australia's participation rate — hovering around 44% — leaves most of that saving unrealised, and the new opt-in architecture for 45–49 year olds threatens to replicate the same structural failure in the age group with the fastest-rising incidence.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 078 documented the doubling of early-onset colorectal cancer cases in Australia since 2000 and the July 2024 expansion of the NBCSP to 45–49 year olds on an opt-in basis. Thread 1 noted that the clinical urgency of catching CRC early is inseparable from the economic logic — stage I CRC costs approximately $14,000 to treat while stage IV exceeds $150,000, and the NBCSP's age expansion changes the stage distribution of detected cancers. This report examines that cost differential in detail, sets it against the NBCSP's budget, and shows how the program's funding design shapes Australia's downstream treatment burden.

---

## Background: Why Stage Determines Everything

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is simultaneously one of the most survivable cancers when caught early and one of the most lethal when caught late. Five-year survival for stage I CRC exceeds 90%; for stage IV (metastatic) disease, it is approximately 12–16%. The difference between a stage I and stage IV diagnosis is not merely clinical — it represents a fundamentally different set of interventions, care episodes, and health system costs extending across years of treatment.

Australia diagnosed an estimated 15,400 new cases of CRC in 2025, making it the second-most-commonly diagnosed cancer (after non-melanoma skin cancer). At the current mix of staging at diagnosis, the average cost per case is approximately $50,000–$61,000 in the first year alone. But that average conceals enormous stage-driven variation that is the entire financial argument for a population screening programme.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Treatment Cost Stack by Stage

The most complete Australian stage-stratified cost data comes from a 2021 NSW-based study (Daffodil Centre/Menzies) examining health system costs and hospital days for CRC patients in New South Wales, with figures expressed in 2020 Australian dollars.

**Average first-year health system costs:**
- Colon cancer: **$50,434** per case
- Rectal cancer: **$60,877** per case
- Average hospital days: 16 (colon) and 18.5 (rectal)

These averages mask the stage gradient, which is steep. A 2016 Australian study (*Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology*) examining the rapidly escalating cost of treating CRC found that while early-stage disease costs had not substantially increased over time, stage IV treatment costs were rising sharply — driven by the introduction of chemotherapy agents oxaliplatin and irinotecan, and subsequently by targeted therapies:

- **Stage IV base chemotherapy:** approximately **$71,156 per patient** (in the period under study)
- Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF targeted therapy): adds **~$10,247 per patient** in a treatment course; can run up to **$8,000 per month** with indefinite use
- Cetuximab (anti-EGFR): adds a further **~$12,022 per patient**
- Total stage IV treatment cost (with modern targeted regimens): routinely **$80,000–$120,000+** in the first year of treatment, with ongoing costs for continuing treatment

In 2025, bevacizumab, cetuximab, and encorafenib are all PBS-subsidised for eligible patients with metastatic CRC. The co-payment for PBS-listed chemotherapy items from 1 July 2025 is $31.60 (general) or $7.70 (concession), meaning the health system absorbs essentially the entire drug cost — which for bevacizumab can run indefinitely for as long as it maintains disease control.

Patient out-of-pocket costs for CRC are relatively modest in the public system — averaging approximately **$441 per year** in direct medical costs, with total mean direct costs of $3,860 per annum and Medicare reimbursement averaging $3,420. However, patients treated in private hospitals face higher gap payments on surgical procedures and outpatient oncology consultations. A 2024 study found CRC out-of-pocket costs and Medicare payments varied substantially by diagnostic stage, tumour anatomical location, mutation status, and socioeconomic position.

**The stage differential in practical terms:**
- Stage I CRC: predominantly surgical management, typically one or two hospital admissions; costs in the range of **$15,000–$35,000 total** across the care episode
- Stage II/III CRC: surgery plus adjuvant chemotherapy (typically FOLFOX or CAPOX for 6 months); total first-year costs of **$40,000–$70,000**
- Stage IV CRC (metastatic): ongoing palliative chemotherapy, targeted therapy, potentially multiple lines of treatment, hospice; total lifetime costs **$100,000–$200,000+**, with individual cases substantially higher

### 2. The $42 Million vs $1.85 Billion Gap

In 2023–24, AIHW data shows:
- **$1.85 billion** spent on bowel cancer treatment (health system total)
- **$42.1 million** spent running the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program

This produces a treatment-to-screening ratio of approximately **44:1**. Bowel cancer ranked as one of the top four cancers by spending, alongside lung cancer, non-melanoma skin cancer, and breast cancer. Cancer spending overall reached $19.7 billion in 2023–24, more than double the 2013–14 figure, with bowel cancer's share rising as PBS-listed targeted therapies for metastatic disease have become standard of care.

The NBCSP's annual operating cost ($42 million) includes program administration, the call-recall system, iFOBT kit procurement and processing, results communication, and registry maintenance — but does not include the colonoscopy follow-up costs for positive results. Those colonoscopy costs — approximately $800–$1,200 per procedure including anaesthetic — fall partly on Medicare (MBS items 32090 and 32093, which schedule fees of $556 and $640 respectively) and partly on patients through gap payments in private settings.

### 3. The Stage Shift: What Screening Actually Does to the Cost Curve

The economic case for the NBCSP rests entirely on its ability to shift the stage distribution of diagnosis toward earlier, less expensive, more survivable stages. The evidence on this is clear:

**Stage distribution comparison (screen-detected vs non-screen):**

| Stage | NBCSP screen-detected | Symptom-detected / non-participant |
|-------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Stage I (localised) | ~40% | ~14% |
| Stage II | ~28% | ~26% |
| Stage III | ~29% | ~45% |
| Stage IV (metastatic) | ~3–5.5% | ~15–20.6% |

Source: NBCSP monitoring data and a 2024 ANZ Journal of Surgery study (*National screening for colorectal cancer is associated with stage shift to earlier diagnosis*, Sarofim et al.).

The survival consequence of this stage shift is striking: an Australian hospital-based observational study comparing NBCSP-detected cases with those diagnosed outside screening found 92% vs 70% 5-year survival. The 5.5% stage IV rate in screen-detected cases vs 20.6% in non-screen cases represents the mechanism through which screening converts what would otherwise be expensive, palliative-intent care into lower-cost, curative-intent treatment.

**The economic translation:**
If all symptom-detected stage IV patients could instead be detected at stage I, the per-case saving across a treatment episode is approximately **$65,000–$150,000** (depending on the treatment regimen that would have been required). Across Australia's ~15,400 new CRC cases per year, even a modest stage shift — moving 5% of cases from stage IV to stage I — would save approximately **$50–75 million annually** in treatment costs, more than covering the entire NBCSP operating budget.

### 4. The Lancet Modelling: When Does Screening Pay for Itself?

The landmark long-term evaluation of the NBCSP (*Lancet Public Health*, 2017, Policy1-Bowel microsimulation model) remains the primary economic analysis underpinning the program's design. Its key projections:

- At **40% participation**, the NBCSP is projected to prevent 92,200 cancer cases and 59,000 deaths between 2015 and 2040
- The total annual health system cost of iFOBT screening, colonoscopy follow-up, and treatment combined was projected to **fall below the cost of treatment alone** (i.e., the program would cost the system nothing on net) within a decade of full rollout — that is, by approximately **2030**
- Cumulative savings in expenditure attributable to reduced treatment costs:
  - At 40% participation: **AUD$1.7 billion** (2030–2040, 2015 prices)
  - At 50% participation: **AUD$2.0 billion**
  - At 60% participation: **AUD$2.1 billion**

A separate cost-effectiveness analysis (*PLOS One*, 2020) found the program cost-effective at **$38,217 per life-year gained** at current (40%) participation, well within the implicit willingness-to-pay threshold of approximately $50,000–$64,000 per LYG used by MSAC and PBAC for health technology assessments.

The critical constraint is participation. The program's modelled savings were premised on 40–60% population uptake. Australia's current participation rate — approximately **44%** for the 50–74 cohort — leaves substantial value unrealised. Increasing participation from 44% to 60% is estimated to prevent a further **37,300 cancer cases** and **24,800 deaths** over the projection period.

### 5. The 45–49 Opt-In Expansion: An Equity and Cost Problem Combined

The July 2024 opt-in extension of the NBCSP to 45–49 year olds introduces a structural problem at the intersection of equity and economics. Opt-in participation systematically underrepresents the populations at highest CRC risk and lowest baseline access to preventive services:

- **First Nations Australians** participate in the NBCSP at substantially lower rates than the non-Indigenous population; the opt-in design amplifies this by removing automatic dispatch
- **Rural and remote populations** — who already face colonoscopy follow-up gaps — must proactively request a kit that will then require follow-up in colonoscopy-deficient settings
- **45–49 year olds as a cohort** are, by epidemiological evidence (Report 078), the fastest-growing early-onset CRC risk group in Australia; they are also among the least engaged with preventive health services

The AIHW reported that 236,421 kits were requested by 45–49 year olds in the period to the 2026 monitoring window, with a 56.2% return rate — but the denominator for Australia's approximately 1.5 million 45–49 year olds suggests that the opt-in program has reached fewer than 1 in 6 of its eligible population in its first two years. At a positivity rate of 4.7%, the program is finding real cancers — but in a population systematically biased toward health-engaged individuals who would most likely present symptomatically anyway.

The economic implication is that the opt-in architecture produces a diluted stage-shift benefit precisely in the age cohort where early-onset CRC is rising fastest. The stage IV cases that are being missed — in young First Nations Australians, in remote communities, in people who don't engage with GP services — are the ones that will cost $100,000+ to treat and are survivable at 90%+ if caught at stage I.

### 6. The PBS Component: Targeted Therapies Driving Cost Growth

The fastest-growing component of Australia's bowel cancer treatment expenditure is PBS-subsidised targeted therapy for stage IV disease. Key listings as of 2025:

- **Bevacizumab** (Avastin and biosimilars): first-line treatment for metastatic CRC; PBS-subsidised; up to $8,000/month with no defined treatment duration ceiling for responding patients
- **Cetuximab** (Erbitux): PBS-listed for RAS wild-type metastatic CRC, both first-line combination and second-line monotherapy
- **Encorafenib + cetuximab**: PBS-listed for BRAF V600E-variant metastatic CRC after prior therapy (a genotype-defined precision medicine listing)
- **FOLFOX/CAPOX chemotherapy components** (oxaliplatin, capecitabine): PBS-listed for adjuvant and palliative use

Bevacizumab biosimilars (Mvasi, Zirabev) entered the Australian market in 2020–21, delivering price disclosure savings but with slow uptake relative to originator. The PBS price for bevacizumab following biosimilar competition has declined, but total expenditure has continued to rise as the eligible stage IV population grows — driven by rising CRC incidence and extended treatment durations.

The structure of PBS subsidisation for stage IV CRC means that the health system absorbs the marginal cost of each additional month of treatment. This is appropriate — the drugs extend life and the patients are eligible — but it creates a strong fiscal incentive to invest upstream in prevention and early detection, where a single iFOBT kit ($30–40 in program delivery cost) can divert a patient from the palliative chemotherapy pathway entirely.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Program Pays for Itself, But Only If People Use It

The Lancet modelling's projected $1.7–2.1 billion in cumulative savings between 2030 and 2040 is conditional on maintaining participation rates of 40–60%. Australia's current 44% is barely at the threshold. Every percentage point of lost participation represents approximately 900 additional cancer cases and $50–70 million in foregone treatment cost savings over the projection period, based on modelling parameters. There is no policy mechanism currently in place that is projected to materially improve participation toward 60% — the population-wide SMS interventions and GP endorsement programmes tested in the SMARTscreen trial (a randomised controlled trial) produced modest but statistically significant uplift, but were not deployed at national scale.

### Tension 2: The Opt-In Expansion Undermines the Economic Argument at Its Most Vulnerable Point

The NBCSP's economic argument depends on reaching people before they develop symptoms — and specifically on reaching people who would not otherwise present until stage III or IV. The opt-in design for 45–49 year olds, by selecting for health-engaged individuals, systematically misses exactly the people whose stage IV diagnoses are most costly and least necessary. An auto-dispatch model for 45–49 year olds would cost more to administer (mailing kits to 1.5 million people biannually rather than receiving ~236,000 requests) but would realise substantially more of the program's economic benefit.

### Tension 3: PBS Cost Growth in Stage IV Is Not Addressable Through Screening Alone

Even at 60% NBCSP participation, Australia will continue to diagnose approximately 2,500–3,000 stage IV CRC cases annually. PBS-subsidised targeted therapies for these patients will continue to grow as new agents are listed (the pipeline includes KRAS G12C inhibitors and immunotherapy combinations for MSI-H metastatic CRC). The NBCSP's economic benefit offsets but does not eliminate the PBS cost trajectory for stage IV disease.

### Tension 4: The Cost Data Is Aging and Understates the True Burden

The most authoritative Australian stage-specific cost data comes from a 2021 study using 2020 AUD costs, and from a 2016 study on targeted therapy costs. Both substantially understate the current cost of treating metastatic CRC, which has increased with the addition of second- and third-line regimens not available in those study periods. AIHW's 2023–24 $1.85 billion expenditure figure is the best current system-level estimate, but a rigorous bottom-up per-stage cost analysis using current MBS and PBS data has not been published for the 2024–25 era.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **MBS items 32090 and 32093: the colonoscopy funding gap that undermines NBCSP.** Medicare pays a schedule fee of $556–$640 for diagnostic colonoscopy. The actual cost of a private colonoscopy is $1,500–$3,000. The gap falls on uninsured patients — exactly the demographic least likely to follow up a positive NBCSP result. A detailed examination of the colonoscopy funding architecture and what targeted MBS reform would need to look like.

2. **Bevacizumab biosimilar uptake and PBS price disclosure: is Australia realising the savings?** Bevacizumab biosimilars entered the market in 2020–21, but Australia's biosimilar uptake in oncology is substantially below OECD comparators. An examination of why, and what the foregone savings imply.

3. **Bowel cancer survival trends by stage and socioeconomic status: does the screening program reach people whose survival it would most improve?** AIHW and Cancer Australia data both show that bowel cancer survival is significantly lower for people in lower socioeconomic quintiles and for First Nations Australians. Are these the groups the NBCSP is reaching, or are the participation and follow-up gaps concentrated precisely where stage-shift benefit would be greatest?

4. **The colorectal liver metastasis pathway: surgery, ablation, MBS coverage, and out-of-pocket costs.** Approximately 20% of CRC patients present with synchronous liver metastases; another 25–30% develop metachronous liver metastases. Hepatic resection — potentially curative for selected patients — is an MBS-funded procedure, but the gap between MBS schedule fees and actual surgical costs in private settings generates substantial out-of-pocket liability. A 2025 Australian study examined these costs.

5. **The NBCSP participation gap: what does the evidence say about why 56% of eligible Australians don't screen?** Understanding the behavioural, structural, and systemic barriers to NBCSP participation is prerequisite to raising it. Australia's participation rate has barely moved in five years despite GP endorsement campaigns. What would it actually take to reach 60%?
