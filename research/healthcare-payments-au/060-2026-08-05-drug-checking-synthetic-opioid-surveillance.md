# Drug Checking as Public Health Surveillance: CanTEST, Synthetic Opioids, and Australia's Preparedness Gap

**Report 060 — 2026-08-05**

**Summary:** Australia has the beginnings of a sophisticated drug supply intelligence system — wastewater monitoring, a fixed-site drug checking service in the ACT, and nascent festival-based services in NSW — but it remains dangerously fragmented at the moment synthetic opioids (nitazenes, illicit fentanyl) are appearing with increasing frequency in the Australian illicit drug market. Nitazenes have been detected in every Australian state and territory through wastewater surveillance, 33 Australians have died from nitazene exposure since 2021, and Queensland in September 2025 became the first jurisdiction in the country to *ban* drug checking by legislation — the inverse of what the evidence recommends. This report examines how drug checking and drug supply surveillance are funded, what they have found, and why a patchwork of state-by-state approaches is structurally inadequate for a synthetic opioid threat that has no jurisdictional boundaries.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 059 documented how supply-restricting opioid policies can displace demand to illicit markets, and flagged illicit synthetic opioids — specifically fentanyl analogues and nitazenes — as a potential "next wave" risk for Australia. This report examines the detection infrastructure that would give Australia early warning of that wave, and the political economy that shapes whether it exists.

## Background

### What Drug Checking Services Do

Drug checking services allow people who possess illicit drugs to have their substances analysed — typically by a combination of immunoassay (rapid test strips), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and mass spectrometry — and receive information from a trained clinician about what is in the substance, what it is not, and what the risks are. The service is anonymous, non-judgmental, and, in Australia, free.

The public health rationale is not simply that individual users make safer choices (though the evidence shows that many do, with roughly 10% of service clients voluntarily discarding their drugs after testing). The more important value is epidemiological: drug checking services are, in effect, **real-time drug supply intelligence networks**. Every sample that is tested tells the service — and, when aggregated, public health authorities — something about what is circulating in the local drug market at that moment in time. Anomalous detections (unknown compounds, unusual purity levels, unexpected adulterants) can be escalated into public drug alerts within hours, well before the same signal would appear in emergency department presentations, toxicology reports, or post-mortem data.

This intelligence function is increasingly important as the global drug supply becomes more complex. The emergence of novel synthetic opioids — substances engineered in overseas laboratories, often by modifying the chemical structure of established opioids to evade scheduling — means that the drug market is no longer a relatively stable set of known substances. New compounds can appear with minimal warning, at potencies far exceeding anything previously encountered, and with effects that do not respond to standard overdose reversal (particularly the case with xylazine, which is not an opioid and cannot be reversed with naloxone).

## Key Findings

### 1. CanTEST: Australia's Model Service, Geographically Isolated

**CanTEST Health & Drug Checking Service**, which opened in the ACT on 21 July 2022, is Australia's first and only fixed-site, publicly funded drug checking service. It was the first of its kind in Australia — and remains, as of mid-2026, the only jurisdiction in Australia where a person can walk off the street on a weekday evening and have their drugs tested at no cost, with no identifying information required, by a trained health team.

The service is delivered by **Directions Health Services** in partnership with **CAHMA** (Canberra Alliance for Harm Minimisation and Advocacy) and **Pill Testing Australia**, and is funded entirely by **ACT Health**. It operates from the City Community Health Centre at 1 Moore Street, Civic, and is open:
- Thursday 3pm–6pm
- Friday 6pm–9pm

Cumulative impact data through mid-2026:
- **Over 6,000 samples tested** since July 2022
- **2,072 samples tested in Year 3** (July 2024 – July 2025) — the most productive year to date
- **>10% of samples voluntarily discarded** by clients following testing in the first 22 months — a concrete behavioural change driven by the service
- Year 3 findings showed **rising purity of MDMA, methamphetamine, and heroin**, and a **decrease in substitution and adulteration** across multiple drug classes — a changing supply picture that has direct public health significance

The critical finding for synthetic opioid risk: in 2025, CanTEST detected **N-pyrrolidino isotonitazene (NPI)**, a potent nitazene-class synthetic opioid, in a sample presented at the service — the first confirmed detection of this compound at an Australian drug checking service (Curtis et al., *Drug Testing and Analysis*, 2025). NPI is structurally related to isotonitazene, which has caused deaths in multiple jurisdictions. The detection triggered a public drug alert and demonstrated that CanTEST is functioning as an early-warning system — catching a novel substance *before* it appeared in hospital data.

**Funding sustainability:** In 2024, ACT Chief Minister Andrew Barr announced a further extension of CanTEST funding to **June 2027**, with an additional $1.8 million committed. The ACT Government's March 2025 cost-benefit analysis of harm reduction services explicitly found that CanTEST and other harm reduction services returned substantial savings to the healthcare system — with drug checking services preventing emergency department presentations and, in some cases, deaths.

The limitation is geographical: the ACT covers approximately 2.1% of Australia's land area and less than 1.5% of the national population. For the 98.5% of Australians who do not live in the ACT, CanTEST does not exist.

### 2. NSW: Festival Testing, No Fixed Site

In March 2025, NSW commenced a 12-month **mobile drug checking trial at music festivals**, the result of a recommendation from the 2024 NSW Drug Summit and the long-delayed implementation of a 2019 State Coroner recommendation following overdose deaths at festival events.

The trial is funded by **NSW Health** and delivered by a partnership of peer workers from the **National Alliance for Action on Alcohol and Drugs (NUAA)** and qualified health staff. The first site was at the Yours and Owls festival in Wollongong (March 2025), with Midnight Mafia at Sydney Olympic Park (May 2025) as the second.

The NSW trial is a meaningful step but has structural limitations:
- It covers **festivals only** — the highest-visibility but not the highest-harm context for drug use in NSW
- There is **no fixed site** in Sydney or elsewhere in NSW, meaning people who use drugs in non-festival settings have no access to checking
- The trial is **time-limited** (12 months), subject to a government evaluation, with no commitment to permanent funding
- Organisations including **Uniting** and harm reduction advocates have called for a permanent fixed-site service, but as of mid-2026 none has been established

The NSW drug checking model generates intelligence on the festival drug supply — useful, but a different population and different drugs than what circulates in the general community.

### 3. Queensland: The Counter-Model — A Legislative Ban

Queensland represents the sharpest policy divergence in Australia. The LNP Queensland Government, elected in late 2024, declined to renew funding for **CheQpoint**, the country's second fixed-site drug checking service, which had operated from Bowen Hills (Brisbane, April 2024) and later Gold Coast.

CheQpoint's Brisbane and Gold Coast sites **closed in April 2025**. In September 2025, the Crisafulli Government went further, passing legislation that **made drug checking operations illegal in Queensland** — the first and, as of mid-2026, only such ban in Australia.

Health Minister Tim Nicholls stated the government "does not support publicly or privately funded pill testing," citing a "zero-tolerance approach to illicit drugs." The RACGP responded directly: its April 2025 media release, titled "RACGP: Drug testing services save lives," stated that drug checking is an evidence-based harm reduction intervention and called on all jurisdictions to fund and legalise it.

The impact is material. Queensland's overdose death toll from illicit drugs now exceeds its road toll — a statistic Loop Australia CEO Cameron Francis cited at the time of the ban. With no drug checking service and no early-warning system for novel substances, Queensland public health authorities have no mechanism to detect a novel synthetic opioid in the drug supply until it begins killing people at a scale sufficient to appear in emergency department or morgue data — typically weeks to months after the substance has already spread.

### 4. The Synthetic Opioid Threat: What Wastewater and Seizure Data Reveal

The most alarming dimension of the drug checking gap is not what current services have found, but what the parallel wastewater surveillance system has revealed about what is already in the national drug supply.

The **National Wastewater Drug Monitoring Program (NWDMP)**, operated by the **Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission (ACIC)** in partnership with the University of Queensland and Adelaide University, is the world's most extensive national wastewater-based drug surveillance system. As of August 2025, it covers approximately **57% of the Australian population** (14.5 million people) across all states and territories, testing for 12 substances including — from 2024–25 — nitazenes and xylazine.

**Report 25 of the NWDMP** (2025) contained findings that should be read as a national alarm signal:
- **Nitazenes were detected in all Australian states and territories**, with a detection frequency fluctuating between **6% and 16%** across sampling collections throughout the year
- **Xylazine was detected at a 26% detection frequency** nationally — meaning more than one in four wastewater samples from Australian catchments showed evidence of xylazine consumption
- 26.8 tonnes of methylamphetamine, cocaine, heroin and MDMA were estimated to have been consumed in the 12 months to August 2025

These wastewater findings confirm that what has appeared at CanTEST (a single NPI detection) vastly underrepresents the scale of synthetic opioid presence in Australian drug markets. The NWDMP provides national coverage but at low temporal and geographic resolution — it tells you that nitazenes are present in a catchment, not which product they are in, not at what concentration, and not with enough specificity to generate a timely public drug alert.

The **mortality data** confirms real harm. As of the 2026 *Drug and Alcohol Review* scoping review (Mammoliti et al.):
- **33 confirmed nitazene deaths** in Australia since 2021 — 24 in Victoria, 7 in South Australia, 2 in NSW
- **11 nitazene emergency department toxicity presentations** since 2021
- **47 nitazene seizures** by the Australian Federal Police since July 2023
- Novel synthetic opioids were found in tablets misrepresented as MDMA, powders misrepresented as or mixed with cocaine, and powders sold as heroin — **cross-product contamination** that means the person who thinks they are taking a stimulant may unknowingly be ingesting an opioid 1,000 times more potent than morphine

The **xylazine finding** is particularly critical. Xylazine is a veterinary sedative increasingly used as a cutting agent in opioid products; its effects are not reversed by naloxone. A person overdosing on a xylazine-adulterated opioid product will not respond to standard emergency naloxone administration in the way they would for a pure opioid overdose. The person's life may depend on the attending clinician or bystander knowing that xylazine might be present — information that drug checking services could, in principle, provide, and that poison control and emergency services cannot currently deliver systematically.

### 5. The Funding Architecture: Harm Reduction's 1.6%

The fundamental structural problem is funding. Australia's drug policy budget is dominated by law enforcement. According to UNSW analysis, **harm reduction services receive approximately $89 million — 1.6% of total national drug policy expenditure**. The vast majority of spending flows to policing, border control, prosecution, and incarceration.

Drug checking services receive a fraction of the harm reduction budget — ACT's $1.8 million extension over three years is a rounding error in the context of national drug policy spending. There is no Commonwealth funding for drug checking. There is no Medicare Benefit Schedule item for drug checking. There is no national commissioning body for drug supply intelligence, and the NWDMP — the closest Australia has to a national surveillance network — operates through the ACIC (a law enforcement intelligence body) rather than a public health agency.

This creates a structural misalignment. The most valuable intelligence on the drug supply — the specific substances circulating at the street level, their purity, their adulterants — is captured by drug checking services that operate in two jurisdictions, on a charitable or small-government-grant model, with no systematic mechanism for aggregating findings into a national alert system. The wastewater program detects what is being consumed; the drug checking services detect what people are being sold. The two systems generate complementary intelligence and currently have no formal integration.

### 6. Naloxone and the Response Gap

Australia's response to synthetic opioid risk is partly dependent on naloxone availability — the emergency antidote for opioid overdose. In 2023, Schedule 3 naloxone became available without prescription through pharmacies, and community take-home naloxone programs exist in most jurisdictions. However:
- **Naloxone is not effective against xylazine**, which is increasingly detected in Australian drug samples alongside opioids
- **Naloxone may require multiple doses** for high-potency synthetic opioids like fentanyl analogues and nitazenes, and community members administering a standard 400mcg dose may not know they need more
- **Naloxone distribution** remains uneven — rural and regional areas where drug checking services are entirely absent also have lower naloxone distribution rates

The November 2025 withdrawal of Sublocade (long-acting injectable buprenorphine) from the Australian market, documented in Report 058, means that thousands of patients stable on LAIB are being transitioned to oral buprenorphine — a transition that may create vulnerability to illicit opioid use. If those patients encounter a product adulterated with a nitazene, the outcome could be fatal.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Law enforcement vs. harm reduction framing.** The Queensland ban illustrates that drug checking remains politically contested in Australia. The zero-tolerance framing — that providing drug checking signals approval of drug use — conflicts with the public health framing that people who are going to use drugs should have access to information that keeps them alive. The evidence base is firmly on the side of drug checking; the political economy is not uniformly aligned with evidence.

**2. Who should own national drug supply intelligence?** The NWDMP sits within ACIC, a law enforcement body. Drug checking services sit within health ministries (where they exist). There is no national agency responsible for integrating drug supply intelligence into real-time public health alerts. The closest equivalent internationally is the **EMCDDA Early Warning System** in the European Union — a model Australia does not currently have a domestic equivalent for.

**3. The Commonwealth gap.** The Commonwealth funds the NWDMP and has shown willingness to regulate (the 2025 strengthening of Commonwealth controls on synthetic opioids). But it has not funded drug checking directly, has not created a Medicare item for drug checking consultations, and has not established a national commissioning framework. Drug checking is effectively treated as a state/territory health matter, when the drug supply it is monitoring is national and increasingly international.

**4. The timeliness problem.** A novel synthetic opioid that appears in the drug supply today may take 6–8 weeks to appear in overdose mortality data. It may take 2–4 weeks to appear in emergency department presentations. A drug checking service can detect it in minutes and issue a public alert the same day. In a market where nitazenes are present in 6–16% of wastewater samples, this is not a theoretical problem — it is an active one.

**5. Scope of services.** CanTEST operates two sessions per week, for six hours total. NSW's festival service operates at specific events. There is no drug checking in Western Australia, South Australia (beyond research contexts), the Northern Territory, Tasmania, or regional Australia anywhere. Nitazenes have been detected everywhere.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — consultation underway as of mid-2026; whether it will include binding targets for drug checking coverage, national supply intelligence, and harm reduction funding; the political economy of strategy renewal
2. **Naloxone access and distribution reform** — moving beyond pharmacy availability to active community distribution; school, workplace, and transport hub placement; the adequacy of current supply for a synthetic opioid scenario
3. **First Nations communities and synthetic opioid risk** — the particular danger for remote communities with no drug checking access, lower naloxone distribution, and limited emergency response capacity if a synthetic opioid enters the supply
4. **Methamphetamine harm reduction in the absence of pharmacotherapy** — the continuing dominance of methylamphetamine in Australian drug markets (26.8T consumed in 2024–25); no OAT equivalent; stimulant harm reduction funding gaps
5. **Victoria's drug policy infrastructure** — as the state with the most nitazene deaths (24) and the longest-running supervised injecting facility, Victoria's emerging role as the de facto harm reduction state; its drug policy funding architecture and what other jurisdictions can learn
