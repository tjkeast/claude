# Prescribed Opioids, Benzodiazepines, and the Drug-Driving Asymmetry: Australia's Screening Gap and its Healthcare Cost

**One-line summary:** Australia's roadside drug-testing panels detect cannabis but not opioids or benzodiazepines, creating a legal paradox in which prescribed opioids — associated with 17.3% of fatal crashes in Victoria — carry lighter legal exposure than prescribed THC, even as reforming jurisdictions struggle to set an evidence-based threshold for either substance.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 116 mapped the diverging jurisdictional responses to medicinal cannabis and driving — noting that the NSW, ACT, and Victorian reform debates hinge on an explicit comparison with how opioids are already treated. Today's report examines that comparison in depth.

---

## Background

Every Australian state and territory operates a system of random roadside drug testing (RDT) using oral fluid saliva swabs. The technology screens for a narrow panel of substances: THC (cannabis), methamphetamine, MDMA, and — in most jurisdictions from 2025 — cocaine. Prescribed opioids (oxycodone, codeine, fentanyl, morphine, methadone, buprenorphine) and benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam, temazepam) are not included in the panel.

This exclusion is partly technical — oral fluid immunoassay strips are calibrated for specific substances — and partly historical. When roadside testing was introduced in Victoria in 2004 and progressively adopted by other states, the design rationale was to detect recreational illicit drugs. Opioids were framed as prescribed medicines, and the enforcement architecture assumed that the impairment offence was adequate to cover their misuse.

The result is a structural legal asymmetry. Patients prescribed cannabis face two types of offences: the presence offence (detectable THC, regardless of impairment) and the impairment offence (any drug that impairs driving ability). Patients prescribed opioids or benzodiazepines face only the impairment offence. Since impairment is far harder to establish at the roadside than mere presence, the two groups operate under materially different legal risks.

## Key Findings

### 1. Opioids Are Proportionally More Present in Fatal Crashes Than Cannabis

The most comprehensive Australian data is a Victorian study of fatal crashes covering 2006–2016. Among fatally injured drivers and motorcyclists who were toxicologically tested:

- Alcohol: most commonly detected drug
- **Opioids: detected in 17.3% of cases**
- **THC (cannabis): detected in 13.1% of cases**
- Antidepressants: 9.7%
- Benzodiazepines: 8.8%

Critically, the study found that the incidence of **strong opioids** (oxycodone, fentanyl, morphine, and methadone) increased at **6% per year** over the decade. These are the same PBS-listed and LSDP-funded medicines whose prescribing volumes have continued to grow through the 2020s.

For pedestrians and pedal cyclists — road users not protected by a vehicle — the opioid detection rate was even higher: 20.9%. The study notes that many of these fatal crashes involved older individuals with chronic pain conditions taking multiple sedating medicines concurrently.

This data predates the medicinal cannabis prescription boom (which began February 2016) and the explosion of SAS-B prescribing (177,762 approvals in 2024 alone). It represents a period when cannabis was almost entirely illegal. The current landscape — with an estimated 245,000 Australians holding active THC-containing prescriptions — means crash data from the next decade may look quite different. But based on available evidence, opioids are the prescription medicine most associated with fatal road trauma.

### 2. The Roadside Testing Gap Is Structural, Not Incidental

A Transport for NSW spokesperson has confirmed that roadside testing "only detects the presence of four illegal drugs" and that opioids are not included because they are "prescribed for pain management." The RACGP, in a submission to the NSW Legislative Council, stated it "does not hold a formal position nor make suggestion that NSW law enforcement should test for opiate and benzodiazepine impairment at the roadside."

This institutional reluctance reflects genuine practical challenges:
- Opioid oral fluid concentrations are highly variable across individuals
- Therapeutic concentrations overlap with potentially impairing concentrations
- Cross-reactivity with some immunoassay strips creates false positive risks
- The prescriber–patient relationship is regulated by the PBS and TGA, creating a separate enforcement framework

But the consequence is that enforcement of prescription drug impairment relies entirely on behavioural cues observed by police at a stop, rather than chemical detection. A driver who appears unimpaired but whose reaction time or cognitive function is materially degraded by opioids faces effectively no risk of detection unless they cause a crash.

### 3. Almost One in Four Drivers Ignores Prescription Drug Warning Labels

A CARRS-Q survey (Centre for Accident Research and Road Safety — Queensland) found that approximately **1 in 4 Australian drivers** continues to drive after taking prescription medicines that carry warning labels about driving. This includes opioids, benzodiazepines, antidepressants, and sedating antihistamines.

The same research body found that combined sedating drug use markedly exacerbates risk — alcohol combined with opioids or benzodiazepines represents the highest-risk profile in Australian crash data, yet this combination does not currently trigger any additional enforcement mechanism (beyond the alcohol breath test, which alcohol alone triggers).

### 4. The Legal Asymmetry Varies by Jurisdiction But Is Universal in Structure

As of October 2026, the jurisdictional picture is:

| Jurisdiction | THC presence offence | THC prescription defence | Opioid presence offence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tasmania | Yes (Poisons Act) | **Yes — statutory** | No |
| Victoria | Yes | Partial (courts may not cancel licence) | No |
| NSW | Yes | **Passed Sept 2026 — pending commencement** | No |
| ACT | Yes | Bill in committee, not passed | No |
| Queensland | Yes — increased penalties Dec 2026 | No | No |
| SA | Yes | No | No |
| WA | Yes | No | No |
| NT | Yes | No | No |

In every jurisdiction, opioids and benzodiazepines carry **no presence offence** — only the general impairment offence that applies to all substances and all drivers regardless of prescription status.

The ACT's explanatory material for its reform bill states this most plainly: "A person prescribed opioids or benzodiazepines, which can cause material driving impairment, is subject only to the impairment offence under section 24. Medicinal cannabis patients, by contrast, are additionally subject to the strict presence offence."

### 5. The Science Cannot Yet Support Any Presence Threshold — For Either Substance

The Australasian College of Road Safety (ACRS) has called for evidence-based reform and explicitly stated that "current scientific evidence is unable to provide a reliable THC presence threshold that can consistently distinguish safe from unsafe driving." This is the core problem that all jurisdictional reforms — including NSW's 50 ng/mL threshold — are attempting to solve before the science fully supports it.

The same logic applies to opioids. There is no validated plasma concentration of oxycodone, for example, that reliably separates safe from impaired driving. Crash risk depends on individual tolerance (chronic users develop pharmacological tolerance), concomitant substances, fatigue, and driving environment. Dr Michael White, a New Zealand researcher, has noted that roadside saliva tests would miss "many prescription opioids and benzodiazepines" even if expanded — because the relationship between oral fluid concentration and blood concentration is poorly characterised for many opioids.

### 6. The Healthcare Payments Externality: Cannabis Bans Drive Patients Back to the PBS

A significant but under-quantified healthcare cost flows from the driving restrictions on cannabis patients. Montu's 2025 survey of 6,000+ patients found that 91% reported increased anxiety around driving as a result of drug-driving laws. The Cannabis as Medicine Survey 2022–23 (CAMS-22, UNSW/NDARC) found 55.5% of respondents said driving laws deterred them from medicinal cannabis use.

When patients stop cannabis or choose not to start, they typically return to alternative medications funded through the PBS:
- **Opioids** (Schedule 8 controlled substances on the PBS, fully screened-free for driving purposes)
- **Benzodiazepines** (PBS-listed anxiolytics and hypnotics, also not in the roadside testing panel)
- **Antidepressants** and **antipsychotics** (PBS-funded, no presence screening)

The irony embedded in the healthcare payment system is that the medicines patients are redirected to are the same medicines most prominently associated with fatal crash data. Opioid prescriptions cost the PBS approximately $1.2 billion annually. Benzodiazepine prescriptions — despite being controlled — remain widespread, particularly in older Australians with chronic pain and anxiety.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The enforcement gap for high-volume opioid prescribers.** If opioid impairment is driving 17.3% of fatal crashes, why is no law enforcement jurisdiction actively investigating this? The answer appears to be a combination of political economy (opioids are mainstream medical prescriptions, cannabis is newer and more contested) and institutional inertia (the RDT architecture was designed around illicit drugs, and adding opioids requires new technology and legal frameworks).

**2. The commercial driver double standard.** Heavy vehicle operators are subject to fatigue management legislation, pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing under Australian Standard 4308, and "fitness for work" assessments. But AS 4308 specifically states it is designed for workplace use, not criminal prosecution — and it does not include opioids in its standard testing panel either. A long-haul truck driver on prescribed fentanyl for chronic back pain may lawfully drive if they pass the general impairment test, while a courier prescribed cannabis for the same condition faces a presence offence in most jurisdictions.

**3. The Productivity Commission interaction.** The Commission's National Injury Insurance Scheme and long-term disability cost projections do not currently model the road trauma cost attributable to prescription drug-impaired driving. This is a measurement gap that prevents evidence-based policy.

**4. The PBS as an unintended enabler.** The PBS heavily subsidises the opioid and benzodiazepine medicines that face the least driving enforcement scrutiny, while the SAS-B mechanism funds the medicines that face the most. The payment architecture thus inadvertently incentivises patients away from the (less-screened) non-PBS cannabis market toward the (more-screened, but also more road-risky) PBS-funded alternatives.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Prescriber duty of care and driving warnings** — what GPs, pharmacists, and specialists are legally and ethically required to tell patients about driving while on opioids, benzodiazepines, or cannabis, and whether MBS consultation item structures support this disclosure
2. **Commercial driver health assessments and opioid prescriptions** — how Austroads medical standards for commercial vehicle licences (Class MR and above) handle opioid prescribing, and whether NHVR enforcement reaches the clinical interface
3. **Road trauma cost modelling and the NIIS** — the National Injury Insurance Scheme's coverage of prescription drug-impaired crashes and the absence of population-level attributable fraction estimates
4. **Impairment-based detection technology** — the scientific state of play on whether field sobriety tests, eye-tracking, psychomotor apps, or new saliva biomarkers could provide legally defensible impairment evidence for opioids and cannabis alike
5. **The PBS–cannabis interaction** — a direct payment-system analysis: when cannabis laws deter prescriptions, what is the net PBS cost of patients reverting to opioids and benzodiazepines, and who bears it
