# CAR-T Therapy Delivery in Australia: Public-Only Access, Geographic Concentration, and the Manufacturing Advantage

**One-line summary:** Australia funds four CAR-T cell therapies through its public hospital system at no cost to eligible patients, but the treatment is only available in selected hospitals concentrated in three states — leaving patients in South Australia, Western Australia, Tasmania, the ACT, and the Northern Territory either travelling interstate or entirely without access.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 106 examined gene therapy payment models and identified the CAR-T Highly Specialised Therapy (HST) pathway as a distinct funding stream from the PBS — one that restricts access to public hospitals and creates a geographic equity problem with no equivalent in the broader PBS/MBS system.

---

## Background

CAR-T (chimeric antigen receptor T-cell) therapy is a form of living immunotherapy in which a patient's own T cells are extracted via apheresis, genetically engineered to target cancer antigens, manufactured in a specialist facility, and reinfused after a short lymphodepletion chemotherapy course. The vein-to-vein process — from cell collection to reinfusion — typically takes three to six weeks under current commercial manufacturing protocols.

For eligible patients with relapsed or refractory blood cancers, CAR-T offers response rates that earlier-line therapies cannot match. For some patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) or B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (B-ALL), it provides durable remissions previously unachievable.

The commercial list price for CAR-T products in Australia ranges from approximately AUD $465,000 to $750,000 for the drug alone, with total treatment costs — including hospital stay, conditioning chemotherapy, and monitoring — pushing the all-in cost above AUD $500,000 per patient episode. Without government subsidy, these therapies are inaccessible to virtually all Australians.

## Key Findings

### 1. Four Publicly Funded CAR-T Therapies — But Only in Public Hospitals

As of July 2026, four CAR-T products are funded through Australia's Highly Specialised Therapies (HST) program under the National Health Reform Agreement (NHRA):

| Product | Indication | Funded since |
|---|---|---|
| **Kymriah** (tisagenlecleucel) | r/r B-ALL aged ≤25; r/r adult DLBCL | 2019 |
| **Yescarta** (axicabtagene ciloleucel) | r/r adult large B-cell lymphoma (2nd/3rd line) | 2021 |
| **Tecartus** (brexucabtagene autoleucel) | r/r mantle cell lymphoma; r/r B-ALL | 2024 |
| **Carvykti** (ciltacabtagene autoleucel) | r/r multiple myeloma (5th+ line) | 2025 |

The critical structural feature is the funding vehicle: unlike PBS-listed medicines, which are subsidised at retail pharmacies and in outpatient settings, CAR-T products are funded through the HST program and delivered exclusively in designated public hospital sites. Patients treated in private hospitals — or who seek access through private health insurance — receive no subsidy for the therapeutic agent. PHI covers the hospitalisation component, but not a drug costing half a million dollars. The full commercial cost falls entirely on the patient.

The HST program is jointly funded between the Commonwealth and state/territory governments. High-cost HSTs are defined as therapies with an average annual treatment cost exceeding $200,000 per patient, as determined by MSAC or PBAC in consultation with the Independent Hospital Pricing Authority (IHACPA). The joint funding model means that each jurisdiction must invest in the infrastructure to deliver these therapies — which has produced the uneven geographic rollout described below.

### 2. Geographic Distribution: Three States, ~11 Sites, Three Jurisdictions Without Access

CAR-T therapy is currently available in:

- **Victoria:** Alfred Health (Melbourne), Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (Melbourne), The Royal Children's Hospital (Melbourne) — the most developed national cluster
- **New South Wales:** Royal Prince Alfred Hospital (Sydney), Westmead Hospital (Western Sydney), Sydney Children's Hospitals Network
- **Queensland:** Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital, Princess Alexandra Hospital, Townsville Hospital and Health Service

**South Australia** will establish a dedicated service at the Royal Adelaide Hospital from mid-2026, funded by $2.8 million in the 2025-26 state budget — initially treating approximately four patients in the first year and scaling up over time. Around 30 South Australians per year are currently forced to relocate or travel interstate, spending roughly two months away from home, their community, and their support networks.

**Western Australia** has a treatment site listed (Fiona Stanley Fremantle Hospitals Group), but no established large-volume commercial programme as of mid-2026. A clinical trial is expected to begin in 2026 through a partnership between Royal Perth Hospital and Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, focused on building local manufacturing capability.

**Tasmania, Northern Territory, and the Australian Capital Territory** have no CAR-T treatment centres. Patients in these jurisdictions face interstate relocation as their only option for publicly funded access. Travel and accommodation support is available, but the requirement to leave home for two months during an already serious illness is a substantial non-financial barrier.

The Leukaemia Foundation has explicitly called for CAR-T treatment centres in every Australian state and territory as the minimum geographic equity standard.

### 3. The Manufacturing Milestone: Peter MacCallum

The single most important recent infrastructure development for Australian CAR-T delivery is the TGA approval of Cell Therapies Pty Ltd at Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre as the first and only approved commercial CAR-T manufacturing site in Australia, granted in February 2021. This followed a $105 million facility investment.

Before this approval, Australian patients receiving Kymriah had their T cells extracted, cryopreserved, and shipped to Novartis's facility in Morris Plains, New Jersey — a round trip that added weeks to the vein-to-vein timeline and introduced cold-chain logistical risk. Onshore manufacturing reduces the turnaround time, eliminates international shipping delays, and keeps the entire process within Australian regulatory oversight.

The practical significance is substantial: for patients with fast-progressing disease, every week of manufacturing delay is a week of potential clinical deterioration that can render a patient ineligible for or unable to tolerate infusion by the time the product is ready. Shorter vein-to-vein time has been shown in international literature to correlate with improved patient selection and outcomes.

The limitation of the Peter MacCallum facility is that it is a single site in Melbourne, producing product for the entire national network. WA's initiative to develop local manufacturing capability reflects a recognition that geographic distribution of manufacturing capacity — not just clinical delivery — matters for resilience and turnaround times.

### 4. The Private Hospital Exclusion: A Structural Access Barrier

The HST funding model creates a hard binary: public hospital patients with the right diagnosis at the right site pay nothing for CAR-T therapy; everyone else faces the full commercial cost of AUD $500,000–$750,000.

This has several consequences. First, haematologists at private hospitals — even large, well-resourced ones — cannot offer this therapy to their patients without directing them to the public system. This disrupts established patient-clinician relationships at a critical juncture. Second, Epworth HealthCare, a major Victorian private hospital group, has expressed interest in establishing CAR-T delivery capacity but cannot bill for HST-funded products in the private sector. Third, the geography of private haematology practice does not align with the geography of public CAR-T centres: many haematologists work across both systems, but the therapy is only accessible in one.

There is no equivalent exclusion in the PBS: PBS-listed medicines are available at private-hospital-contracted pharmacies. The HST architecture inherits from the hospital funding model, which has always had stronger Commonwealth-state cost-sharing mechanisms in the public sector.

### 5. Patient Numbers and Scale

The eligible populations for individual CAR-T indications are small. The Australian DLBCL population eligible for second-line Kymriah or Yescarta is estimated in the hundreds nationally per year, not thousands. The myeloma population eligible for Carvykti (fifth-line, heavily pre-treated) is smaller still. Total national CAR-T infusions likely number in the low hundreds annually across all indications.

This small patient volume is both a reason why per-patient costs are so high (no economies of scale in autologous manufacturing) and a reason why geographic access remains so constrained: sites need to treat sufficient patients to maintain staff competency, institutional accreditation, and the specialised infrastructure (apheresis capacity, ICU backup, cytokine release syndrome management protocols) that CAR-T delivery requires.

### 6. Comparison: NHS England's Networked Commissioning Model

NHS England has established approximately 25–30 designated specialist centres for CAR-T therapy, with NHS Blood and Transplant managing the national apheresis collection network. The more distributed centre model — supported by a national blood service infrastructure that already runs apheresis at many sites — allows more patients to access treatment within their region. Manufacturing remains outsourced to commercial partners, with vein-to-vein times of four to six weeks, and NHS Blood and Transplant opened a Clinical Biotechnology Centre in Bristol in March 2023 to build domestic viral vector and cell therapy manufacturing capacity.

Australia's approach is more concentrated: fewer centres, single manufacturing site, stronger geographic inequality. The NHS model demonstrates that a more distributed centre network is operationally feasible, but requires commissioning frameworks, apheresis service infrastructure, and staff training pipelines that Australia is only beginning to build.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**The volume-quality trade-off.** CAR-T therapy requires specialised expertise in cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity (ICANS) management. Distributing treatment to lower-volume centres could maintain geographic equity but raise safety risks if centres do not treat enough patients to maintain competency. The optimal minimum annual volume per site has not been established in Australian policy.

**Who bears the cost of the two-month relocation?** Travel and accommodation support exists but is not standardised. The cost to a patient's employer, family, and mental health of relocating interstate for two months is not captured in any formal benefit-cost analysis of the HST program.

**The private hospital exclusion and wait-time interaction.** Public CAR-T centres face capacity constraints. If a patient is eligible but their local public centre has a long wait, they cannot opt for private access — there is no funded private pathway. The result is a queue with no safety valve.

**Manufacturing concentration risk.** A single TGA-approved commercial manufacturing site — at one Melbourne hospital — represents a single point of failure for the national supply chain. The resilience argument for distributed manufacturing is not only about equity but about supply security.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **NZ vs Australia rare disease access** — PHARMAC's fixed-budget model has not funded any commercial CAR-T therapy; examining whether New Zealand's more systematic access failures for expensive therapies represent a different kind of inequity than Australia's geographic concentration
2. **PBAC ICER threshold reform** — the implicit ~AU$50,000/QALY threshold is under pressure from multiple directions and the gene therapy/CAR-T pipeline is forcing the debate into the open
3. **Rare disease registries and real-world evidence** — outcomes-based agreements for CAR-T and gene therapies depend on data infrastructure that largely does not exist; examining the national rare disease registry policy gap
4. **Cytokine release syndrome and ICANS: the ICU economics of CAR-T** — the hospital cost of managing severe adverse events represents a substantial and poorly-quantified portion of the true per-patient cost; examining whether the HST price captures this
5. **Allogeneic CAR-T (off-the-shelf)** — manufacturing at scale from donor cells; if allogeneic products reach market, the geographic equity problem changes fundamentally; what does Australia's regulatory and funding pathway look like for allogeneic CAR-T
