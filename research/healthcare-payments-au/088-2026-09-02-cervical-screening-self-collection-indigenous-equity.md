# Cervical Screening's Self-Collection Revolution: A Model for Indigenous Equity?

**Report 088 — 2026-09-02**

**Summary:** Australia's National Cervical Screening Program underwent a radical structural redesign in 2017 — replacing 2-yearly Pap smears with 5-yearly HPV testing — and again in 2022 when self-collection became universally available to all participants. Together, these changes have materially improved cervical screening participation among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women: self-collection now accounts for 56% of screens among recently screened First Nations participants, up from 4% before the 2022 policy change. Yet a March 2026 modelling study finds cervical cancer elimination among Indigenous women will not occur until 2047 — 12 years behind the national 2035 target — and the parallel NBCSP bowel screening program, which faces structurally identical access barriers, has not applied the same logic of redesign.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 087 documented that First Nations Australians participate in the NBCSP bowel screening program at significantly lower rates, face worse follow-up, and have lower colorectal cancer survival — and that the alternative access model (kit distribution through primary health services) has not been scaled or funded to its full potential. Cervical screening confronted the same equity problem and solved it differently.

---

## Background

Cancer screening programs can be designed in ways that embed or counteract health inequities. Australia's two largest population cancer screening programs — cervical screening and bowel cancer screening — target overlapping populations, face similar structural access barriers in First Nations communities, and are administered through similar mechanisms (centralised registers, Medicare linkage, state/territory delivery). But they have responded to evidence of Indigenous equity failure in fundamentally different ways.

The National Cervical Screening Program (NCSP) has been structurally redesigned twice since 2017: first, a whole-program renewal that replaced the underlying test technology and screening interval; and second, a 2022 policy change that removed the requirement for clinician-collected sampling as the default, making self-collection universally available. The National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP) has added an "alternative access" pathway as an optional add-on to an otherwise unchanged mailout model.

This report examines what the cervical screening redesign achieved for Indigenous equity, where the gaps remain, and what lessons the NBCSP could draw from a decade of cervical equity policy.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Disease Burden: A More Extreme Disparity Than Bowel Cancer

Cervical cancer in Australia is a disease that disproportionately kills Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women. The AIHW NCSP Monitoring Report 2025 documents:

- **Age-adjusted incidence (2017–2021):** 2.1 times higher for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women aged 25–74 than non-Indigenous women
- **Age-adjusted mortality (2019–2023):** 2.8–3 times higher for Indigenous women than non-Indigenous women
- **High-grade abnormalities detected:** 13 per 1,000 Indigenous participants screened (2024) — a marker of underlying disease burden

In the most recent 5-year cohort, 194 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women were diagnosed with cervical cancer and 71 died — in a population where, with adequate screening, the vast majority of these cases are theoretically preventable.

For context, cervical cancer is classified by the WHO as eliminable — defined as an incidence rate below 4 per 100,000 women. Australia overall is approaching this threshold. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women, incidence remains far above it. The gap is not genetic; it is structural.

### 2. The 2017 Renewal: Structural Redesign, Not Just Access Improvements

Unlike the NBCSP's incremental approach, the NCSP's 2017 renewal involved a fundamental reconceptualisation of the program:

| Element | Pre-2017 | Post-2017 |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| Test type | Pap smear (cytology) | HPV test (molecular) |
| Screening interval | Every 2 years | Every 5 years |
| Starting age | 18 | 25 |
| Ending age | 69 | 74 |
| Self-collection | Not available | Available for under-screened women ≥30 |
| Detection improvement | Baseline | 90 additional cancers found in first 2 years that Pap would have missed |

The HPV-primary test is more sensitive than cytology — within the first two years of the new program, 90 cancers were detected that would not have been identified by Pap smear. Moving to 5-yearly testing reduced the burden on women who were adequately screened while focusing program energy on reaching those who were not. The longer interval is clinically safe precisely because HPV detection has a longer lead time before cancer progression than cytology detected cell changes.

For First Nations women specifically, the 5-yearly interval reduced the opportunity for adverse encounters with the health system — a clinically relevant equity factor — while the initial introduction of self-collection for under-screened women addressed one of the most documented barriers to participation: the requirement for a clinician-collected speculum examination.

### 3. July 2022: Universal Self-Collection Changes the Game

The defining shift for First Nations equity in cervical screening came on **1 July 2022**, when self-collection was extended from a limited pathway (only for women over 30 who declined clinician sampling) to a universal option — available to all NCSP-eligible participants, at any clinical encounter, as an equal alternative to clinician-collected sampling.

The impact was immediate and dramatic:

- **Self-collection uptake among recently screened First Nations participants:** rose from **4%** (pre-2022) to **56%** by late 2024
- **Of Indigenous women offered the choice:** 67% chose self-collection over clinician collection
- **Across all women in Victoria** (the most granular uptake data, from a BMC Medicine study, 2025): self-collection uptake grew exponentially in the 2 years since universal availability

These figures reflect a phenomenon that health systems researchers have consistently observed: when a barrier is removed structurally — by making the less invasive, more private option the default choice rather than an exception requiring clinical negotiation — uptake rises rapidly in populations for whom the original barrier was most severe.

The experiences reported by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women about self-collection are consistent across qualitative studies: it "avoided the shame, embarrassment and discomfort surrounding the clinician-collected sample." In many Aboriginal communities, bodily privacy norms make the speculum examination a significant deterrent to screening. Self-collection removes this deterrent at the point of care rather than asking women to overcome it.

### 4. Current Participation: Improvement, But Still Short of Elimination Target

Despite this progress, participation remains below the level needed to drive cervical cancer toward elimination in First Nations communities:

- **As at June 2024:** 47–51% of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander regular female clients of Indigenous-specific primary health-care services aged 25–74 had been screened in the previous 5 years
- **Among ACCHOs specifically (June 2023):** 42% screening coverage — below both the national average and the program's equity target

For context, the WHO cervical cancer elimination 90-70-90 target requires 70% of women screened with a high-sensitivity test by age 35, and again by 45. Australia overall is approaching this threshold. First Nations participation at 42–51% is well below it.

The **PREVENT Project** — a Lancet Public Health implementation study (2025) — tested a three-component model in six remote Kimberley Aboriginal communities: self-collection + point-of-care HPV testing + same-day colposcopy. Of 303 women directly invited, 108 (36%) participated during a 4-month implementation period. Of these, 21% tested positive for oncogenic HPV, and 95% of positive testers completed same-day colposcopic assessment — meaning almost all women with high-risk HPV received a diagnostic examination in the same encounter.

This is the critical insight: the follow-up problem — a positive screen requiring a second appointment, potentially involving travel to a major centre — was dissolved by co-locating screening and diagnostic assessment in a single community visit. The model, while resource-intensive, is the logical extension of the self-collection principle: make the entire screening pathway available where people are, rather than requiring people to come to the pathway.

### 5. The Equity Gap That Remains: Modelling the 2047 Problem

A landmark modelling study published in **The Lancet Public Health (2026)** — by researchers at the Australian National University and the University of Sydney — provided the most rigorous projection to date of the cervical cancer elimination timeline for Indigenous women.

Key findings:

- **Australia overall:** projected to achieve elimination (incidence <4/100,000) by approximately **2026–2028** under existing program trajectories
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women:** projected elimination **not until 2047** under current trajectories — a **12–21 year gap** depending on the assumption set
- **A "screening blitz" scenario** — combining intensive self-collection outreach, community-based point-of-care testing, and prioritised colposcopy access — could close the gap to within **a generation** of the national target

The study identified the specific levers: vaccination coverage, screening participation, and timely follow-up. For First Nations women, the binding constraint is not HPV vaccination (coverage has improved substantially through school-based programs) — it is screening participation and, for those who screen positive, timely follow-up colposcopy.

### 6. Government Investment: $48.2 Million Over Four Years

In **November 2023**, the Albanese government launched the **National Strategy for the Elimination of Cervical Cancer in Australia**, backed by a **$48.2 million** investment over four years, with allocations specifically targeting Indigenous equity:

- **$12.7 million** to the National Aboriginal Community-Controlled Health Organisation (NACCHO) to improve access for First Nations women, enabling screening closer to home in local communities
- **$8.3 million** for innovative screening models — including self-collection community models, point-of-care testing, and priority population programs

The investment is significant and clearly linked to equity outcomes. Its adequacy in the face of a 2047 elimination projection is, however, an open question — particularly given the geographic scale of the task in very remote communities.

### 7. The 'Screen Your Way' Program: Co-Design in Practice

The **Screen Your Way** program (2024–2026, running across Queensland, NSW, and the Northern Territory) is the most systematic current attempt to embed HPV self-collection into First Nations-specific primary health services through co-design. The program embeds research partnerships within ACCHOs and community-controlled health organisations, conducts a co-design phase tailored to local needs before implementation, and runs a 12-month implementation period to assess whether community-driven models can sustainably increase cervical screening participation.

This approach directly addresses one of the key lessons from the NBCSP experience (and from the NCSP's own early post-2022 implementation data): nationally available does not mean locally accessible. The self-collection option was made available in July 2022, but initial implementation was characterised by "variable knowledge and adoption among the primary care workforce resulting in inconsistent promotion." Making the pathway available at the national policy level requires active investment in local clinical practice change to translate into participation.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Structural Redesign vs. Add-On Pathways
The NCSP redesigned its core test, its interval, and its delivery mechanism. The NBCSP added an optional alternative access pathway on top of an unchanged mailout infrastructure. The evidence from cervical screening suggests that structural redesign — where the equity-enabling model becomes the default, not the exception — produces larger and faster improvements than optional add-ons. The NBCSP's alternative access model has been available since 2022. By 2024, Indigenous participation has risen by approximately 7 percentage points since 2018 — a meaningful but not transformative change. The equivalent question for bowel screening is: what would it look like to make community-delivered kit distribution the default delivery mechanism for First Nations Australians, rather than an opt-in for services willing to place bulk orders?

### Tension 2: The Follow-Up Problem Is the Same in Both Programs
The PREVENT Project's key innovation — same-day colposcopy — addresses the cervical screening follow-up problem in remote communities. The NBCSP bowel screening follow-up problem (positive iFOBT → colonoscopy) is analogous: a positive result in a remote community requires access to a colonoscopy service typically available only in a regional or metropolitan centre. Outreach colonoscopy programs exist but are not systematically funded through the NBCSP. The cervical screening experience suggests that follow-up access is at least as important as initial participation — and that co-location of screening and diagnostic assessment, even if resource-intensive, can dramatically improve the yield of screening investment.

### Tension 3: Self-Collection Works, But Implementation Gaps Persist
The rapid post-2022 uptake of self-collection among Indigenous women demonstrates that the underlying demand exists. The problem has been and remains the consistency of its promotion. Qualitative and quantitative data both show that a substantial proportion of Indigenous women who screen at ACCHOs are still not being offered the self-collection choice. Implementation is not complete just because policy has changed; it requires sustained clinical practice change at the provider level.

### Tension 4: The Modelling Gap — When Do We Act?
The 2047 projection creates a policy problem: a 21-year equity gap from today's vantage point looks abstract. The same gap looked abstract from 2017. Australia has been running a renewed cervical screening program for nearly a decade, has invested $48.2 million, and is still not on a trajectory to eliminate the disease in First Nations communities within the target generation. This is not an argument against the program — it is an argument for higher urgency and a different resource calculus. The question is whether the policy system can respond to a projected 2047 date with the same urgency it would respond to an event that is happening now.

### Tension 5: The NBCSP as a Comparison Case
The NBCSP and NCSP face structurally similar barriers in Indigenous communities but have received very different levels of redesign investment targeted at equity. The National Strategy for Cervical Cancer Elimination is explicit and funded. The NBCSP has no equivalent equity strategy with comparable investment. Report 087 documented that the NBCSP has no explicit Indigenous participation target and no accountability mechanism for follow-up rates. The cervical screening comparison raises the question: why does the NBCSP not have an equivalent strategic framework, given the comparable — and for men, greater — burden of bowel cancer in First Nations communities?

1. **The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025 and its quality governance architecture.** The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care published a major revision in 2025 — the first since 2018. What does it say about equity, follow-up, and accountability for remote access? (Flagged in Report 087 as thread 1.)

2. **PHI and colonoscopy out-of-pocket costs: the gap-insurance question for NBCSP follow-up.** NBCSP-positive patients face significant anaesthetic gaps, specialist gaps, and day hospital facility fees in the private sector. How does this create a second-order equity barrier within the follow-up pipeline?

3. **The National Cancer Plan 2023–2033 equity commitments for First Nations cancer care.** Does the plan create the accountability framework for bowel screening that the National Cervical Cancer Elimination Strategy created for cervical screening? Are they materially different in ambition and resourcing?

4. **HPV vaccination coverage among Indigenous youth and the future cervical cancer prevention pipeline.** Vaccination is the upstream prevention lever for cervical cancer. What is current coverage among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adolescents, and is it on a trajectory to deliver the generation-gap closure the modelling studies require?

5. **Outreach colonoscopy services in the NT and Queensland: state-funded programs, capacity, and clinical outcomes.** Some state and territory governments fund outreach gastroenterology services to bring diagnostic colonoscopy to remote communities — the equivalent of the PREVENT Project's same-day colposcopy model. What is their scope and clinical yield?
