# Adenomyosis: Endometriosis' Sibling Condition and Australia's Women's Health Policy Blind Spot

**One-line summary:** Adenomyosis — a chronic uterine condition affecting an estimated 10–28% of women — shares the diagnostic delays, treatment barriers, and funding gaps of endometriosis, but receives no dedicated national action plan, no condition-specific clinic network, and no standalone MBS items, despite Australia investing $127 million in endometriosis since 2018.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 122 documented Australia's network of 33 Endometriosis and Pelvic Pain Clinics and the broader $127 million National Action Plan for Endometriosis. It noted that adenomyosis — a condition with comparable disease burden and overlapping patient population — has received "far less policy attention, no dedicated MBS items, and no equivalent clinic network." Today's report examines that gap in detail.

---

## Background

Adenomyosis is a chronic gynaecological condition in which endometrial-like tissue — the tissue that normally lines the uterus — grows within and through the muscular wall of the uterus (the myometrium). Unlike endometriosis, where similar tissue implants outside the uterus entirely, adenomyosis is contained within the uterine structure itself. The result is a thickened, enlarged, and often painful uterus, with heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), severe dysmenorrhoea (painful periods), and chronic pelvic pain as the dominant clinical features.

The condition is frequently described as endometriosis' "sibling" or "evil twin" because the conditions share pathological tissue type, overlapping symptom profiles, hormonal dependency, and many of the same treatment options. Crucially, they often co-occur: estimates suggest that somewhere between 20% and 80% of women with surgically confirmed endometriosis also have adenomyosis, depending on the diagnostic method used — a co-occurrence rate that means many women in the existing EPPC network almost certainly carry an adenomyosis diagnosis as well. Yet while endometriosis has received sustained national policy attention since 2018, adenomyosis has been largely treated as a footnote.

One major reason for adenomyosis' neglect is that, for most of modern medicine's history, definitive diagnosis required histological examination of the uterine wall — which meant the condition could only be confirmed after hysterectomy. This deeply problematic diagnostic architecture — where the only certain test is the most irreversible treatment — both delayed recognition of the condition's burden and created a perverse situation: women who had not yet decided to forgo future fertility could not, strictly speaking, receive a confirmed diagnosis.

That architecture has now changed, but the policy response has not yet caught up.

## Key Findings

### 1. Prevalence: Staggering Uncertainty, Substantial Burden

The wide uncertainty in adenomyosis prevalence estimates — ranging from 10% to 28% of women globally, and some Australian advocacy estimates suggesting 1 in 5 people assigned female at birth — reflects a fundamental measurement problem: until recently, population prevalence could only be estimated from hysterectomy case series, which introduce severe selection bias (older women, heavier disease, those who had exhausted other options).

A November 2025 global systematic review and meta-analysis published in *BMC Medicine* attempted to produce a pooled prevalence estimate across imaging-based and surgical-based studies, but acknowledged that wide methodological heterogeneity prevented a single reliable number. The most commonly cited Australian estimate — from advocacy and clinical sources — is around 1 in 5 women of reproductive age, which would suggest roughly 1.4 million Australians. However, a figure derived from imaging-based studies in reproductive populations (the most reliable modern approach) tends to cluster at the lower end, around 10–15%.

What is not in doubt is that adenomyosis is associated with:
- **Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB)**, with some estimates suggesting adenomyosis accounts for 20–30% of HMB presentations in primary care
- **Subfertility and pregnancy loss**: a 2025 meta-analysis found adenomyosis reduces clinical pregnancy rates by 31–43% in assisted reproduction, reduces live birth rates by up to 55%, and increases miscarriage risk (OR 2.1–3.4)
- **Severe chronic pelvic pain and dysmenorrhoea**, overlapping substantially with endometriosis presentations
- **Reduced quality of life** comparable in magnitude to endometriosis, though there is no Australian-specific economic cost-of-illness study equivalent to Armour et al.'s $9.7 billion endometriosis estimate

The AIHW's endometriosis reporting notes that "uterine endometriosis" (which includes adenomyosis) accounted for approximately 18% of principal diagnoses in endometriosis-related hospitalisations in 2021–22. A 2026 AIHW media release on endometriosis hospitalisations notes that around two-thirds of abdominal hysterectomies linked to endometriosis in 2024–25 listed "uterine endometriosis, including adenomyosis" as the principal diagnosis — approximately 2,100 hysterectomies per year on that basis, though this is a partial picture.

### 2. The Diagnostic Revolution That Medicare Has Not Caught Up With

Until approximately a decade ago, definitive adenomyosis diagnosis was made histologically: the pathologist confirmed the presence of endometrial glands and stroma within the myometrium from a hysterectomy specimen. This meant that the condition was, in practice, an incidental finding in older women undergoing hysterectomy for other indications — or a diagnosis confirmed only after the most definitive surgical treatment had already been performed.

The development of high-resolution transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) and MRI has fundamentally changed this. Multiple systematic reviews and expert working groups have now validated non-invasive ultrasound diagnosis. The **RANZCOG Australian Living Evidence Guideline for Endometriosis** (updated March 2026) includes a conditional recommendation — graded on low-certainty evidence — that TVUS be offered as first-line investigation when adenomyosis is suspected, with pelvic MRI as second-line when TVUS is unavailable or when deep infiltrating disease is suspected.

An October 2026 article in *Australian Prescriber* confirmed that the updated guideline "supports clinical diagnosis and early management in primary care without waiting for surgical confirmation" — a meaningful shift in clinical culture that prioritises earlier intervention and fertility-preserving options.

The key funding development is **MBS item 55080**, introduced 1 November 2025, which provides a dedicated Medicare rebate for specialised pelvic ultrasound for complex gynaecological conditions including adenomyosis and deep infiltrating endometriosis. The schedule fee is **$255**, requires a minimum 30 minutes of scanning time, and cannot be billed on the same day as another pelvic ultrasound item. Prior to this, patients had to use the generic pelvic ultrasound item 55065, which did not adequately reimburse the expertise and time required.

**The gap that remains:** Item 55080 addresses sonography. It does not address pelvic MRI for adenomyosis. Medicare funds MRI for endometriosis investigation in specific circumstances related to severe disease or infertility work-up, but there is no dedicated MRI item for adenomyosis as a standalone indication. Given that MRI is often required to characterise diffuse versus focal disease — a distinction that directly determines surgical planning and fertility counselling — this represents a meaningful diagnostic funding gap. Private pelvic MRI costs $300–$700 out of pocket without Medicare rebate.

### 3. Treatment: The PBS Architecture Was Not Designed for Adenomyosis

The standard pharmacological management of adenomyosis in Australia relies on:

**1. Levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS, Mirena):** The most widely used and best-evidenced first-line medical treatment. The LNG-IUS reduces heavy menstrual bleeding by 70–90% and is associated with significant pain relief. It is the treatment most recommended by the RANZCOG guideline as first-line. Critically for the fertility question, it is reversible. **The PBS restriction for Mirena, however, is for "idiopathic menorrhagia" and heavy menstrual bleeding — not adenomyosis specifically.** A patient with adenomyosis who presents to their GP may access Mirena at the PBS co-payment under the HMB restriction, but this is a workaround rather than an explicit policy recognition. There is no PBS restriction specifically endorsing Mirena for adenomyosis management.

**2. Combined oral contraceptives and progestin-only therapies:** Continuous-use OCP is widely used for symptomatic management. These are listed on PBS under general hormonal contraception restrictions, not adenomyosis indications.

**3. GnRH analogues (leuprorelin, goserelin):** These are PBS-listed for endometriosis but not specifically for adenomyosis. A clinician may prescribe them for adenomyosis under the endometriosis restriction if the conditions co-exist, but a woman with adenomyosis alone may face off-label prescription or private expense. Leuprorelin injections cost $200–$400 per month privately.

**4. Dienogest:** As discussed in Report 122, Ryeqo (relugolix/estradiol/norethisterone) has been PBS-listed from May 2025, and dienogest (Visanne) is available privately at approximately $90–$150 per month. Neither carries a PBS restriction specifically for adenomyosis.

**5. GnRH antagonists (Ryeqo, relugolix combination therapy):** PBS-listed as of May 2025, primarily for endometriosis. The same access pathway issue applies for women who have adenomyosis without confirmed co-occurring endometriosis.

The pattern across all pharmacological treatments is consistent: the PBS architecture for hormonal management was built for endometriosis (and contraception), and adenomyosis sits in a regulatory grey zone, accessing treatments via overlapping indications rather than direct recognition.

### 4. The Hysterectomy Default and Its Costs

Hysterectomy — surgical removal of the uterus — remains the only definitive cure for adenomyosis. For women who have completed their families and exhausted medical management, it is a well-evidenced, highly effective intervention with high patient satisfaction rates. Australia performs approximately 27,000–30,000 hysterectomies per year (across all indications), with heavy menstrual bleeding (including adenomyosis) among the top indications.

The funding architecture for hysterectomy is standard: it is a surgical procedure covered under MBS item numbers for abdominal and laparoscopic hysterectomy (items 35673, 35677, 35684 etc.) with private health insurance hospital cover enabling private admission. But access to hysterectomy — particularly through the public system — reflects the broader problems of surgical wait times.

Anecdotal data from patient forums and ACT parliamentary submissions paint a consistent picture: category 3 (non-urgent) classification for adenomyosis hysterectomy in the public system, with indicative treatment windows of 12+ months common in multiple jurisdictions. Women categorised as category 3 may wait 12–18 months at major public hospitals; anecdotal reports cite waits of 9–18 months in NSW and WA.

**The fertility-sparing surgery gap:** For the estimated 10–15% of adenomyosis presentations in younger, fertility-seeking women, hysterectomy is not an option, and the hormonal management options that are reversible (LNG-IUS, GnRH analogues) only suppress symptoms while in use — they do not alter the underlying uterine pathology. **Adenomyomectomy** — surgical excision of adenomyotic lesions from the myometrium while preserving the uterus — offers an alternative, but:

- Evidence for the procedure remains limited, with no randomised controlled trial data. A 2025 international review reported pregnancy rates of >50% and live birth rates of up to 70% for focal disease, but with a uterine rupture risk in subsequent pregnancy of up to 6%.
- Very few Australian gynaecologists have been trained in the technique, and there is no training pathway recognised or funded by RANZCOG or ACHS.
- It is not described in any MBS item specifically designed for the procedure; surgeons use existing hysterectomy/laparotomy items for the billing.
- The absence of high-quality evidence and formal credentialling means the procedure exists at the margins of recognised clinical practice, which in turn means it is rarely offered as an informed option to fertility-seeking patients — a significant equity and clinical completeness issue.

### 5. The Policy Gap: No Action Plan, No Clinics, No Dedicated Items

Against the backdrop above, the policy contrast with endometriosis is stark:

| Measure | Endometriosis | Adenomyosis |
|---|---|---|
| National Action Plan | Yes (2018, $127M+ to date) | No |
| Dedicated clinic network | Yes (33 EPPCs, $39.9M) | No |
| Condition-specific MBS items | Yes (items 125/126/127/129, 55080) | No (uses same items, named but not specific) |
| PBS-listed dedicated treatment | Yes (Ryeqo, dienogest via endometriosis) | No (accessed via overlap with other indications) |
| AIHW dedicated prevalence reporting | Yes | No |
| Research stream funding | Yes (within NAPE) | Partial (within endometriosis stream) |

The government's broader **$792.9 million women's health package** in the 2025–26 Budget provides additional primary care workforce capacity and service access funding, but it does not include a dedicated adenomyosis component. Advocates and clinicians have noted that the EPPCs — while technically accessible to adenomyosis patients given their broad "pelvic pain" mandate from 2025 — are operationally focused on endometriosis, were not resourced or staffed for the adenomyosis population, and are not named in the condition-specific communications around the clinics.

The **National Health Genomics Policy Framework** precedent — where a single framework encompasses multiple conditions — is not replicated here. Adenomyosis has neither its own framework nor full inclusion in the endometriosis framework.

### 6. Research Infrastructure: An Emerging but Thin Picture

The RANZCOG guideline's March 2026 update improved adenomyosis recommendations, but explicitly acknowledged that most adenomyosis recommendations are graded as low-certainty or conditional evidence — reflecting the thin research base.

Key structural issues in the research landscape:
- **No Australian prevalence cohort study**: unlike endometriosis (where AIHW and several academic groups maintain cohort data), there is no nationally representative adenomyosis prevalence or incidence dataset in Australia.
- **No dedicated Australian cost-of-illness study**: the Armour et al. figure of $9.7 billion for endometriosis has no equivalent for adenomyosis.
- **No national registry**: adenomyosis cases are not systematically recorded in a way that would allow longitudinal research, outcomes monitoring, or system-level planning.
- **Diagnostic criteria inconsistency**: internationally, there is no agreed consensus definition for ultrasound-based adenomyosis diagnosis, which makes Australian data difficult to interpret against international benchmarks. The Morphological Uterus Sonographic Assessment (MUSA) group published consensus criteria in 2022, but their adoption in Australian clinical settings is variable.

A 2026 scoping review in *Gynecological Surgery* found that the global evidence base for adenomyosis management remains dominated by retrospective case series and small cohort studies, with very few prospective controlled trials. This is, in part, a consequence of the condition's historical diagnostic architecture — a condition you cannot diagnose without destroying the organ you're studying is very difficult to study prospectively.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The co-occurrence problem creates equity anomalies.** Women who have both endometriosis and adenomyosis (a sizeable proportion of both populations) can now access EPPCs, the endometriosis-specific MBS items, and PBS-listed treatments. Women who have adenomyosis alone — potentially hundreds of thousands — receive none of these. This is an internal equity problem within women's health policy that is not currently acknowledged in any government framework.

**2. The hysterectomy pathway normalises a permanent intervention.** By failing to fund fertility-sparing diagnosis and conservative surgery for adenomyosis, Australia's healthcare system implicitly routes women toward a single definitive option. This is particularly problematic for women aged 25–35 with severe adenomyosis who have not completed — or have not yet decided about — their families. The RANZCOG guideline's conditional recommendation to "offer hormonal therapy before hysterectomy" is good practice guidance, but it does not create a funded pathway for the conservative surgical option.

**3. The PBS grey zone risks out-of-pocket cost escalation.** As GnRH antagonists and combination therapies are increasingly preferred over long-term analogue suppression (due to add-back hormone therapy and bone density preservation), the cost of private-prescription treatment will rise for adenomyosis patients who cannot access the endometriosis-restricted PBS items. A patient paying for Ryeqo privately (without the PBS endometriosis restriction) faces approximately $2,700 per year — the pre-May 2025 price the PBS listing was specifically designed to avoid.

**4. The fertility-and-pain paradox is unresolved.** The most effective reversible medical treatment (LNG-IUS) prevents pregnancy. The most effective fertility-preserving surgical option (adenomyomectomy) carries up to 6% uterine rupture risk. Women who want both pain control and future fertility face a genuine therapeutic cliff, and no funded clinical pathway exists specifically to navigate it.

**5. The EPPCs are not designed for adenomyosis at scale.** While EPPCs formally cover "pelvic pain" broadly, each clinic receives approximately $256,000 per year — enough for partial GP staffing plus some allied health. The clinics were designed for endometriosis presentations and funded on endometriosis case-load assumptions. Adding adenomyosis patients at any meaningful scale would require additional funding and a revised clinical model.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Women's health and the Medicare gap — specialist OOP costs**: the 2025 RANZCOG report found women face higher out-of-pocket specialist costs than men for equivalent complexity; gynaecology is a key site of this gap, and the new long consultation items are only a partial response to the broader fee-gap problem
2. **PBS hormonal treatment trends in women's health**: the trajectory of dienogest, Ryeqo, leuprorelin, and the GnRH antagonist class on the PBS — how prescribing volumes and costs interact with the endometriosis/adenomyosis treatment pipeline
3. **Gynaecologist training and distribution**: how many laparoscopic and fertility-sparing gynaecologists are there, where are they, and how does the training pipeline constrain access to surgery for pelvic pain conditions in regional and rural areas
4. **Heavy menstrual bleeding beyond adenomyosis**: the PBS and MBS architecture for HMB management, including uterine fibroid treatments (recently expanded in Australia), and the diagnostic-treatment interface for heavy bleeding across the full spectrum of gynaecological causes
5. **Women's health research funding architecture**: Australia's MRFF investment in endometriosis vs adenomyosis vs other gynaecological conditions — who sets the agenda, how projects are prioritised, and whether the condition-salience bias that produced an endometriosis action plan but not an adenomyosis one reflects deeper structural issues in clinical research funding
