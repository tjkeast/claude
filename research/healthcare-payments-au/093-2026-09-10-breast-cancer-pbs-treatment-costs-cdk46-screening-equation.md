# Breast Cancer and the PBS: The Rising Cost of CDK4/6 Inhibitors, Antibody-Drug Conjugates, and the Screening-Treatment Cost Equation

**Report 093 — 2026-09-10**

**Summary:** A wave of highly effective but expensive breast cancer medicines — CDK4/6 inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates — has been listed on the PBS since 2018, shifting incurable metastatic breast cancer toward a manageable chronic disease. That success is transforming the PBS budget: antineoplastic medicines are the fastest-growing expenditure category on a scheme already at $19.5 billion. With 20,800 Australians now living with metastatic disease, and each requiring treatment costing $44,000–$180,000 annually before subsidy, the screening-treatment cost equation — whether BreastScreen's low participation rate is inadvertently growing the treatment-dependent population — has never been more financially consequential.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 092 documented BreastScreen Australia's 35-year record — 52% participation, a First Nations equity gap, and a $2 billion reform need backed by only $1.3 million — and identified the rising PBS oncology bill as a thread requiring investigation. If earlier detection prevents progression to metastatic disease, the cost of under-participation in screening is not only measured in lives lost but in treatment expenditure foregone.

---

## Background: The Metastatic Breast Cancer Burden

For most of Australia's healthcare funding history, metastatic breast cancer (MBC) was a relatively rapid terminal diagnosis — median survival after metastatic diagnosis was approximately 18–24 months in the pre-targeted-therapy era. The population of people living *with* metastatic disease at any one time was consequently small.

The AIHW's landmark **first national estimates report (November 2025)** revealed just how dramatically that has changed: approximately **20,800 women and 150 men were living with metastatic breast cancer in Australia in 2024**. This figure — double previous estimates based on modelled projections — was made possible by new linkage methods connecting cancer registry, hospital, and mortality data. AIHW noted that survival is increasing and incidence is growing, meaning the prevalent MBC population will continue to expand.

These 20,950 Australians require ongoing systemic treatment — indefinitely, in most cases. The drugs sustaining them are among the most expensive on the PBS.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Wave of PBS-Listed Breast Cancer Medicines: 2018–2026

Australia's PBS has absorbed a wave of high-cost breast cancer medicines over eight years. The critical listings, with approximate unsubsidised costs, are:

#### CDK4/6 Inhibitors (Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 4 and 6 Inhibitors)

CDK4/6 inhibitors are now the **standard first-line treatment for hormone receptor-positive (HR+), HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer**, the most common subtype (~70% of all breast cancers). Three agents are approved and PBS-listed:

| Drug | Brand | PBS Listing (Metastatic) | Cost Without PBS | PBS Status (Adjuvant/Early) |
|------|-------|--------------------------|-------------------|-----------------------------|
| Ribociclib | Kisqali | June 2018 | ~$3,700–$4,000/month | **July 2025** — high-risk early BC |
| Palbociclib | Ibrance | May 2019 | ~$4,000/month | Not yet listed adjuvant |
| Abemaciclib | Verzenio | ~2022 | ~$3,500/month | Listed adjuvant (high-risk early) |

In clinical use, patients take CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with an aromatase inhibitor or fulvestrant for as long as disease does not progress — typically 1–4 years or more. At $3,700–$4,000 per month without subsidy, the unsubsidised annual cost is approximately **$44,400–$48,000 per patient per year**. With PBS subsidy, patients pay a maximum of $31.60 per script (or $7.70 with a concession card).

Palbociclib was the first listed. In its first two years on the PBS, **1,540 patients** received it in Year 1 and **2,261** in Year 2 (PBS 24-month analysis, February 2022). By 2021 Q3, palbociclib had the **highest PBS expenditure** of all breast cancer listings. Patient numbers have grown substantially since, as ribociclib and abemaciclib were listed and prescriber experience broadened.

**The July 2025 ribociclib adjuvant expansion** is a significant cost-escalator: the NATALEE trial demonstrated ribociclib reduces recurrence risk by 28.5% in high-risk HR+/HER2- stage II–III breast cancer when added to hormone therapy. The PBS listing enables ribociclib for an early-stage population that is substantially larger than the metastatic pool. The drug previously cost patients more than **$140,000 per three-year adjuvant course** without subsidy.

#### PARP Inhibitors (Poly ADP-Ribose Polymerase Inhibitors)

PARP inhibitors target breast cancers with germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations — approximately **5–7% of all breast cancer patients**, but a group at substantially elevated risk and with limited alternative targeted options.

| Drug | Brand | PBS Indication | Cost Without PBS |
|------|-------|----------------|------------------|
| Olaparib | Lynparza | July 2024 — HER2-negative, high-risk early breast cancer with germline BRCA1/2 mutation, post-chemotherapy | ~$72,000 per course |
| Olaparib | Lynparza | January 2025 — metastatic BRCA-mutated breast cancer | ~$72,000 per course |
| Talazoparib | Talzenna | Metastatic breast cancer with germline BRCA1/2 | ~$101,000 per course |

Without subsidy, a full course of olaparib costs approximately **$72,000** and talazoparib approximately **$101,000**. PBS listing reduces patient cost to $31.60 per script. These medications represent the first genetically targeted option for BRCA-mutated early breast cancer and mark a shift toward biomarker-stratified prescribing.

#### Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs)

The most expensive new category. ADCs combine a monoclonal antibody with a cytotoxic payload, delivering targeted chemotherapy directly to cancer cells.

- **Trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu):** Listed November 2023 for HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer; expanded **September 2024** to HER2-low metastatic breast cancer (approximately 55–60% of all HR+ metastatic cases). Pre-PBS cost: **$10,000–$15,000 per treatment cycle** (administered every three weeks). Approximately 1,700 patients per year benefit from the PBS listing. In DESTINY-Breast04, the drug extended progression-free survival by approximately 10 months and overall survival by more than 23 months versus standard chemotherapy — one of the largest survival gains in metastatic breast cancer history.

- **Sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy):** PBS-listed for triple-negative metastatic breast cancer (TNBC), the most aggressive subtype. PBAC declined an expansion to HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer (nominated for early re-entry pathway as of 2025). The denial leaves a therapy with phase III evidence (TROPiCS-02: +6 months median OS) inaccessible on PBS for the HR+/HER2- population.

#### AKT Inhibitors

- **Capivasertib (Truqap):** PBS listing **1 October 2025** for HR+/HER2- locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer with a PIK3CA, AKT1, or PTEN alteration, in combination with fulvestrant. Pre-PBS cost: more than **$98,000**. Approximately **3,000 patients per year** are expected to benefit. The drug targets the AKT pathway, which is overactive in approximately 40–50% of HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancers that have progressed on CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy — making it a second-line option in a population already maintained on PBS-listed CDK4/6 inhibitors.

### 2. The PBS Cost Trajectory

Australia's PBS reached **$19.5 billion in government spending in 2024–25** — a 7.5% increase on 2023–24's $17.4 billion in government subsidies. The fastest-growing therapeutic class is **antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents** — cancer medicines — which represent the single highest-expenditure therapeutic category on the PBS at approximately **$259 per person per year in government spending** (2023 estimate; the figure will be materially higher by 2025–26 given the October 2025 capivasertib listing and the July 2025 ribociclib adjuvant expansion).

Within oncology, the fastest-growing sub-categories are:
- **Monoclonal antibody drugs** (including trastuzumab deruxtecan, sacituzumab govitecan): 23% increase in use in recent years
- **Kinase inhibitors** (including CDK4/6 inhibitors): 16% increase in use

Breast cancer medicines are a major driver of both categories. The cumulative effect of listing palbociclib (2019), ribociclib (2018, expanded 2025), abemaciclib (adjuvant), olaparib (2024/2025), talazoparib, trastuzumab deruxtecan (2023, expanded 2024), sacituzumab govitecan, and capivasertib (2025) is a breast cancer medicines PBS spend that has grown from near zero in 2017 to what is plausibly now one of the three largest oncology sub-budgets on the PBS.

The PBS does not publish a per-disease breakdown of expenditure. The aggregate pattern — total PBS spend growing faster than population and inflation, antineoplastics the fastest-growing category — is, however, clear from available reports.

### 3. The Screening-Treatment Cost Equation

This is the financial logic that connects Report 092's screening participation data to the PBS cost trajectory documented above.

**The pathway from under-screening to higher PBS cost:**

> Low BreastScreen participation → cancers detected at later stages → higher proportion of women with metastatic or high-risk early disease → more patients enter the CDK4/6 inhibitor / ADC / PARP inhibitor treatment pipeline → higher PBS expenditure per patient-year → higher total PBS breast cancer spending

The magnitude of this effect depends on stage-at-detection data — which, as noted in prior reports, Australia does not collect systematically nationally. The most defensible proxies are:

- **Screen-detected cancers are caught earlier:** AIHW BreastScreen monitoring consistently shows screen-detected cancers have lower rates of lymph node involvement and are more likely to be localised at detection.
- **Localised vs. metastatic treatment costs differ by an order of magnitude:** Treating localised (stage I–II) breast cancer typically involves surgery, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy — estimated total lifetime treatment cost in the range of **$30,000–$60,000 per patient** in Australia. Treating metastatic disease with CDK4/6 inhibitors and subsequent ADCs costs the PBS **$44,000–$72,000 per patient per year**, with median duration of treatment measured in years.
- **~20,950 Australians currently living with metastatic disease** (AIHW 2025): even if only a fraction of these cases could have been prevented or delayed by earlier detection, the treatment cost displacement is substantial.

The AIHW's own BreastScreen cost-effectiveness estimates — **8 breast cancer deaths prevented per 1,000 women screened** — focus on mortality rather than downstream treatment expenditure. No Australian study has quantified the PBS cost offset from higher BreastScreen participation (a significant research gap).

**The adjuvant expansion paradox:** The July 2025 ribociclib adjuvant listing illustrates a specific complexity. CDK4/6 inhibitors being extended to high-risk *early* breast cancer patients is clinically excellent — it reduces recurrence risk by 28.5% — but it also brings a very large population onto a PBS-listed drug that costs the government approximately $44,000 per patient year. This cost is partially offset if that patient does not later develop metastatic disease requiring even more expensive treatment. But the net budget impact depends critically on the recurrence risk reduction translating to fewer metastatic cases entering the treatment pipeline — precisely the kind of long-run cost-effectiveness argument that challenges PBAC's standard 12-month budget horizon.

### 4. Patient Out-of-Pocket Costs: The Gap Before PBS Access

Before each of these drugs reached PBS listing, Australian patients who could access them at all paid the full commercial price or participated in clinical trials. A 2020 study of MBS and PBS co-payments for Australian breast cancer patients in the first three years post-diagnosis found:

- **Median MBS co-payment:** AUD $748 (IQR $87–$2,121; maximum $32,249)
- **Median PBS co-payment:** AUD $835 (IQR $480–$1,289; maximum $5,390)
- Co-payments varied significantly by socioeconomic status and Indigenous status; patients with greatest financial disadvantage faced the highest relative burden

These figures predate the major CDK4/6 inhibitor listings (palbociclib listed May 2019, the study's data cutoff). As PBS listings have expanded, co-payments within the subsidy system have become more manageable. But:

1. The **safety net threshold** (approximately $2,075 general / $348 concession for PBS in 2025) protects against high annual PBS out-of-pocket costs, but women with multiple listings (CDK4/6 inhibitor + olaparib + later ADC) can still accumulate costs approaching the general threshold before protection triggers.
2. The **MBS gap** for oncology specialist consultations, diagnostic imaging, genetic testing, and pathology remains substantial and is not covered by the PBS safety net.
3. **Sacituzumab govitecan for HR+/HER2- disease** — declined by PBAC and nominated for re-entry — represents a real access gap: patients with HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer who progress on CDK4/6 inhibitors and capivasertib currently have no PBS-funded sacituzumab govitecan option, meaning the drug's $10,000–$15,000/cycle cost falls entirely on the patient or is unavailable.

### 5. The Access Gap: What's Still Not Listed

Despite the rapid expansion since 2018, several effective therapies remain off-PBS for Australian breast cancer patients as of mid-2026:

- **Sacituzumab govitecan (HR+/HER2- MBC):** PBAC declined; early re-entry pathway nominated. Approximately 40–50% of metastatic HR+ patients are eligible but cannot access PBS subsidy.
- **Datopotamab deruxtecan:** TGA registered mid-2026 for HR+/HER2- MBC; PBAC submission not yet complete.
- **Inavolisib + palbociclib + fulvestrant:** Phase III data mature (INAVO120); not yet submitted for PBAC.
- **Elacestrant:** Oral SERD; approved US/EU; PBAC submission timeline unknown.

BCNA has consistently warned that Australia's access-to-approval timeline for oncology medicines — typically 2–4 years from international approval to PBS listing — means Australians routinely wait longer than their counterparts in the UK, US, and Canada for standard-of-care therapies.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The PBAC Sustainability Horizon

The PBAC assesses medicines against a **cost-effectiveness threshold** of approximately $50,000–$75,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) — a figure last formally reviewed in 2008. The new breast cancer medicines — CDK4/6 inhibitors, ADCs — are priced to fall just within or below this threshold after confidential rebate negotiations with manufacturers. But as the list grows and patient numbers expand (particularly with adjuvant indications), the aggregate PBS expenditure becomes a political and fiscal pressure point. There is no published government framework for aggregate oncology spend management; each medicine is assessed in isolation. The cumulative cost of 8–10 major breast cancer listings on the PBS budget is not formally tracked or bounded.

### Tension 2: The Survival Paradox

Better treatment extends survival, which increases the number of people living with metastatic disease, which increases the number of patients on ongoing PBS-listed therapy. This is a structurally different cost dynamic from acute conditions. The AIHW's first MBC prevalence estimate (20,950 in 2024) will grow — by design, as treatment improves. Australia's PBS is effectively underwriting a growing chronic-disease population of metastatic breast cancer patients, with per-patient annual costs of $44,000–$100,000+. Long-run fiscal projections are not published.

### Tension 3: The Research Gap on Screening-Treatment Cost Offset

No Australian study has quantified the reduction in PBS breast cancer medicines spending attributable to BreastScreen participation. The screening program and the PBS operate under entirely separate funding frameworks — BreastScreen under a Commonwealth-state cost-sharing agreement, PBS medicines under Commonwealth Pharmaceutical Benefits appropriations. There is no institutional mechanism linking BreastScreen's prevention budget to the PBS's treatment savings. This silo makes it structurally impossible to argue — even if the data supported it — that investing $2 billion in BreastScreen reform would offset PBS expenditure.

### Tension 4: Equity in Access to High-Cost Medicines

The PBS safety net mechanisms are designed to prevent high co-payment accumulation. But:
- First Nations women, who have both lower BreastScreen participation (36%) and higher breast cancer mortality, also have lower rates of accessing the specialist oncology services that prescribe CDK4/6 inhibitors and PARP inhibitors.
- Remote patients face structural barriers to the specialist-delivered oncology care the PBS listings require (specialist certification, hospital-based infusion for some regimens).
- The MBS gap for oncology specialists compounds cost burden for non-concession patients.

PBS listing is a necessary but not sufficient condition for equitable access.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHI and the BreastScreen diagnostic aftermarket.** Women referred from BreastScreen with abnormal results — recalled for breast MRI, specialist breast surgery consultation, image-guided biopsy — enter a diagnostic pathway that is partially Medicare-funded but carries significant specialist gaps. Dense-breast notification reform (from Report 092) will expand this population. What does the private diagnostic pathway cost women, and how does PHI coverage interact?

2. **Trastuzumab deruxtecan in practice: the HER2-testing infrastructure burden.** Expanding ADC access to HER2-low disease (55–60% of HR+ metastatic breast cancer) requires HER2 immunohistochemistry testing capable of distinguishing HER2-low from HER2-zero — a pathology capability that varies by laboratory. What is the MBS funding for HER2 testing, what is the testing quality gap, and how is Australia building the infrastructure for precision oncology at scale?

3. **PBAC: how Australia decides which medicines get listed — and who waits.** The sacituzumab govitecan rejection for HR+/HER2- disease, capivasertib's AKT pathway requirement, and elacestrant's PBAC timeline illustrate the PBAC's decision-making framework. How does PBAC weigh clinical evidence, cost-effectiveness, budget impact, and clinical need — and how does Australia's access-to-listing timeline compare with NICE (UK), CADTH (Canada), and FDA/CMS (US)?

4. **Breast cancer genetic testing and the Medicare gap.** PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated breast cancer require germline BRCA1/2 testing. MBS rebates for BRCA testing exist but coverage is incomplete — some testing costs $300–$600 out-of-pocket. Cascade testing for affected families and somatic tumour testing for treatment stratification are partially unfunded. What is the MBS framework for cancer genomics, and where are the gaps?

5. **The adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor budget impact: a 3-year picture.** Ribociclib (July 2025) and abemaciclib are now listed for adjuvant use in high-risk early breast cancer. The patient population eligible for adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitors is estimated at 2,000–4,000 per year nationally. At $44,000/patient/year for 2–3 years, the three-year PBS budget impact is potentially $200–$500 million in additional expenditure. Is PBAC's modelling for this population matched by real-world prescribing?
