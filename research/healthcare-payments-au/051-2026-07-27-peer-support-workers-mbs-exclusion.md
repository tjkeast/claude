# Peer Support Workers and the MBS Exclusion: Australia's Workforce That Medicare Cannot Fund

**Report 051 — 2026-07-27**

**Summary:** Australia has approximately 5,000 community-managed mental health peer workers in NSW alone — people with lived experience of mental illness employed to support others in recovery — yet none can bill Medicare for their services. The MBS requires AHPRA registration; peer workers are unregistered. The result is a workforce with demonstrated outcomes (the NSW Peer-STOC program generated $9.77M in health system savings from a $7.92M investment) funded entirely through precarious block grants, structurally excluded from the primary payment mechanism that funds nearly every other healthcare encounter in Australia. A national association for peer workers launched this month — but whether that leads to Medicare access remains deeply contested.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 050 showed that Australia's community-managed mental health sector workforce operates on block-grant funding that chronically lags behind award wage increases, creating a structural squeeze. Peer support workers are the clearest example of this structural gap: they are the fastest-growing occupational category in community-managed mental health, their effectiveness is backed by strong evidence, and they are entirely excluded from the MBS — the funding mechanism that would give their services a sustainable financial foundation.

---

## Background

Peer support work in mental health is built on a simple but powerful premise: people who have lived experience of mental illness — who have navigated the mental health system, survived crisis, and found ways forward — can offer something that clinical professionals cannot. Not therapy. Not diagnosis. Not medication management. Something different: hope that recovery is possible, knowledge of what services and strategies actually help, solidarity from someone who has been where you are.

That distinctive contribution has driven the rapid growth of the peer workforce globally. In England, NHS commissioned services now employ over 1,000 peer support workers — directly employed, salary-banded, integrated into clinical teams. In the United States, Medicaid has covered peer support specialist services since Georgia first introduced reimbursement in 2001; as of 2024, dozens of states and the District of Columbia have implemented similar mechanisms, and US Medicare reforms have begun supporting peer support specialists as part of care teams.

In Australia, the picture is structurally different — and structurally worse. Peer workers are employed predominantly through community-managed NGOs, funded by block grants from Primary Health Networks, state health departments, and the NDIS. They cannot bill Medicare. They cannot have a Medicare provider number. They are excluded from the Benefits Schedule because they are not registered with the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) — the gateway through which nearly every other clinical and allied health profession accesses Commonwealth funding.

## Key Findings

### 1. Who Peer Workers Are and What They Do

Peer workers in Australian mental health are people employed specifically because of their lived experience of mental illness, mental health service use, or associated experiences (including suicide attempts and recovery). Their work includes:

- **Psychosocial recovery coaching**: supporting NDIS participants with psychosocial disability to build capacity, pursue goals, and navigate services
- **Peer support**: one-to-one or group contact to share lived experience, promote hope, and reduce isolation
- **Post-hospital transition support**: supporting people discharged from inpatient units to navigate the return to community (the Peer-STOC model)
- **Crisis peer support**: non-clinical support during or after crisis episodes
- **Community development**: facilitating peer-led programs, self-help groups, and advocacy

The highest qualification pathway for peer workers in Australia is the **Certificate IV in Mental Health Peer Work** — a nationally recognised but vocational qualification that sits below any AHPRA-registered health profession. This qualification ceiling directly constrains pay classification under the SCHADS Award (Report 050) and creates a structural career ceiling: experienced peer workers with 15 years in the sector cannot access classification levels available to certificate or degree-holding clinicians.

### 2. Workforce Numbers: Growing But Hard to Count

Nationally, **328.8 full-time equivalent paid peer support workers** were employed in specialist mental health services as of the most recent AIHW data — a figure that captures only those in public and directly commissioned services and significantly undercounts the sector.

In community-managed organisations — the NGO sector — the picture is larger. In NSW alone, approximately **5,000 paid workers** are employed in community-managed mental health organisations (Mental Health Coordinating Council, 2023), comprising roughly 25% of the state's total mental health workforce. Peer workers are now **the second-largest occupational group** in NSW's community-managed mental health sector, comprising **12.3% of all workers** — larger than any individual clinical profession in that sector. The MHCC notes that allied health workers in community-managed organisations nearly doubled between 2021 and 2023, and peer worker numbers have grown commensurately.

The National Mental Health Workforce Strategy 2022-2032 acknowledges the peer workforce as "a valued component of the mental health sector" but does not include peer workers in its health professional pipeline models — because they are not a regulated profession, they do not appear in health workforce data collections the way nurses, psychologists, or GPs do.

### 3. The MBS Exclusion: Why It Exists and What It Costs

The MBS is structured around registered health professionals. To receive a Medicare provider number — required to submit claims to the Benefits Schedule — a practitioner must be:

- An AHPRA-registered health professional (GP, specialist, psychologist, occupational therapist, physiotherapist, social worker meeting specific criteria, etc.)
- Or recognised under a specific determination of the Health Insurance Act 1973 (covering categories such as Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Workers)

Peer workers are neither. The Certificate IV in Mental Health Peer Work is not an AHPRA-registered qualification. There is no route under existing law for a peer worker to obtain a Medicare provider number, regardless of their experience, training, or the evidence base for their work.

The consequence is structural: **every interaction between a peer worker and a person in recovery is funded from somewhere other than Medicare**. That means:

- PHN flexible mental health funding (block grant, regionally distributed)
- State health department contracts (block grant, state-specific)
- NDIS funding (for NDIS participants with psychosocial disability who include peer support in their plan)
- The Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP) — extended in MYEFO 2024-25 with $272.1 million for two years to June 2027, funding psychosocial support for people with severe mental illness who do not have NDIS access
- Philanthropy and program grants (minor, project-based)

None of these are sustainable in the way Medicare is sustainable. Block grants are time-limited, regionally variable, subject to renegotiation, and — as Report 050 showed — persistently indexed below award wage growth. The CPSP extension is two years; its future beyond June 2027 is unresolved.

By contrast, a GP consultation under Item 23, a psychologist session under Better Access, or a nurse practitioner-led care plan each have a direct claim pathway to the Commonwealth that is not subject to annual budget negotiations.

### 4. The Evidence Base: What Peer Workers Actually Deliver

The case for peer work is not merely ideological. The evidence base has strengthened materially:

**NSW Peer-STOC (Peer-Supported Transfer of Care):**

NSW Health launched the Peer-STOC program statewide in 2017 — providing recovery-focused peer worker support for six weeks post-discharge from inpatient mental health units. The independent evaluation (NSW Mental Health Commission, 2022) found:

- **Reduced 28-day readmission rates** compared to a matched comparison group of 4,122 similar individuals
- **Reduced hospitalisation** over the 12 months following Peer-STOC contact
- **Improved community-based service contacts** and consumer recovery outcome scores
- Economic result: NSW Health invested **$7.92 million** in the first three years; Peer-STOC was associated with health system savings of at least **$9.77 million** — equivalent to the release of 7,904 hospital bed days

**Systematic reviews and meta-analyses:**

International systematic reviews confirm that peer support produces: improved engagement with services, reduced substance use, reduced unmet needs, improved self-management, increased hope, improved recovery quality of life scores, and in multiple studies, **reduced hospitalisation rates**. A 2022 meta-analysis in *Psychiatric Services* (Lloyd-Evans et al.) found peer support was effective across personal and clinical recovery domains.

**Primary care RCT:**

A stepped-wedge cluster randomised controlled trial of peer support in Australian general practices (PS-PC trial, protocol published PMC 2024) is currently underway — testing 12 hours of peer worker support for people with mental health conditions in primary care settings. Results are expected to provide Australian-specific primary care evidence; the protocol notes that peer workers' presence in primary care "has been limited, despite potential benefits."

**Youth mental health:**

A 2023 systematic review (*BMC Psychiatry*) found peer support effective for young people with anxiety and depression, including in reducing symptoms and improving engagement with help-seeking.

### 5. The Professionalisation Question and the New Association

The logical path from "evidence is strong, funding is precarious, MBS requires registration" would seem to be: professionalise peer workers, achieve AHPRA registration or equivalent, access the MBS. The reality is more complicated.

**The new national association (August 2026):**

The Australian Government committed **$4.78 million** (March 2026–December 2028) as seed funding to establish the **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Peer Workforce Association**, which became operational in August 2026. The Association is facilitated by the National Mental Health Consumer Alliance (NMHCA) in partnership with the Indigenous Australian Lived Experience Centre (IALEC) and Mental Health Carers Australia (MHCA). Peer workers lead the organisation.

The Association's mandate includes: developing role definitions and core functions; building a national supervision framework; defining career pathways; establishing national training pathways; and developing workplace safety standards. This is professionalisation infrastructure — the groundwork for a credentialling system.

**The tension in professionalisation:**

The peer support field has a deep tension about what professionalisation means for the authenticity of the work. Published research (including a 2025 paper in *Healthcare* and PMC literature on peer support assimilation) identifies several risks:

- **Fidelity erosion**: As peer workers are integrated into clinical teams and subject to professional standards, they may be pressured to conform to clinical norms — suppressing disclosure of lived experience, adopting clinical language, performing care rather than peer relationship
- **Institutional assimilation**: The "horizontal and mutualistic nature of peer support" conflicts with "the bureaucratic and hierarchical logics of clinical contexts"
- **Credentialism as exclusion**: Raising qualification requirements for peer workers may exclude the very people who should be doing the work — those with the most acute experience of mental illness and service systems, who are often also the least positioned to complete formal study

The 2019 *Towards Professionalisation* report (Lived Experience Australia, updated in consultation in 2025) wrestled explicitly with these tensions. Its recommendations deliberately sought professionalisation without AHPRA registration — a middle path involving sector-owned credentialling rather than government-regulated registration.

**AHPRA registration: a distant or undesirable goal:**

There is no current proposal before AHPRA or the Health Practitioner Regulation National Law to create a registered profession of peer work. Many in the peer workforce movement actively oppose AHPRA registration, fearing it would narrow the pool of people who can do peer work, create clinical professional capture, and undermine the peer relationship that is the core of the work.

This means the primary route to MBS access — AHPRA registration — is both structurally distant and actively contested within the workforce it would affect.

### 6. Alternative Funding Pathways: What's Possible Without AHPRA

There are precedents for non-AHPRA providers accessing Medicare under specific conditions:

- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Workers** (ATSI HWs) have a specific MBS item pathway under the Health Insurance Act without full AHPRA general registration — using a limited recognition mechanism
- **Mental Health Nurses** without full AHPRA registration may deliver some services under specific items
- **Practice nurses** can provide certain Medicare services through a supervisory billing model under the GP's provider number

A supervisory billing model is the most frequently discussed near-term option for peer workers: a peer worker delivers a service, but it is billed under the supervising clinician's (e.g., GP's or psychiatrist's) provider number. This model:
- Does not require peer worker AHPRA registration
- Exists in some form for practice nurses now
- Would still restrict peer worker independence and cap where they can work
- Raises questions about clinical liability and professional responsibility

A dedicated MBS item determination under the Health Insurance Act — creating a specific peer worker category analogous to the ATSI Health Worker pathway — is a second option. This has been proposed in sector submissions but has not been taken up in any recent federal budget or policy instrument.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The MBS pathway and identity conflict.** The most durable route to Medicare access is AHPRA registration. The peer workforce movement does not want AHPRA registration. This is not a resolvable technical problem — it is a genuine values conflict about what peer support is and who controls it.

**2. The block grant cliff in 2027.** The CPSP, which funds psychosocial support for people who don't qualify for NDIS, expires June 2027. If it is not renewed, and no Medicare pathway has been created, the primary Commonwealth funding stream for non-NDIS peer support disappears. This is a structural risk with a specific date.

**3. Peer workers in NDIS vs. non-NDIS:** NDIS funding is available for peer support as a support type — but only for NDIS participants with approved psychosocial disability plans. The people who most need peer support are often exactly those who fall between NDIS eligibility and sufficient wealth to access private services. The CPSP is the funding instrument for this group; its precarity is a direct risk to peer support access.

**4. Certificate IV ceiling and professionalisation pace.** The national association has $4.78M and a mandate to build career pathways. But the qualification ceiling for peer workers under the SCHADS Award is already limiting pay and career progression (Report 050). A credential above Certificate IV — even a sector-owned one — would require funding for training, wage parity with the new classification, and employer willingness to recognise it.

**5. The evidence gap in primary care.** Most Australian peer support evidence comes from post-inpatient or community-managed settings. The PS-PC trial is generating primary care evidence, but it is not yet complete. Without primary care evidence, the case for a GP-adjacent billing mechanism for peer workers cannot be made with full force.
