# Cancer Genomics and the Medicare Funding Gap: How Australia's Precision Oncology Pipeline Is Built on Testing Infrastructure That Isn't Funded

**Report 094 — 2026-09-11**

**Summary:** Australia has listed a wave of precision oncology medicines on the PBS — CDK4/6 inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, antibody-drug conjugates, and targeted therapies — that require genomic testing to establish patient eligibility. Yet the Medicare Benefits Schedule covers only a narrow set of single-gene germline tests in specific cancer-indication pairs. Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP), the technology enabling tumour-wide precision treatment matching, is not routinely funded through Medicare for most cancers. Instead, 23,000 Australians per year access free CGP through the research-funded Omico/PrOSPeCT program — a clinical-trial workaround that is now the de facto standard of care for the advanced-cancer population it serves, backed by $71 million in Budget 2026-27 funding but still structurally outside routine Medicare. A January 2025 National Framework for Genomics in Cancer Control articulates the vision; the payment system hasn't yet delivered it.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 093 documented the PBS listing of PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib), CDK4/6 inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates for breast cancer — and noted that each of these therapies requires specific genomic or biomarker testing to establish PBS eligibility. This report examines the other side of that access equation: what Australia's Medicare system actually funds in cancer genomic testing, where the gaps are, and how a fragmented testing landscape creates inequity in precision oncology access.

---

## Background: The Genomic Gatekeeper Problem

Modern cancer treatment is increasingly defined by a simple principle: identify the molecular alteration driving a tumour, then target it with a drug designed for that alteration. The principle is straightforward; the infrastructure is not.

To access the precision oncology drugs Australia's PBS now subsidises, patients must first *have the test* that establishes their eligibility. PARP inhibitors require BRCA1/2 or HRD (homologous recombination deficiency) testing. Trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) for HER2-low disease requires immunohistochemistry capable of distinguishing HER2-low from HER2-zero. Capivasertib (Truqap) requires PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alteration testing. Targeted therapies in lung cancer require EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, KRAS G12C, MET, BRAF, and NTRK testing. Pembrolizumab (Keytruda) requires microsatellite instability (MSI) or PD-L1 testing across multiple tumour types.

The genomic test is not optional; it is the PBS eligibility criterion. A patient whose BRCA status is unknown cannot receive olaparib under PBS authority conditions. A patient whose tumour is not tested for PIK3CA mutation cannot receive capivasertib. The testing infrastructure is, in effect, part of the medicine's delivery mechanism — but unlike the medicine, it is funded (or not funded) by a separate scheme with separate criteria, administered by different institutions, and subject to different access barriers.

## Key Findings

### 1. The MBS Genetic Testing Landscape: Narrow but Expanding

Australia's Medicare Benefits Schedule covers a defined — and slowly growing — set of cancer genomic tests. The current framework involves several distinct categories:

#### Germline BRCA Testing (MBS Items 73295–73304)

The most mature and well-funded pathway covers **germline BRCA1/2 testing** — testing of heritable variants in a patient's blood or saliva, not their tumour.

**Item 73296** (and the related 73297): Germline genetic characterisation in patients with breast, ovarian, fallopian tube or primary peritoneal cancer with at least a **10% prior probability** of a pathogenic variant. This is the primary diagnostic item. As of **1 March 2025**, MSAC-supported amendments expanded these items to allow clinicians to test additional genes beyond BRCA1/2 (e.g., PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, BRIP1, RAD51C/D) where clinically appropriate — a significant expansion from single-gene to panel testing within the same Medicare framework.

**Items 73303/73304**: Cascade testing for **family members** of a proband with an identified pathogenic variant — the most cost-effective use of genetic testing (one positive result reveals at-risk relatives). As of **1 January 2025**, these items were amended to enable their use in identifying PBS treatment eligibility, not only risk management — directly connecting germline testing to precision oncology access.

**Triple-negative breast cancer**: All patients with TNBC are eligible for bulk-billed testing due to the ≥10% BRCA pathogenic variant prevalence in this subtype.

**Prostate cancer**: From **1 April 2022**, patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) can access MBS-funded testing (both somatic and germline) to establish BRCA1/2 status for olaparib PBS eligibility — a disease-specific carve-out reflecting the drug's PBS listing.

**Gap between germline testing and eligibility thresholds**: Prior to the TNBC expansion, patients who fell below the 10% prior-probability threshold but wanted BRCA testing faced out-of-pocket costs of approximately **$1,200 per test** (Sydney Cancer Genetics 2024 pricing). The family history scoring system used to determine the 10% threshold was documented as inequitable — patients from small families, adoptees, or those with limited family health history may not score above the threshold even when they carry a mutation.

#### HRD Testing for Ovarian Cancer

A dedicated MBS item for **homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) testing** in newly diagnosed advanced ovarian, fallopian tube and peritoneal cancer was introduced to support PBS access to PARP inhibitors (niraparib, olaparib) in the maintenance setting. HRD status (including BRCA somatic mutations) determines eligibility for PBS-subsidised PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy. This item represents the MBS entering somatic testing territory — a significant conceptual step.

#### Lung Cancer Biomarker Testing (MBS Item — New 2025)

A new MBS item for **comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tumour tissue** was assessed by MSAC (Application 1634) and listed from **1 July 2025**. This is Australia's first MBS-funded CGP item for tumour tissue and a landmark in the move toward panel-based, multi-gene testing coverage.

The NSCLC CGP item enables the detection of multiple biomarkers simultaneously (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, KRAS, MET, BRAF, NTRK, PD-L1) using a single next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay, replacing a cascade of single-gene tests. The item is critical for patients who would otherwise need multiple sequential tests — each with its own MBS item, its own processing time, and potentially its own gap — to establish eligibility for the eight or more PBS-funded targeted therapies in NSCLC.

However, **lung cancer is currently the only solid tumour cancer type** with an MBS-funded CGP test. All other tumour types remain in the single-gene, indication-specific testing model.

### 2. The Comprehensive Genomic Profiling Gap

For cancer types without a CGP item — which means **all cancers except NSCLC** — patients and oncologists face a stark choice:

**Option A: MBS-funded single-gene testing** — Order each biomarker test as a separate MBS item for each gene where one exists. This is slow (requiring sequential test ordering and reporting), incomplete (not all genes have MBS items), and resource-intensive for pathology labs. For cancers requiring testing across multiple pathways simultaneously, sequential testing may produce results faster than each piece reveals it is negative.

**Option B: Out-of-pocket CGP testing** — Patient pays $2,000–$4,500 for a commercial CGP panel (Foundation Medicine, Caris Molecular Intelligence, Illumina TSO500, Tempus) or whole genome sequencing. This is inaccessible for most patients outside major metropolitan centres and is rarely covered by private health insurance (which covers procedures, not diagnostic tests ordered on an outpatient basis).

**Option C: Omico/PrOSPeCT enrolment** — Access free CGP through Australia's federally funded clinical trials program (described below).

The gap is documented clearly in peer-reviewed literature. A 2024 study in *Clinical and Translational Science* (Kong et al., 2026) analysed Medicare data and found that **utilisation of cancer biomarker testing is geographically uneven**, with test uptake highest in New South Wales (23.8% of incident cancers) and lowest in the Northern Territory (8.7%). A Victorian Precision Oncology Summit analysis (PMC 2024) concluded that "equitable access to genomic molecular testing for Australian cancer patients" was "hindered by fragmented funding, lack of standardisation, and geographic disparities."

A consumer-led survey from Victoria (2026) found that many patients reported delays of weeks to months in receiving tumour genomic profiling results, with cost cited as a barrier to accessing non-MBS-funded testing.

### 3. The Omico/PrOSPeCT Workaround: Research Infrastructure as Standard of Care

The most important — and structurally unusual — component of Australia's cancer genomics landscape is the **Omico/PrOSPeCT platform**: a federally funded not-for-profit program providing free CGP to Australians with advanced and incurable cancers.

**Origins:** Omico (formerly known as the Garvan Institute's Molecular Screening and Therapeutics program) has operated precision oncology programs since 2016. Since then, more than **22,300 Australians** have accessed precision oncology innovations through Omico programs, with referrals from over 1,300 clinicians.

**Current program:** The **Precision Oncology Screening Platform Enabling Clinical Trials (PrOSPeCT)** provides:
- Free comprehensive genomic profiling of tumour DNA (NGS panel)
- Treatment matching to PBS-listed medicines where an actionable mutation is identified
- Clinical trial matching across 90+ active oncology trials
- Biological banking and molecular tumour board review at participating centres

**2026-27 Budget investment:** The Albanese government committed **$71 million over three years** (to 2028–29) to continue PrOSPeCT, enabling access to free CGP for an estimated **23,000 Australians with advanced or incurable cancers** over that period.

**The structural paradox:** PrOSPeCT is the *de facto* routine clinical service for CGP in most of Australia — not a research program. Clinicians refer patients to it not to enrol them in trials but to access the genomic profiling that should, in clinical terms, be routine care. The $71 million three-year commitment is substantially more efficient per patient than individual MBS funding would be (Omico's scale and procurement achieve a lower per-test cost), but it:

- Is not available in all regions (participating centres are concentrated in metropolitan academic hospitals)
- Requires enrolment in a research registry (raising governance and consent considerations)
- Has no guaranteed continuity beyond 2028-29 without further political decisions
- Does not cover all cancer types with equal depth (some tumour types are under-enrolled relative to their burden)

As BioPharmaDispatch (May 2026) noted: "The $71 million budget allocation represents a continuation of the status quo rather than the structural integration of genomic testing into Medicare — a distinction that patient advocates and oncologists have called out."

### 4. The National Framework for Genomics in Cancer Control (January 2025)

Cancer Australia released the **National Framework for Genomics in Cancer Control** in January 2025, a document that explicitly states the vision of integrating genomics into clinical practice as the "standard of care" for all Australians.

Key framework elements:
- **Equity emphasis**: Specific recognition of the need for "culturally safe and accessible" testing for First Nations Australians, and acknowledgment that current testing access is inequitable across socioeconomic, geographic, and demographic lines
- **Care continuum approach**: Genomics coverage across prevention, early detection, diagnosis, treatment, and survivorship — not only late-stage treatment eligibility
- **Infrastructure requirements**: Policy guidance on testing infrastructure quality, genetic counselling workforce, privacy and Indigenous data sovereignty, and ongoing monitoring
- **Funding commitment**: A **$3 million Cancer Genomics Clinical Trials Fund** was announced alongside the Framework to support trials incorporating genomics

The contrast between the $3 million trials fund and the $71 million PrOSPeCT commitment illustrates the funding architecture: the research infrastructure (Omico) receives substantially more than the strategic framework designed to move toward systematic clinical integration. The Framework does not include a funded MBS reform pathway or a timeline for CGP coverage beyond NSCLC.

### 5. The PBS-MBS Access Loop: Tests That Must Be Done, Funding That Doesn't Cover Them

The precision oncology access gap is not abstract — it is encoded in specific PBS authority conditions. Several PBS-listed therapies have authorisation conditions that require testing results that are not routinely MBS-funded:

| Drug (PBS listed) | Required test | MBS funded? |
|---|---|---|
| Olaparib (breast cancer adjuvant, July 2024) | Germline BRCA1/2 | Yes (item 73296) |
| Olaparib (ovarian cancer maintenance) | Somatic + germline BRCA; HRD | Yes (HRD item) |
| Talazoparib (breast cancer) | Germline BRCA1/2 | Yes (item 73296) |
| Capivasertib (Truqap, Oct 2025) | PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alteration | **No dedicated MBS item** |
| Trastuzumab deruxtecan HER2-low (Sept 2024) | HER2 IHC score 1+ or 2+/ISH- | Partially (existing pathology items) |
| Sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy — TNBC only) | TNBC confirmation | Via existing items |
| Pembrolizumab (MSI-H solid tumours) | MSI/MMR testing | Limited MBS items; gaps in some tumour types |
| NSCLC targeted therapies (osimertinib, alectinib, etc.) | Multi-gene panel | Yes from July 2025 (new CGP item) |

The capivasertib case is illustrative. Listed on the PBS from October 2025 for HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer with PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations, capivasertib requires a somatic tumour test to identify the relevant mutation. No MBS item exists for PIK3CA somatic testing in breast cancer as of 2026. Patients in the public system may access this through Omico; patients in private practice may need an out-of-pocket somatic panel test. The Australian Prescriber article on capivasertib (2026) notes this testing requirement but does not detail the funding mechanism, reflecting that the funding architecture is not yet settled.

### 6. The Genetic Counselling Bottleneck

Genomic testing is not a standalone laboratory transaction. Clinical guidelines require pre-test genetic counselling (to establish appropriateness and informed consent) and post-test counselling (to interpret results, discuss implications, and facilitate cascade testing of family members). Medicare funds genetic counsellor services only through a **geneticist consultation** model — there is no direct MBS funding for genetic counsellors (as distinct from clinical geneticists).

Australia has approximately **550–600 accredited genetic counsellors** (Human Genetics Society of Australasia 2024), concentrated in major metropolitan Familial Cancer Centres. Workforce capacity is insufficient for the volume of patients now eligible for germline testing, particularly as BRCA and related panel testing has expanded:

- Waiting times at Familial Cancer Centres for non-urgent genetic counselling: **3–12 months** in most states
- Telehealth-based genetic counselling has expanded since COVID-19 but remains inconsistent in remote areas
- No Medicare item funds asynchronous or digital genetic counselling models at scale
- GPs, breast specialists and oncologists can order BRCA testing without genetic counsellor involvement (as the MBS allows) but often lack capacity to deliver pre- and post-test counselling

The National Framework acknowledges this gap explicitly: "routine access to genetic counselling" is listed as a requirement, but the workforce and funding model to deliver it are not specified.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Research funding vs. entitlement funding:** The PrOSPeCT program provides what should be a standard clinical service through a research registration mechanism. This is efficient in the short term but does not constitute a patient entitlement — it can be defunded, restricted, or geographically excluded in ways that Medicare items cannot. The $71 million extension to 2028-29 defers the question of structural Medicare integration.

**2. The NSCLC MBS item as a test case:** The July 2025 NSCLC CGP item is the first of its kind and will provide real-world data on MBS CGP utilisation, cost, and quality. If the item demonstrates cost-effectiveness (through treatment matching and avoiding sequential testing costs), it should be the template for colorectal cancer, breast cancer, biliary cancer, and other tumour types with actionable targets. MSAC will be the decision-maker for each extension.

**3. Equity by cancer type:** Access to precision oncology testing is currently more equitable in NSCLC (new CGP item) and hereditary breast/ovarian cancer (established BRCA pathway) than in other tumour types. Patients with less common cancers or cancers with fewer advocacy groups may face longer waits for MBS coverage of clinically necessary tests.

**4. Private vs. public access asymmetry:** Patients enrolled in Omico/PrOSPeCT tend to be at participating academic public hospital centres. Private oncology patients, particularly those outside metro areas, are less likely to be enrolled and may face out-of-pocket costs for CGP if their oncologist does not have an Omico relationship.

**5. Indigenous data sovereignty:** The National Framework explicitly raises Indigenous Data Sovereignty as a consideration in genomic data collection — a significant issue given that genomic data from Indigenous cancer patients has research value but must be governed by communities. The tension between large-scale molecular registries (Omico's model) and Indigenous data sovereignty principles is not yet resolved in Australian policy.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **MSAC Application 1634 and the NSCLC CGP item as a template: what comes next?** Which tumour types are being assessed for MBS-funded CGP? What is the MSAC pipeline for precision oncology testing items, and how does Australia compare with NICE's molecular diagnostics evaluation program and France's INCa molecular platform?

2. **Genetic counselling workforce: the bottleneck in hereditary cancer access.** With 550 genetic counsellors for a continent of 27 million — and waiting lists of up to 12 months at Familial Cancer Centres — the BRCA testing pathway's clinical value is bottlenecked by post-test counselling capacity. Who trains genetic counsellors, how many does Australia need, and what would fund the gap?

3. **PHI and the BreastScreen diagnostic aftermarket: the cost of being recalled.** Women recalled from BreastScreen for abnormal results enter a diagnostic pathway — breast MRI, specialist consultation, image-guided biopsy — that is partially Medicare-funded but often carries significant specialist gap fees. Dense-breast notification reform (from Report 092) will expand this population. How much does a BreastScreen-triggered diagnostic workup cost, and does PHI cover it?

4. **Cascade genetic testing for families: the preventive opportunity Australia isn't funding well.** A patient identified with a germline BRCA2 pathogenic variant has, on average, 0.5 affected siblings and 0.25 affected parents still alive. Cascade testing of biological relatives (now MBS-funded via items 73303/73304) is among the highest-return preventive interventions in oncology. How many cascade tests are being performed relative to the probands identified, and is the funding model adequate?

5. **Pembrolizumab, MSI testing, and the "tumour agnostic" PBS listing frontier.** Pembrolizumab (Keytruda) is PBS-listed for MSI-H solid tumours across multiple indications. MSI testing is not uniformly funded across all relevant tumour types. As "tumour agnostic" drug approvals become more common globally — targeting molecular markers rather than tissue of origin — the MBS will need to fund the testing to identify these patients. What does Australia's framework for tumour-agnostic therapies look like?
