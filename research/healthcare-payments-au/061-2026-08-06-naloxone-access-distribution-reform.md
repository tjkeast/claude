# Naloxone Access and Distribution Reform: Australia's Life-Saving Drug and the Gaps That Still Kill People

**Report 061 — 2026-08-06**

**Summary:** Australia's Take-Home Naloxone (THN) Program has achieved genuinely significant progress since its national launch in July 2022 — pharmacy stocking has risen from 23% to 73%, ~3,000 doses were used in the first year alone, and new channels including vending machines and postal services are emerging. But with opioid overdose deaths now exceeding 2,272 per year (2023 data), with nitazenes appearing in drug supplies that may require multiple naloxone doses to reverse, and with xylazine — which naloxone cannot reverse at all — detected in more than one in four Australian wastewater samples, the question is whether a $19.6 million program distributed through pharmacies and harm reduction services is structurally adequate for the threat landscape. The evidence says it is not.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 060 documented that nitazenes have been detected in every Australian state and territory, that 33 Australians have died from nitazene exposure since 2021, and that xylazine — a veterinary sedative naloxone cannot reverse — is present in 26% of wastewater samples. It flagged that standard naloxone administration may be insufficient for these overdoses and that the distribution gap is a critical vulnerability. This report examines how naloxone access currently works, where the gaps are, what innovation is underway, and what structural reform would actually match the scale of the problem.

---

## Background

### What Naloxone Is and Why It Matters

Naloxone (brand names Nyxoid, Prenoxad; generic nasal spray under THN Program) is an opioid antagonist — it competitively blocks opioid receptors and rapidly reverses the respiratory depression that kills people in opioid overdose. Administered intranasally (the most common community route), it typically takes effect within 2–5 minutes. It has a benign safety profile: if administered to someone who has not taken opioids, it has no effect.

For most conventional opioid overdoses — heroin, morphine, oxycodone — naloxone works reliably at the doses distributed through Australia's THN Program (a 400mcg/mL intranasal spray, typically two doses in a kit). It has been a transformative harm reduction intervention: the first generation of community take-home naloxone programs, beginning in the late 1990s in Italy and expanding through the US, UK, Europe and Australia, demonstrated that non-medical bystanders could use it effectively with minimal training, and that widespread distribution reduced overdose mortality.

The complications now arriving from the synthetic opioid landscape — described in detail in Report 060 — raise new questions about whether the doses distributed, the locations naloxone is placed, and the knowledge bystanders have are adequate for a world where nitazenes contaminate stimulant supplies and xylazine adulterates opioid products.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Problem: A Decade of More Than 2,000 Deaths Per Year

Australia has been recording more than 2,000 overdose deaths per year every year since 2014. According to the Penington Institute's *Annual Overdose Report 2025*, there were **2,272 overdose deaths in 2023**, the equivalent of over six Australians every day. Since 2001, there have been **42,526 overdose deaths** in Australia — a figure that far exceeds road fatalities over the same period. Opioids remain the most common drug involved in unintentional overdose deaths.

Preliminary 2024 data, compiled across multiple sources, indicates an even higher toll. Penington Institute's *Overdose Snapshot 2025* and media reports of SBS analysis pointed to an estimated **2,596 drug-induced deaths in 2024** — if confirmed, the highest annual toll on record. The rate has risen to **7.6 per 100,000** — the highest since the data series began in 2001.

Crucially, this toll is occurring while naloxone distribution is scaling up. The two trends are running in parallel: naloxone access is improving, but so is the drug supply's lethality. The implication is not that naloxone doesn't work; it is that distribution cannot keep pace with the growing complexity and volume of the threat.

### 2. The Take-Home Naloxone Program: What It Is and How It Works

Australia's national THN Program, launched **1 July 2022** by the Commonwealth Department of Health and Aged Care, made naloxone available **free of charge, without a prescription**, to:
- People at risk of opioid overdose
- Carers, friends, and family members of people at risk
- Anyone who may witness an opioid overdose

The program was funded at **$19.6 million over four years** in the 2022–23 Federal Budget — approximately $4.9 million per year. Approved providers under the program include:
- Community pharmacies (the dominant channel)
- Hospital-based pharmacies
- Approved medical practitioners
- **Authorised Alternative Suppliers (AAS)**: alcohol and other drug treatment services, needle and syringe programs (NSPs), injecting centres, and homelessness/outreach services

Pharmacies and AAS providers are reimbursed for naloxone supplied under the THN Program through the Pharmacy Programs Administrator (PPA). The supply is reported via the PPA portal, giving the Commonwealth visibility on distribution volumes.

The THN Program was not built from scratch. It formalised and scaled state programs that had operated in NSW, Victoria, WA, and SA in various forms since 2012. The national rollout standardised eligibility and removed prescription requirements everywhere.

**In its first year (1 July 2022 – 30 June 2023)**, the Pharmaceutical Society of Australia (PSA) reported that an estimated **3,000 doses were used** in the community to reverse opioid overdose — approximately 8 uses per day. Pharmacists distributed more than **90% of all naloxone** through the program in this period.

### 3. Pharmacy Stocking: Progress and Persistent Gaps

The story of naloxone stocking in Australian pharmacies is one of dramatic improvement accompanied by persistent, geographically concentrated gaps.

In **2015**, a national pharmacy survey found that only **23% of community pharmacies** stocked naloxone — even though it had been available without prescription since February 2016 (Schedule 3). This reflected a combination of stigma, prescriber-level barriers, and lack of awareness.

By **2025**, UNSW's National Drug and Alcohol Research Centre (NDARC) *Drug Trends* reports documented that naloxone stocking had reached **73% of Australian community pharmacies** — making it the most commonly provided harm reduction service in Australian pharmacies, surpassing needle and syringe provision.

This represents a greater-than-threefold increase in a decade, largely driven by the 2022 national THN Program launch. The PSA has run targeted awareness and training campaigns for pharmacists, and the removal of reimbursement barriers lowered the cost to pharmacies of holding stock.

However, the remaining **27% of pharmacies not stocking naloxone** are not randomly distributed. Research published in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Karthikeyan et al., 2024) on a cross-sectional survey of 530 community pharmacies across Victoria, NSW, Queensland, and WA found that pharmacies providing opioid agonist treatment (OAT — methadone, buprenorphine) were significantly more likely to stock naloxone. This creates a troubling geographic pattern: rural and regional pharmacies, which are also less likely to provide OAT, are less likely to stock naloxone — in precisely the areas where alternative distribution channels (NSPs, injecting centres) are absent.

The **Authorised Alternative Supplier (AAS) pathway** — which allows NSPs, outreach services, and homelessness organisations to supply naloxone free — extends distribution beyond pharmacies, but its reach is also geographically uneven. AAS providers are concentrated in metropolitan areas and inner-regional centres. In MM3–7 Modified Monash communities (a population of roughly 7 million Australians), the combination of thin pharmacy stock and absent AAS networks creates genuine dead zones for naloxone access.

### 4. Who Is Getting Naloxone — and Who Is Not

The 2025 NDARC *Illicit Drug Reporting System (IDRS)* survey of people who inject drugs — the population at highest risk of opioid overdose — showed strong progress in awareness and access:
- **86%** had heard of naloxone (up from 80% in 2024)
- **80%** had heard of take-home naloxone programs (up from 73%)
- **65%** had obtained naloxone at least once in their lifetime (up from 54%)
- **55%** had obtained naloxone in the past year (up from 46%)
- **32%** had used naloxone to resuscitate someone at least once

These are positive trends, but they also reveal the gap. Fifteen percent of the highest-risk population — people who inject drugs — have *never* obtained naloxone. Forty-five percent did not obtain it in the past year.

More concerning is the **witness problem**. Many overdose deaths occur when a witness is present but does not have naloxone. The Penington Institute has documented that overdoses are frequently bystander events — occurring in homes, in shared spaces, in the presence of family or friends who do not have access to the antidote. The THN Program targets "anyone who may witness an opioid overdose," but pharmacy-based distribution creates a friction point: a person must proactively enter a pharmacy, identify themselves as at risk or as a potential witness, and ask for the medication. For people without existing harm reduction service relationships, this barrier remains meaningful.

### 5. Victoria's Vending Machine Innovation and the 24/7 Access Problem

The most significant structural innovation in Australian naloxone distribution in 2025–26 has been **Victoria's naloxone vending machine trial**, operating under the state's **$95.11 million Statewide Action Plan to Reduce Drug Harms**.

The Victorian Government has deployed **20 naloxone vending machines** across the state — 10 in metropolitan Melbourne and 10 in regional and rural communities — available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Each machine dispenses a naloxone nasal spray kit containing two single-dose sprays, free of charge. Current locations include the Alfred Emergency and Trauma Centre, Sunshine Hospital (Western Health, Brimbank), Bayside Health in Melbourne, and additional sites being rolled out. The trial runs until **June 2027**.

The 24/7 availability dimension addresses a critical gap in pharmacy-based distribution. Pharmacies are closed for significant parts of the day and weekend. Overdoses do not cluster during business hours: they occur at night, on weekends, in social settings and in private homes. A person seeking naloxone at 2am on a Saturday has no pharmacy option in most Australian communities. Vending machines in hospital emergency departments, homelessness services, and community centres fill this gap — though 20 machines across a state with 6.8 million people and 5.9 million square kilometres is a beginning, not a solution.

The Statewide Action Plan also includes Victoria's first **overdose prevention and response helpline**, the appointment of Victoria's first **Chief Addiction Medicine Adviser**, and the delivery of Victoria's first statewide drug harm reduction strategy — making Victoria, as Report 060 noted, the de facto harm reduction state in Australia.

### 6. Postal Distribution: Reaching Beyond the Pharmacy Network

A complementary innovation targeting geographic barriers is **postal naloxone distribution**. Two models have emerged:

**NSW**: The NSW Users and AIDS Association (NUAA) established a pilot **online postal THN service** allowing people to register online, watch an educational video, complete an assessment, and receive naloxone by post. Research published in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Harrod et al., 2025) evaluating the NUAA service found it substantially improved access for people who faced barriers to face-to-face services — including geography, stigma, and confidentiality concerns. The service reached populations who had not previously accessed naloxone through pharmacy or NSP channels.

**Western Australia**: **Peer Based Harm Reduction WA** operates a postal naloxone service covering the entire state. After a 10–15 minute brief education session by phone, the organisation sends free naloxone via Australia Post to anywhere in WA — a significant reach in a state where the nearest pharmacy may be hours away.

These services represent a different distribution philosophy: moving from a model that requires the person to present to a physical location, to one that meets people where they are. The evidence supports expanding both the postal model and digital-first registration.

### 7. The ANZCOR Guideline Change: Mainstreaming Naloxone in First Aid

One of the most significant policy developments in 2025 was the **Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation (ANZCOR) guideline update**, which came into effect in **July 2025**. From July 2025, all first aid courses in Australia and New Zealand are required to include instruction on the administration of intranasal naloxone as part of the standard first aid curriculum.

This is a population-level intervention. First aid training reaches tens of millions of Australians through workplaces, schools, sporting clubs, and community organisations. Embedding naloxone administration into first aid training normalises it — moves it from a "drug user service" to a mainstream emergency preparedness skill, analogous to CPR — and creates a population of trained bystanders who are prepared to respond to an opioid overdose. The Penington Institute and others have recommended following the WA Police Force's model of including naloxone in all first responder kits, which builds on the ANZCOR change.

The critical gap is that the ANZCOR guideline changes the training standard but does not automatically supply naloxone to trained first-aiders. A person who completes a first aid course including naloxone training, but who does not then obtain a kit from a pharmacy or program, is not a functional naloxone carrier.

### 8. The Synthetic Opioid Problem: Is Standard Naloxone Enough?

The emergence of nitazenes in the Australian drug supply raises a question that the evidence does not yet definitively answer: is the standard THN Program naloxone kit adequate for synthetic opioid overdoses?

The pharmacology is concerning. Some nitazene compounds — particularly benzimidazole opioids like metonitazene and etonitazene — bind to mu-opioid receptors with extremely high affinity and dissociate slowly. This could mean that the competitive antagonism provided by standard naloxone doses is overcome more rapidly than in a heroin overdose, requiring higher or more frequent doses to maintain reversal. A NUAA case report described a client who required **five doses of naloxone plus CPR** before regaining consciousness from a nitazene overdose.

Research published in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Nielsen et al., 2026 — *Behavioural Effects and Naloxone Effectiveness With New Synthetic Opioids*) examined the evidence on this question. The picture is mixed: while some synthetic opioids clearly require higher or more frequent naloxone dosing, the review also found that standard doses can be effective, and that the key message should be to administer naloxone and be prepared to give additional doses. The concern about "fentanyl-resistant naloxone" — a claim circulating in some US harm reduction contexts — is not supported by the evidence; naloxone remains effective against fentanyl and nitazenes, but may need to be administered more than once.

The **xylazine problem** is categorical, not pharmacological. Xylazine is *not an opioid* and cannot be reversed by naloxone under any dose. When xylazine is combined with an opioid product (as it increasingly is in Australian drug supplies — detected in 26% of wastewater samples nationally), naloxone will reverse the opioid component of the overdose but leave the xylazine-induced respiratory and CNS depression ongoing. A bystander or first responder who sees naloxone fail to fully reverse an apparent opioid overdose may escalate to emergency services — but the key point is that naloxone should still be administered, because it will address the opioid component, while additional emergency response is needed for the xylazine component.

Neither the standard THN Program kit nor the community training accompanying it currently addresses xylazine specifically. The public health messaging has not caught up with the wastewater detection data.

### 9. The Funding Gap: $19.6 Million for a 2,000-Deaths-Per-Year Problem

The structural underfunding of naloxone distribution is significant. The Commonwealth's THN Program budget of $19.6 million over four years represents approximately $4.9 million per year — comparable to the staffing costs of a single tertiary hospital clinical department. Against a toll of 2,272+ deaths per year, this is implausibly small.

The ACT Government's March 2025 **cost-benefit analysis of harm reduction services** provides a useful benchmark. The analysis found that maintaining needle and syringe programs, take-home naloxone, opioid agonist treatment, and drug checking services in the ACT would cost **$24.6 million over 2026–2030** (for a population of ~470,000 people) and avert 454 opioid and 20 non-opioid overdoses, preventing **70 overdose-related deaths**. This represents compelling value for money in health economic terms.

Scaling this to a national level is not straightforward (the ACT has higher-than-average harm reduction infrastructure per capita), but the proportional gap is clear. Australia's $4.9 million per year federal THN Program budget would, on ACT-equivalent estimates, cover a population of roughly 100,000 people. The remaining 25.6 million Australians are served by pharmacies (which are reimbursed per supply), the existing PBS listing, and state programs.

The Penington Institute's *Annual Overdose Report 2025* called specifically for:
1. All first responders in Australia to carry naloxone (following the WA Police Force model)
2. Naloxone to be made available in libraries, social services, hospitals, police first aid, and home first aid kits
3. A comprehensive **national overdose prevention strategy** to replace the current piecemeal approach

No national overdose prevention strategy, separate from the broader National Drug Strategy (whose consultation for a 2027 successor is ongoing), has been announced by the Commonwealth as of August 2026.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The pharmacy-centric model and its limits.** Australia's THN Program is structurally pharmacy-dependent — pharmacists dispense 90%+ of naloxone. Pharmacies are only open for a fraction of the day, are unevenly distributed geographically, and create a friction point for people without existing healthcare relationships. The vending machine and postal models are promising supplements but remain small-scale and state-specific.

**2. Who carries naloxone vs who needs it.** The growing awareness of naloxone among people who inject drugs is positive, but overdose deaths disproportionately affect people who are unknown to harm reduction services, people in middle age (the 50–59 age group now records the highest number of deaths), and people in rural/regional areas. None of these groups are well-served by the current distribution architecture.

**3. The xylazine messaging gap.** Naloxone awareness campaigns universally position naloxone as the response to opioid overdose. They do not address xylazine — a substance present in more than one in four Australian wastewater samples — which cannot be reversed by naloxone. Bystanders trained to administer naloxone need to know that if it does not produce full reversal, they must call emergency services immediately; the opioid component may be reversed while xylazine-induced effects continue.

**4. Dose adequacy for synthetic opioids.** The standard naloxone kit distributed through the THN Program contains 400mcg/mL nasal spray. For synthetic opioid overdoses involving high-affinity compounds, this may be insufficient. Higher-dose formulations exist internationally (8mg intranasal is available in some US jurisdictions), but a US study (MMWR, 2024) found no benefit of 8mg over 4mg in law enforcement use. The evidence remains uncertain. The practical recommendation — administer what you have, repeat if needed, call emergency services — is reasonable, but public education has not emphasised repeat dosing clearly enough.

**5. The national strategy gap.** There is no Australian equivalent of the EU's Early Warning System for novel psychoactive substances, and no National Overdose Prevention Strategy. The National Drug Strategy provides a broad framework, but its successor (being developed as of mid-2026) has not committed to specific naloxone distribution targets, first-responder coverage, or synthetic opioid preparedness measures. Without binding targets, the patchwork state-by-state approach will persist.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — consultation underway; whether the new strategy will include binding targets for naloxone access, synthetic opioid preparedness, and harm reduction funding; the political economy of strategy renewal under a government committed to "balanced" drug policy
2. **Victoria's drug policy infrastructure** — as the state with the most nitazene deaths (24) and 20 naloxone vending machines, Victoria is running the most ambitious harm reduction experiment in Australia; its policy architecture, outcomes data, and implications for other jurisdictions
3. **First Nations communities and synthetic opioid risk** — the particular danger for remote communities with no drug checking access, lower naloxone distribution, limited emergency response capacity, and triple the overdose mortality rate of non-Indigenous Australians
4. **Supervised injecting centres: economics and expansion** — the Sydney Medically Supervised Injecting Centre has been operating since 2001; the evidence on overdose prevention, hospital diversion, and cost-effectiveness; whether the vending machine model and MSIC model together form a coherent harm reduction architecture
5. **Xylazine as an emerging regulatory and clinical challenge** — whether Australia should regulate xylazine use (currently unscheduled in veterinary supply chains), how emergency services should respond to xylazine-adulterated overdoses, and whether wound care for xylazine-associated skin necrosis (a documented complication overseas) is being addressed
