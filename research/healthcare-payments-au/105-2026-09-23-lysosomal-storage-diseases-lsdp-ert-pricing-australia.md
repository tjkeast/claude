# Lysosomal Storage Diseases and the LSDP: Australia's Most Expensive Medicines and the Cost-Effectiveness Threshold It Cannot Apply

**Summary:** Australia funds enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) for lysosomal storage diseases through a separate program — the Life Saving Drugs Program (LSDP) — precisely because these medicines cannot pass the PBS cost-effectiveness test. In 2016–17, the LSDP covered 393 patients at a cost of $116 million (~$295,000/patient/year). Since then, the patient population has grown, ERT costs have risen, and a new wave of gene therapies threatens to remake the economics entirely. A major structural transition is underway: some Fabry disease ERT has moved to PBS from August 2026, but the broader system — and Australia's access record for orphan medicines (ranked 14th of 15 comparator countries) — remains under pressure.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 104 noted that MSAC rejected newborn bloodspot screening for Pompe disease in 2025 — partly because the same LSDP-funded ERT it would detect is both enormously expensive and of uncertain benefit in late-onset cases. That intersection of NBS, LSDP, and ERT cost opens directly onto Australia's most expensive medicines and the funding architecture holding them.

---

## Background: What Are Lysosomal Storage Diseases?

Lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) are a family of approximately 50 inherited metabolic disorders caused by deficiencies in lysosomal enzymes — proteins responsible for breaking down complex molecules inside cells. Without functional enzymes, substrates accumulate progressively in cells across multiple organ systems, causing a spectrum of disability and, in severe forms, early death.

LSDs collectively affect approximately 1 in 5,000–7,000 live births, making any single condition extremely rare — hence "ultra-rare." The main disease groups include:

- **Gaucher disease** (glucocerebrosidase deficiency): most common LSD; types I, II, III. Type I is a chronic systemic form without neurological involvement, amenable to ERT.
- **Fabry disease** (alpha-galactosidase A deficiency): affects heart, kidney, nervous system; X-linked with variable penetrance in females.
- **Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS)**: a sub-family of six named types (I–VII) caused by different enzyme deficiencies — MPS I (Hurler/Hurler-Scheie/Scheie), MPS II (Hunter syndrome), MPS IVA (Morquio A), MPS VI (Maroteaux-Lamy), and others. Most cause progressive multisystem disease; several cause severe intellectual disability and early death if untreated.
- **Pompe disease** (acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency): affects muscle and heart. Infantile-onset is rapidly fatal without treatment; late-onset is a progressive myopathy.

Enzyme replacement therapy — recombinant versions of the deficient enzyme administered by IV infusion, typically fortnightly — became available for Gaucher disease (imiglucerase, Cerezyme; Genzyme, 1994), triggering a wave of ERT development across the LSD family. These were among the first drugs specifically developed under orphan drug legislation, meaning they carried regulatory advantages (market exclusivity, expedited review) but also high development costs recouped across very small patient populations.

## The LSDP: Why a Separate Program Exists

Australia's Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) uses a cost-effectiveness framework to list medicines. The Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) evaluates incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), and while there is no published explicit threshold, industry convention assumes an implicit ceiling of approximately AUD $50,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY).

ERT for LSDs fails this test comprehensively. A typical Gaucher ERT costs approximately AUD $200,000–$310,000 per patient per year. MPS IVA treatment (elosulfase alfa, Vimizim) is among the world's most expensive medicines — international data suggests annual costs of USD $2.08 million to $6.24 million per patient, depending on weight-based dosing. Even at the lower end of the LSD ERT cost spectrum, no standard ICER calculation can justify these costs against the modest QALY gains attainable with chronic infusion therapy.

The Life Saving Drugs Program (LSDP) was created to resolve this tension. The PBAC assesses LSDP medicines for clinical effectiveness — not cost-effectiveness — and the Commonwealth funds those it judges as "clinically necessary and effective" for conditions where otherwise no treatment would be available and the disease is life-threatening. The government then negotiates confidential price-volume arrangements with manufacturers, but the prices remain far above any PBS-compatible threshold.

### Key LSDP parameters (as at mid-2026):

- **Funding structure:** Section 100 (highly specialised) supply through designated specialist centres; medicines provided at no cost to patients.
- **Eligibility:** Patients must be diagnosed by a specialist at an LSDP-accredited site and meet published disease-specific criteria (e.g., for Gaucher disease type 1: symptomatic disease with organ involvement; for MPS II: confirmed Hunter syndrome diagnosis with documented symptoms).
- **Review mechanism:** Medicines reviewed by the LSDP Expert Panel within 24 months of listing; expert panel advises the Chief Medical Officer.
- **Scale (2016–17 baseline):** 393 patients; total cost $116 million (~$295,000 per patient per year). Patient numbers and costs have grown since.

## Key Findings

### 1. The PBAC/PBS threshold problem — and why the LSDP is an elegant workaround with real costs

Australia's dual-pathway architecture represents an internationally distinctive policy choice. Most comparable countries either apply the same HTA framework to rare diseases (sometimes with modifiers), use named-patient compassionate access programs, or have separate rare disease policies. Australia chose to codify an explicit bypass: if PBAC finds a medicine clinically necessary but not cost-effective, it may be funded through a separate ministerial appropriation.

This has important consequences:

- **No upper bound on cost:** The LSDP does not apply a cost per QALY ceiling, meaning there is no principled limit to what the government might commit to fund. Elosulfase alfa for MPS IVA — potentially among the ten most expensive medicines in the world per treated patient — is funded with no disclosed price.
- **Small absolute numbers, large absolute spend:** Australia has approximately 25–50 patients with MPS IVA at any time. Even with negotiated discounts, government spending on elosulfase alfa alone likely runs into tens of millions annually.
- **Precedent pressure:** Each new ERT approval elsewhere creates advocacy pressure for LSDP listing. The Expert Panel's 2-year review mechanism and the Expert Panel's advisory role (advising the CMO, not making binding decisions) means listing decisions involve considerable ministerial discretion.
- **Monitoring and value recovery:** Post-market monitoring requirements exist, but formal outcomes-based rebate mechanisms (outcomes guarantees beyond utilisation-based arrangements) have been limited.

### 2. Australia's orphan drug access record is among the worst in peer countries

A July 2026 IQVIA report — funded by Alexion in Australia — tracking the pathway from orphan drug designation to patient access found:

- Only **39% of medicines** granted orphan drug designation have progressed to reimbursement in Australia over the analysis period.
- The **average time from TGA registration to PBS/LSDP reimbursement** was **22 months**.
- **78% of reimbursed orphan drugs** required more than one HTA submission before achieving a positive recommendation.
- Australia ranked **14th of 15 comparator countries** by number of orphan medicines funded.

These figures relate to all orphan medicines, not only LSDs — rare cancers, rare metabolic diseases, and other conditions are included. But they contextualise the LSDP: even with a dedicated bypass mechanism, Australia's overall access record for rare disease medicines is poor by OECD peer standards. The LSDP appears to assist those with the most well-characterised, severe conditions with established ERT, but a large number of rarer or more recently characterised conditions access neither PBS nor LSDP pathways in timely fashion.

### 3. The Fabry disease transition — a model for PBS graduation?

A notable structural development occurred in 2026. From **1 August 2026**, pegunigalsidase alfa (Elfabrio, Chiesi) — a newer, PEGylated enzyme replacement therapy for Fabry disease offering less frequent infusions — was listed on the PBS. New patients with Fabry disease are no longer eligible to commence treatment through the LSDP from that date.

Separately, the LSDP Expert Panel referred agalsidase alfa (Replagal) and agalsidase beta (Fabrazyme) — the two legacy ERTs for Fabry disease — to the PBAC for consideration of PBS listing at its **July 2026 PBAC meeting**. This represents a potential full "graduation" of Fabry disease treatment from LSDP to PBS.

This is significant for several reasons:

- **Precedent:** It establishes that LSDP medicines can move to PBS. The pathway appears to be: newer generation treatment gains PBS listing; older treatments then considered for PBS transfer so LSDP is freed of that condition entirely.
- **Economics:** If PBS listing comes with confidential price-volume agreements (risk-sharing arrangements with rebates), the PBS framework can accommodate medicines that technically have unacceptable ICERs under confidential commercial terms that are not disclosed publicly.
- **Implications for hereditary tyrosinaemia Type 1 (nitisinone/Orfadin):** This condition also transitioned from LSDP to PBS from August 2026, confirming graduation pathways are being actively managed.

The question is whether similar transitions are feasible for the most expensive LSDs (MPS IVA, MPS II) where ERT costs are so high that even confidential PBS rebate arrangements would be commercially challenging.

### 4. The gene therapy horizon — a fundamental disruption to the ERT economics

Perhaps the most important structural development facing the LSDP is the emergence of gene therapies for conditions currently funded through it.

Historically, ERT for LSDs was a lifelong commitment: patients receive IV infusions fortnightly, typically for decades. For a patient diagnosed in infancy with Gaucher disease type 1, this could mean 60+ years of treatment — a potential cumulative cost exceeding AUD $15–18 million per patient at current ERT prices.

Gene therapies promise a one-time or infrequent treatment that restores adequate enzyme production. Clinical development is most advanced for:

- **Gaucher disease:** Multiple gene therapy programs in Phase II/III trials globally (2025–26). A one-time treatment at USD $2–4 million (the pricing range established by Zolgensma for SMA) would be cost-saving against decades of ERT, potentially dramatically so.
- **Pompe disease:** Avalglucosidase alfa (Nexviazyme, Sanofi) — already a next-generation ERT providing substantially better outcomes than alglucosidase alfa — is LSDP-funded from 2023. Gene therapies for Pompe are in earlier development.
- **MPS I and II:** First gene therapy regulatory approvals occurred in the UK and US in 2024–25. MPS I in particular has a gene therapy (OTL-203, now gilteritinib-alfa; Orchard Therapeutics/Kyowa Kirin) that received EMA conditional approval and US FDA orphan drug designation. The LSDP currently funds laronidase (Aldurazyme) for MPS I.
- **Fabry disease:** GC101 (Freeline) and other gene therapies are in Phase I/II trials.

The LSDP and PBAC face an emerging challenge: one-time gene therapies at AUD $3–5 million per patient may be cost-effective over a 20-year horizon compared with ERT, but the upfront cost is enormous and the evidence base at the time of first listing decisions will be limited (small trials, uncertain durability of benefit). Australia's infrastructure for evaluating and funding one-time curative therapies — the amortised payment models, outcomes guarantees, and budget management tools — remains under-developed relative to this challenge (this is a thread directly related to Report 099's coverage of the National Genomics Strategy and the Zolgensma precedent for SMA).

### 5. The Pompe NBS-LSDP tension

Report 104 covered MSAC's 2025 rejection of newborn bloodspot screening for Pompe disease. The reasoning included that NBS would detect not only infantile-onset Pompe (always severe, always benefiting from early ERT) but also late-onset Pompe (variable, sometimes asymptomatic for decades), creating a population of presymptomatic children labelled with a disease and potentially started on very expensive ERT of uncertain benefit.

The MSAC estimated approximately 58 newborns would be recalled per year if NBS proceeded. Of these, a proportion would have late-onset variants for whom early treatment is not clearly beneficial. Starting these patients on ERT — at an LSDP-funded cost of hundreds of thousands of dollars per year — for an uncertain clinical benefit was assessed as likely to generate more harm (overtreatment anxiety, medical surveillance burden, financial) than good.

This encapsulates a broader tension in the LSDP architecture: the program is designed around therapeutic access for diagnosed, symptomatic patients. When NBS programmes expand the diagnosed population upstream to include presymptomatic cases, the LSDP's eligibility criteria (which typically require symptomatic disease) become the line of defence against potentially unlimited expenditure on cases with uncertain benefit.

### 6. International comparators — how others manage the same tension

Australia's approach has parallels internationally but notable differences:

- **England (NHSE Highly Specialised Technologies pathway):** A separate HTA pathway for ultra-rare conditions (prevalence <1/50,000), with an explicit QALY-based modifier that allows higher cost-effectiveness thresholds (up to £300,000/QALY). Outcomes guarantee arrangements are common.
- **Scotland (SMC ultra-orphan process):** Applies a severity- and burden-adjusted modifier; allows listing at ICERs that would be rejected for common conditions.
- **Canada (CADTH RARE):** A specialist rare disease review pathway with flexibility on evidence standards and economic methods.
- **New Zealand (Pharmac):** No separate rare disease pathway. Rare disease medicines compete within the same budget envelope as common medicines. This means many LSD treatments that are LSDP-funded in Australia are not subsidised in New Zealand at all, creating a striking trans-Tasman access gap.

Australia sits in an unusual position: it has a dedicated bypass mechanism (LSDP) that is more generous than PBAC-only access, but its overall orphan drug access record (14th of 15 comparators) suggests the broader system, including the PBS, delivers poor access for the majority of rare disease patients who don't have a condition covered by the LSDP.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The LSDP governance gap:** Decisions to list medicines on the LSDP involve the LSDP Expert Panel advising the CMO, with no binding cost-effectiveness criterion. This provides flexibility but also opacity. Unlike PBAC deliberations (which are public), LSDP listing rationales are not routinely published with the same level of transparency. As the program grows, calls for a formal deliberative framework with published criteria will intensify.

**2. The gene therapy transition:** Will Australia's system be able to fund one-time gene therapies at $3–5M for conditions currently incurring ongoing LSDP costs? The Zolgensma precedent (SMA, PBS-listed 2021) shows it can be done, but SMA NBS drove the decision logic. For conditions without NBS programmes (most LSDs), the pathway is less clear.

**3. Graduation to PBS:** The Fabry disease August 2026 transition suggests that LSDP → PBS graduation is being actively managed. But it depends on commercial terms the government can negotiate. For MPS IVA, where costs are at the extreme end of the global medicine cost distribution, PBS listing under any confidential risk-sharing arrangement may be arithmetically impossible without public cost transparency that manufacturers resist.

**4. The New Zealand comparison:** NZ's Pharmac model (covered in Report 007) funds most of its rare disease medicines via budget reallocation within a fixed envelope. Most ERT for LSDs is not funded. This creates an implicit ethical question: Is Australia's LSDP, which ring-fences unlimited funding for LSD patients, the right model? Or does it redirect resources that could serve larger numbers of patients with less rare conditions?

**5. Rare Voices Australia and the National Strategic Action Plan:** The original NSAP investment of $3.3 million looks tiny against an LSDP that has grown well past $116M/year. The Plan's priority of improving the funding pathway for rare disease medicines remains largely unaddressed at the structural level — the 14th-of-15 IQVIA ranking is a signal that LSDP fixes only the tail of the LSD problem, not the broader rare disease medicine access problem.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Gene therapy funding models — the amortised payment question:** How do health systems fund one-time curative therapies with multi-million-dollar price tags? Outcomes-based annuity models, budget impact management, the Zolgensma NBS-funding precedent, and what Australia has built (or hasn't) for the next wave.

2. **New Zealand vs Australia rare disease access:** The Pharmac model as the counter-example — NZ's fixed-budget approach denies most LSD ERT access; the trans-Tasman gap as an ethical and political tension.

3. **The PBAC cost-effectiveness framework under pressure:** How Australia decides what to fund — the implicit $50,000/QALY threshold, the modifiers used for severe conditions, confidential risk-sharing arrangements as the real access mechanism, and the accountability gap this creates.

4. **MPS and other LSDs still without effective treatment:** Many MPS types lack ERT entirely (MPS III/Sanfilippo syndrome, MPS VII/Sly syndrome). The natural history of untreated MPS III — progressive severe intellectual disability — highlights what the LSDP cannot currently address; clinical trials in 2025–26 for gene therapy are the primary hope.

5. **Rare disease patient registries and data governance:** Australia's national rare disease registries are fragmented; better data would improve LSDP evidence reviews, support outcomes-based rebate governance, and underpin next-generation gene therapy access decisions.
