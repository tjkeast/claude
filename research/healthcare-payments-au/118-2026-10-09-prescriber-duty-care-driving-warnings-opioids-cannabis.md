# Prescriber Duty of Care and Driving Warnings: The Opioid–Benzodiazepine–Cannabis Disclosure Gap

**One-line summary:** Australia's prescribers operate under a patchwork of weak and largely voluntary obligations to warn patients about driving on opioids, benzodiazepines, and cannabis — a clinical standard documented almost nowhere in the MBS billing architecture and observed in as few as 1% of opioid-initiation consultations.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 117 documented the structural legal asymmetry in Australian roadside drug testing — opioids (in 17.3% of Victorian fatal crashes) face only the impairment offence, while cannabis faces a presence offence. Today's report examines the upstream clinical gap: what are prescribers required to tell patients about driving, and is the payment system structured to support this disclosure?

---

## Background

Every time a Schedule 8 opioid or a Schedule 4 benzodiazepine is prescribed in Australia, the treating clinician enters a brief window in which they hold information — about sedation risk, tolerance timelines, and combinations — that the patient may not have. Whether and how that information is communicated determines whether the patient understands they are potentially impaired behind the wheel.

Australia's framework for managing this clinical moment is multi-layered but thin. The national standard is set by Austroads' *Assessing Fitness to Drive* (AP-G56-22), updated in June 2022. That document guides clinicians in all jurisdictions but carries no MBS item number, no mandatory documentation requirement in primary care, and no enforcement mechanism outside mandatory-reporting jurisdictions. The result is an expected-value problem: the risk is diffuse, the regulatory feedback loop is slow, and the billing incentives are silent.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Austroads Standard: Stable Doses May Not Impair — But the Clinical Picture Is Complex

The 2022 Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* guidelines state that patients on stable doses of opioid analgesics may not have a higher crash risk than the general population, **providing they are not misusing other impairing drugs**. This is the cornerstone of Australia's clinical position, and it is significantly more permissive than many clinicians appreciate.

The same guidelines draw important nuances:

- **Initiation and dose escalation are the highest-risk periods.** Neuroadaptation to opioids develops over two to three weeks; during this window, cognitive and psychomotor impairment can be clinically significant.
- **Combination drug use is the primary risk multiplier.** Opioids combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or sedating antidepressants produce markedly greater impairment than any single agent.
- **Methadone and buprenorphine (OST)** carry specific standards: patients on stable opioid substitution therapy for at least three months, not misusing other impairing drugs, may be eligible for an unconditional licence after assessment — but the treating practitioner must actively assess and document this.
- **Benzodiazepines** are separately flagged. Tolerance to sedative effects of long-acting benzodiazepines may reduce driving impairment over time, but daytime anxiolytic use during the first four weeks of treatment carries a strong advisory against driving.
- **Medicinal cannabis** is now addressed in the 2022 edition, reflecting the post-2016 SAS-B prescription growth. Cannabinoids can cause impairment, and patients may already be unfit to drive due to their underlying condition or concurrent treatments.

### 2. The Education Gap Is Severe: 1% of Opioid Initiations Include Driving Counselling

A 2023 peer-reviewed Australian study published in *BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care* examined 205 patients commenced on opioids at an Australian tertiary referral centre for advanced lung cancer (2015–2019):

- **Only 47 patients (23%)** had driving status documented at opioid initiation.
- **Only 2 patients (1%)** received driving education at opioid initiation.
- A further 10 patients (5%) received driving education at subsequent follow-up appointments.
- Opioid doses were frequently escalated at follow-up — the highest-risk moment — with limited documented driving safety review.

Lead investigator Natasha Smallwood noted that patients were arriving at appointments with little awareness of their impaired driving status while on opioids: "Whether conversations about opioids and driving were not happening or were happening and not being documented was unknown — but both cases were a problem."

While this study is limited to a tertiary cancer cohort, the results align with broader evidence that opioid driving counselling is rare in Australian general practice. A separate CARRS-Q (Queensland road safety research centre) survey found approximately **1 in 4 Australian drivers** continues to drive after taking prescription medicines that carry driving warning labels — suggesting that consumer-facing warnings are insufficient as a standalone intervention.

### 3. The Legal Obligation Is Fragmented and Jurisdiction-Dependent

Australia's prescriber driving-notification framework has three layers:

**Layer 1: Patient statutory duty (all jurisdictions).** In every state and territory, patients have a statutory obligation to report medical conditions that may affect their ability to drive to their driver licensing authority. Clinicians are required to *remind* patients of this obligation — but many do not.

**Layer 2: Prescriber mandatory reporting (SA and NT only).** Mandatory notification of unfit drivers to licensing authorities is legally required of health professionals in South Australia and the Northern Territory. In all other jurisdictions, reporting is **discretionary** — a clinician may notify a licensing authority but is not legally compelled to do so. The AMA has described this as placing practitioners in a difficult position between patient confidentiality and community safety.

**Layer 3: Good-faith protection (all jurisdictions except NT).** Clinicians who report in good faith are protected from civil and criminal liability in all jurisdictions except the Northern Territory, which has no express statutory protection.

Critically, none of this framework is linked to a specific MBS billing item. There is no MBS consultation item requiring — or financially incentivising — a documented driving safety assessment on opioid or benzodiazepine initiation. The existing framework relies entirely on clinicians absorbing this obligation within general consultation time, and on the Austroads training resources being accessed voluntarily.

### 4. The TGA Label Architecture: Pharmacists Hold the Warning System

Australia's principal formal safeguard for opioid-and-driving disclosure sits not with the prescriber but with the pharmacist. The TGA's opioid reform programme (2019–2021) required sponsors to add additional warning statements to Product Information and Consumer Medicine Information (CMI) documents for all opioids. These warnings are to be prominently displayed.

At the dispensing counter, pharmacists apply cautionary advisory labels (CALs) to opioid prescriptions. **Label 24** — introduced as part of the reform programme — warns patients of the risk of overdose and dependence. A separate label class warns of driving impairment, but pharmacist counselling at dispensing is the primary implementation mechanism.

The problem is structural: pharmacist counselling at the point of dispensing is a one-off, time-pressured interaction. It is not a substitute for a clinician's longitudinal assessment of driving fitness across the treatment course — particularly during dose titration, polypharmacy changes, or the addition of a new sedating agent.

For medicinal cannabis, the framework is different again. NSW Health's 2022 fact sheet directs prescribers to the Austroads guide. The prescribing practitioner is expected to assess fitness to drive at initiation and document it. But for cannabis — unlike opioids — patients in most jurisdictions face a legal presence offence if they drive with THC in their system, regardless of clinical fitness. This creates a disclosure asymmetry: prescribers of cannabis have a stronger clinical incentive to document the driving conversation (for medico-legal protection), while prescribers of opioids and benzodiazepines face only the weaker general duty.

### 5. The Medico-Legal Record: Infrequent but Signalling Exposure

Australian coronial findings have not yet established a clear prescriber duty of care for driving harm specifically, but the pattern of inquests points toward increasing scrutiny:

- **NSW Falkholt/Whitall Inquest (findings December 2021):** A driver with potentially fatal levels of prescription medication in his system was found responsible for a crash that killed four members of the Falkholt family in 2017. The driver's prescribers were separately represented. The inquest did not result in prescriber liability findings, but the fact that prescribing was under coronial scrutiny signals the legal direction.

- **NSW Hunter Bus Crash (June 2023):** Driver Brett Button pleaded guilty after crashing while impaired by tramadol. A 2022 pain specialist report had found him dependent on opioids and vulnerable to addiction. Manslaughter charges were withdrawn. Prescribers were not charged.

Avant Mutual's guidance to GPs frames the current exposure precisely: document the Austroads-standard assessment, remind the patient of their statutory reporting duty, and maintain a contemporaneous record. The absence of documentation — rather than the absence of the conversation — is what creates medico-legal vulnerability.

### 6. The MBS Payment System: No Item, No Incentive

There is no MBS consultation item specifically designed for or requiring a fitness-to-drive assessment in the context of opioid or sedating medicine prescribing. The assessment is absorbed into general consultation items (Level B/C/D), creating no documentation trail unless the clinician specifically records it.

Contrast this with:
- MBS Items **90089/90090** (advance care planning) — which fund a structured documentation consultation as a standalone billable service
- MBS chronic disease management items — which fund review consultations with structured outcomes

The absence of a driving-safety consultation item is not merely symbolic. Structured items create documentation habits, recall triggers, and audit trails. The current system outsources the entire driving-risk conversation to incidental clinical time.

For medicinal cannabis prescriptions via the SAS-B pathway — which generated 177,762 approvals in 2024 — the TGA's SAS-B approval form requires prescribers to acknowledge prescribing responsibilities but does not mandate driving documentation. Telehealth platforms issuing SAS-B approvals at scale (as examined in Report 113) have an even weaker incentive to conduct longitudinal fitness-to-drive assessments.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The "stable dose" assumption is doing too much work.** Austroads' permissive standard for stable opioid doses is built on limited evidence. Tolerance is highly individual; chronic users of opioids may have sub-clinical deficits in executive function that are invisible to both the patient and the clinician, particularly in complex traffic situations.

**2. Polypharmacy is the real risk — and is not currently flagged at initiation.** The main crash risk is combinations. A patient on a stable opioid who is then prescribed a benzodiazepine for sleep, or a sedating antidepressant, is not necessarily reassessed for driving fitness. The MBS does not create a trigger for reassessment on any new sedating prescription.

**3. The cannabis–opioid asymmetry in disclosure incentives creates a perverse substitution dynamic.** A GP prescribing cannabis has a strong medico-legal incentive to document a driving warning (because the presence offence makes it legally salient). A GP prescribing oxycodone faces weaker incentives. This is the mirror image of the roadside testing asymmetry identified in Report 116: the payment and liability systems both tilt toward the drug with lower crash prevalence.

**4. Telehealth prescribing has widened the gap.** The post-COVID expansion of telehealth for opioid management and the emergence of high-volume SAS-B platforms for medicinal cannabis have separated prescribing from the face-to-face longitudinal relationship in which fitness-to-drive conversations historically occurred.
