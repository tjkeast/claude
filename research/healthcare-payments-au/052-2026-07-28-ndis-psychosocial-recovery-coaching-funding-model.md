# Psychosocial Recovery Coaching Under the NDIS: A Parallel Funding World

**Report 052 — 2026-07-28**

**Summary:** Psychosocial recovery coaching is the NDIS's dedicated funded support for people whose disability stems from mental illness — introduced in July 2020, now available to approximately 65,000 participants with a primary psychosocial disability, billing at up to $110.44 per hour. But the NDIS-funded recovery coaching world operates on entirely different rules to the block-grant peer support world examined in Report 051: it requires provider registration, formal qualifications, and operates through a market mechanism rather than a grant. Despite this structural investment, it remains one of the most underused supports in the scheme, and it faces replacement by a new 'navigator' model proposed in the 2023 NDIS Review.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 051 showed that Australia's peer support workforce — funded through precarious block grants — is entirely excluded from Medicare. Psychosocial recovery coaching is the NDIS parallel: a specifically designed, government-funded support category for peer-and-recovery-oriented practice that does reach into formal funding infrastructure — but through the NDIS rather than Medicare, with its own qualification requirements, registration mandate, and market pricing structure that creates a two-tier world between those who qualify for the NDIS and those who do not.

---

## Background

When the NDIS was progressively rolled out from 2013, people with psychosocial disability — those whose primary disability is a mental health condition — were among the most difficult to serve well. The scheme was designed around relatively stable, lifelong disability, with supports oriented toward function and independence. Mental health recovery is different: it is often non-linear, episodic, and highly relationship-dependent. Initial NDIS frameworks did not serve this group well; participants with psychosocial disability consistently reported difficulties accessing the scheme, navigating its bureaucratic requirements, and using funded supports effectively.

In October 2019, the Disability Reform Council approved a new NDIS support item in direct response to this feedback: the **Psychosocial Recovery Coach**. Developed in consultation with Mental Health Australia and people with lived experience of psychosocial disability, the role launched in July 2020. Its governing practice guide (version 1.1, July 2020) defined a recovery-oriented coaching function that deliberately drew on mental health recovery principles rather than disability support coordination models.

The design represented a significant policy investment: rather than squeezing people with psychosocial disability into existing NDIS support categories, the government created a new category specifically shaped around mental health recovery logic.

## Key Findings

### 1. Who Accesses Psychosocial Recovery Coaching — and at What Scale

As of June 2023, approximately **62,000 NDIS participants** held a primary psychosocial disability designation — roughly **10% of all scheme participants**. By 2024-25, the cohort had grown to approximately 65,000. These participants are, on average, more expensive to support than other NDIS participants:

- **Total committed funding to psychosocial disability participants in 2024-25: $5.7 billion** — a 9% increase from $5.3 billion in 2023-24 (AIHW)
- **Average payment per psychosocial disability participant in 2024-25: $88,700** — approximately **35% higher** than the NDIS-wide average of $65,800, and a 7% increase from $83,100 in 2023-24

Psychosocial recovery coaching sits within **Support Category 7: Support Coordination**, under the registration group for Assistance In Coordinating Or Managing Life Stages, Transitions And Supports (item codes 07_101_0106_6_3 through 07_106_0106_6_3, covering daytime, night, Saturday, Sunday, and public holiday delivery). It is classified as a capacity-building support — meaning it is designed to build participants' ability to manage their own lives, not to substitute for clinical care.

### 2. Pricing: A Market Mechanism, Not a Block Grant

Recovery coaching is priced through the NDIS Pricing Arrangements and Price Limits (PAPL) — the same mechanism governing all NDIS supports. The published maximum rates for 2025-26 and 2026-27 are:

**2025-26 (effective 1 July 2025):**
- Weekday daytime: **$105.43/hr**
- Weekday night: **$118.31/hr**
- Sunday: **$191.29/hr**
- Public holiday: **$234.23/hr**

**2026-27 (effective 1 July 2026):**
- Weekday daytime: **$110.44/hr** (national); $154.62/hr (remote); $165.66/hr (very remote)
- Saturday: $148.36/hr

These rates are maximums. Registered providers cannot charge above them; plan-managed and self-managed participants may negotiate with providers. The PAPL rate for recovery coaching is indexed annually — unlike PHN block grants, which (as Report 050 showed) have chronically lagged behind SCHADS Award wage increases. This is the structural difference: an hourly billing mechanism creates a cost-pass-through that block grants do not.

The gap between the two funding worlds is stark. An NGO peer worker funded by a PHN block grant may earn $28-33/hr under the SCHADS Award. An NDIS-registered recovery coaching provider billing at $110.44/hr has a revenue stream from which wages, overheads, supervision, and margin must all be met — but the revenue per hour is substantially larger than what block grants generate.

### 3. Who Can Be a Psychosocial Recovery Coach — and the Lived Experience Pathway

The NDIS's qualification requirements for recovery coaches were designed to be deliberately inclusive of the lived experience workforce. The minimum requirements are:

- **Formal qualification pathway**: Certificate IV in Mental Health Peer Work, or Certificate IV in Mental Health, or equivalent training; OR
- **Experience pathway**: A minimum of two years of experience in mental health-related work

Participants are explicitly given the option to select a coach with lived experience — the NDIA's own practice guidance recognises that coaches with personal experience of mental illness can offer a distinctive contribution. This is the closest thing in the Australian funded-care landscape to a formal policy recognition of lived experience as a professional qualification.

In practice, the field draws on both pathways. Many recovery coaches combine a formal qualification (Certificate IV or higher) with personal lived experience. The dual perspective — knowing the system from the inside as well as from direct experience — is described in the sector as particularly valuable.

However, the requirement from **1 July 2025** that all recovery coaches must be registered with the **NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission** creates a tension: NDIS Commission registration requires organisational infrastructure, compliance capacity, and administrative overhead that individual peer workers and small community operators may struggle to meet. The pre-2025 arrangement — where plan-managed and self-managed participants could access unregistered recovery coaches — gave more flexibility to the lived experience workforce. The mandatory registration transition may reduce the participation of solo peer practitioners.

### 4. The Role: What Recovery Coaches Actually Do

The recovery coach role is defined around coaching, not coordination and not clinical care:

- **Recovery-oriented planning**: supporting the participant to articulate what a meaningful life looks like for them, and working out practical steps toward it
- **Capacity building**: strengthening the participant's ability to self-manage their NDIS plan and daily challenges
- **Service navigation**: connecting participants with NDIS and non-NDIS services (including mental health clinical services, peer support groups, community networks)
- **Goal tracking**: monitoring progress against recovery plans, adjusting over time
- **Crisis-adjacent support**: recognising signs of deterioration and facilitating connection to clinical support (not providing clinical care)

A key constraint shapes how recovery coaching is funded: the NDIA **generally will not fund both a support coordinator and a recovery coach** in the same plan, because coordination is a component of both roles. This creates a binary choice for participants — a logistical support coordinator to manage the complexity of the NDIS plan, or a recovery-focused coach to build capacity and navigate recovery. For participants with very complex plans, the absence of dedicated coordination alongside coaching is a documented pain point.

### 5. Underutilisation Despite Plan Funding

Despite being specifically designed for people with psychosocial disability and funded in their NDIS plans, recovery coaching is frequently described as **one of the most underused supports in the NDIS**. Barriers to utilisation include:

- **Supply shortage in some regions**: qualified and registered providers are concentrated in major urban centres; rural and remote access is limited
- **Participant engagement barriers**: people with psychosocial disability often face significant challenges navigating services, engaging with new support relationships, and managing administrative requirements — the same challenges that make recovery coaching valuable are barriers to accessing it
- **Role confusion**: participants (and sometimes planners) are uncertain how recovery coaching differs from support coordination or from clinical case management — the role sits in an unfamiliar conceptual space
- **Transition barriers**: changing to a new coach, or starting with a recovery coach for the first time, requires engagement capacity that fluctuates with mental health status

### 6. The 2023 NDIS Review and the Navigator Transition

The 2023 NDIS Review Final Report (released December 2023) recommended replacing the current landscape of support coordinators, specialist support coordinators, psychosocial recovery coaches, and Local Area Coordinators with a single new role: **NDIS Navigators**. For participants with psychosocial disability, a specialist **psychosocial navigator** sub-role would be created — retaining the mental-health expertise that the recovery coach role was designed to embed.

As of mid-2026, support coordination and recovery coaching continue as normal. The Review recommended a five-year implementation period from 2023, and the transition design phase is ongoing. The NDIS Commission has confirmed that the skills and experience of current recovery coaches will be directly applicable to the navigator role.

For the recovery coaching workforce, this creates a period of structural uncertainty: should individuals and organisations invest in building recovery coaching infrastructure when the role may be absorbed into a different model within three to five years?

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The NDIS/non-NDIS split creates a two-tier psychosocial support world.** People who qualify for the NDIS get recovery coaching at $110/hr from a registered provider. People who don't qualify for the NDIS (but have severe mental illness) get block-grant-funded peer support, often from an NGO with precarious funding, lower wages, and no sustainable Medicare pathway (Report 051). The CPSP — the Commonwealth-funded bridge — expires June 2027. The two tiers are structurally unequal.

**2. Registration mandate may narrow the lived experience workforce.** The July 2025 requirement for NDIS Commission registration of all providers was an appropriate quality measure — but it raises barriers to entry for independent peer practitioners. If the net effect is to concentrate recovery coaching in larger disability service organisations (rather than community-managed mental health NGOs or independent peer workers), the recovery-oriented intent of the role may be compromised by institutional assimilation pressures analogous to those described in Report 051.

**3. Plan underutilisation is a system failure.** If participants are funded for recovery coaching but cannot access it — or do not engage — the scheme is paying for support that is not delivered. Understanding why underutilisation occurs, and addressing it, requires investment in engagement supports before the coaching itself begins. This meta-challenge is not well resolved in current design.

**4. The navigator transition timeline is uncertain.** With the NDIS Review implementation still in design phase, recovery coaches, providers, and participants face multi-year uncertainty about whether the role they are investing in will continue to exist in its current form. This is a structural disincentive to workforce development and service building.

**5. Outcome measurement is immature.** Despite strong conceptual grounding and promising early research, rigorous outcome data for NDIS-funded recovery coaching in Australia is limited. The CHIME-D research (PMC12408737, 2025) and Community Mental Health Journal findings are promising — but without participant-level data at NDIA scale, it is difficult to demonstrate cost-effectiveness or to make the case for expanded access to the scheme.

1. **The Medicare Levy and Commonwealth health hypothecation** — Australia's 2% income-based Medicare Levy raised approximately $31 billion in 2024-25, but is consolidated revenue; how the question of hypothecation shapes debates about funding the community sector, including the NDIS/non-NDIS psychosocial divide
2. **NDIS plan management market** — the rapidly growing plan management sector (plan managers handle $3+ billion in NDIS funds), its fee structure, regulation, and whether it creates incentives that shape what supports participants access, including recovery coaching
3. **NDIS cost trajectory and sustainability** — total scheme expenditure is approaching $50 billion annually; the 2023 Review was driven in part by sustainability concerns; how psychosocial disability participants contribute to cost growth and what reform levers are being pulled
4. **Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP) cliff 2027** — the bridge funding for people with severe mental illness who don't qualify for NDIS expires June 2027; what happens to this population and whether a post-CPSP architecture exists
5. **State mental health community support funding and its relationship to NDIS** — states continued to fund community mental health services after NDIS rollout was supposed to replace much of that role; how the boundary is drawn, who pays for what, and where people fall through
