# CT Colonography and the NBCSP Dead End: Why Australia Has No Alternative When Colonoscopy Is Unavailable

**Report 085 — 2026-08-30**

**Summary:** CT colonography (CTC) — virtual colonoscopy using CT imaging — detects colorectal cancer with 96% sensitivity, costs about $767 less per procedure than optical colonoscopy, and requires no sedation or anaesthetic. Yet Australia's MBS funds it only for a narrow set of indications under a restrictive item that requires specialist referral, and CTC cannot be used as a follow-up pathway for positive NBCSP results. As the US and UK have extended CTC access for screening and follow-up, Australia's NBCSP remains locked into a single-track colonoscopy pipeline with no overflow valve — while 85% of positive-screened patients wait more than 30 days for the procedure.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 084 documented that Australia's colonoscopy system lacks both a functioning quality monitoring infrastructure (no mandated ADR data collection) and a reliable technology solution (AI-assisted detection improves RCT results but shows near-zero real-world effect). This report examines the structural alternative: CT colonography as a parallel diagnostic pathway that could absorb colonoscopy demand when capacity fails, and asks why Australia has not created such a pathway.

---

## Background: What CT Colonography Is

CT colonography (CTC), also called virtual colonoscopy or CT colon, uses a low-dose CT scan of the insufflated colon — air or CO2 is introduced rectally — to generate three-dimensional images of the colonic mucosa. Specialised software reconstructs a "fly-through" view that an experienced radiologist can use to identify polyps, masses, and other colonic abnormalities.

CTC differs from optical colonoscopy in several key ways. It requires no sedation, no anaesthetic, and no gastroenterology suite. The bowel preparation required is generally less extensive than for optical colonoscopy (some centres use a reduced-prep or stool-tagging approach). There is no perforation risk from the scope. However, CTC cannot take tissue samples, cannot perform polypectomy, and cannot provide histopathology — any polyp identified still requires a separate optical colonoscopy for removal. CTC also delivers a dose of ionising radiation (typically 4–6 mSv, comparable to a few years of background radiation). And CTC has lower sensitivity for flat sessile serrated lesions — the lesion type most associated with right-sided interval cancers.

Three TGA-registered CT colonography platforms operate in Australian radiology departments. The procedure is performed in radiology practices and hospital radiology departments, not in endoscopy suites, which means it draws on a completely separate infrastructure and workforce from the colonoscopy system.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Colonoscopy Capacity Crisis: The Platform for CTC's Case

The colonoscopy system in Australia is operating significantly below the demand created by the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP). As documented in Reports 080 and 084:

- Only **14.6% of NBCSP participants with positive iFOBT results** received a follow-up colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day timeframe in 2024 — up marginally from 13.9% in 2023 (AIHW NBCSP Monitoring Report 2025).
- Median wait times for colonoscopy following a positive NBCSP result range from **132 to 202 days** depending on geographic location (2024 data; worsening from 129–179 days in 2023).
- Report 080 documented an estimated shortfall of approximately **50,000 colonoscopy procedures per year** in the NBCSP pipeline, driven by capacity constraints across both public and private settings.

This creates a clinical predicament: a patient receives a positive bowel cancer screening result, knows they need a colonoscopy, and must wait four to six months to get one. During that wait, a polyp that might have been removed at three months could progress; a small cancer present at screening could be staged higher by the time the colonoscopy is performed. Research documents this "stage migration" risk explicitly — delayed diagnosis transforms potentially curative surgery into palliative treatment. No alternative diagnostic pathway is available. There is no funded off-ramp.

### 2. CTC Clinical Performance: Excellent for Cancer, Good for Large Polyps

The evidence base for CTC's diagnostic accuracy is mature and consistently strong for large lesions:

- **Colorectal cancer detection sensitivity: 96.1%** (95% CI: 93.8–97.7%) in a meta-analysis of 11,151 patients — essentially equivalent to optical colonoscopy.
- **Sensitivity for polyps ≥10mm: 90.8%** — approaching optical colonoscopy performance at the size threshold where polypectomy is typically indicated.
- **Sensitivity for polyps ≥6mm: 73–98%** (wide range reflecting radiologist experience and technique).
- **Specificity: 79.6–93.1%** for polyps ≥6mm.

These figures reflect a technology that is clinically appropriate for the primary task of the NBCSP diagnostic assessment: detecting cancer and large pre-cancerous polyps in patients with positive iFOBT results. The limitation — lower sensitivity for flat sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) — is clinically significant, as SSLs account for a proportion of right-sided interval cancers. But this limitation must be weighed against the alternative in the capacity-constrained NBCSP context: a six-month wait for optical colonoscopy during which a cancer may progress.

CTC also does not require anaesthetic, which makes it accessible to patients for whom optical colonoscopy carries elevated anaesthetic risk — the elderly, frail, anticoagulated, or those with significant comorbidities who currently face either a riskier procedure or de facto exclusion from NBCSP follow-up.

### 3. The Existing MBS Item 56553: Narrow, Restrictive, and Not Fit for NBCSP Use

CT colonography does have MBS funding in Australia — but under conditions so narrow that it cannot serve as a meaningful alternative to colonoscopy for the NBCSP.

**MBS item 56553** (existing) funds CTC for the exclusion or diagnosis of colorectal neoplasia in a symptomatic or high-risk patient, subject to:

- The service must be **referred by a specialist or consultant physician who performs colonoscopies** — i.e., a gastroenterologist or colorectal surgeon
- The service **has not been performed on the patient in the preceding 36 months**
- CTC following incomplete colonoscopy is funded, but with a **three-month time limit** — the incomplete colonoscopy must have occurred within the preceding three months
- The item **does not apply in combination with certain other service codes**

The Medicare rebate for a CTC procedure under item 56553 is approximately **$532.55**, compared to an estimated total system cost of approximately **$1,300** for an optical colonoscopy (including proceduralist fee, anaesthetic, and day hospital/day surgery facility charges). This represents a potential cost saving of approximately **$767 per procedure**.

The structural problems with item 56553 for NBCSP use are multiple:

**First**, the referral restriction requires that a patient first access a specialist who performs colonoscopies — a gastroenterologist — before being eligible for MBS-funded CTC. But the reason a patient might need CTC is precisely that they cannot access a gastroenterologist promptly. Requiring specialist referral for the overflow valve inserts a second bottleneck identical to the first.

**Second**, the item does not cover positive NBCSP iFOBT results as an explicit indication. The NBCSP diagnostic pathway mandates colonoscopy following a positive result. There is no funded CTC alternative within the NBCSP architecture, regardless of colonoscopy wait times.

**Third**, the three-month time limit on incomplete colonoscopy follow-up is arbitrary and creates perverse incentives: if a patient's incomplete colonoscopy occurred more than three months ago and no colonoscopy completion has been achieved (due to access constraints), there is no funded CTC pathway.

**Fourth**, the 36-month restriction means a patient who received a CTC in the prior three years cannot receive another MBS-funded CTC even if their clinical situation has changed significantly (for example, a new NBCSP positive result).

### 4. MSAC Application 1789: An Expansion Application, Not a Structural Fix

In late 2024, a radiologist with a special interest in abdominal radiology lodged **MSAC Application 1789**, seeking an amendment to the existing MBS item 56553. The application proposes:

- Expanding the indications to include **additional contraindications to colonoscopy** beyond the current criteria
- **Removing the three-month time limit** restricting CTC use after incomplete colonoscopy

The PASC (PICO Advisory Sub-Committee) ratified the final PICO (Population-Intervention-Comparator-Outcomes) framework at its December 2024 meeting. The public and PASC consultation closed on 8 November 2024. As of mid-2025, MSAC consideration timing remained to be announced, with subscribers notified via the MSAC bulletin.

Application 1789 is an incremental improvement to an existing item — not the structural reform that would create a genuinely accessible CTC alternative within the NBCSP pathway. Even if granted, the specialist referral requirement would remain in place, the NBCSP positive iFOBT indication would not be added, and GP-initiated CTC referral would remain unavailable.

A previous MSAC assessment (Application 1269) resulted in the original CTC MBS item, but with narrow indications that the current application 1789 seeks to modestly expand.

### 5. International Comparison: Australia Is Now Significantly Behind

**United States.** The US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) approved national coverage for **CT colonography as a colorectal cancer screening examination** from January 1, 2025 — a major policy reversal after CMS had previously deemed evidence insufficient. Coverage applies to Medicare beneficiaries aged 45 and over; no coinsurance or deductible applies for the screening use. The procedure is billed under CPT 74263 with a technical component reimbursement of $241.72 in 2025. This makes the US the first major healthcare system to fund CTC as a primary (not just diagnostic) colorectal cancer screening tool at population scale, in a national insurance programme covering over 65 million people.

**United Kingdom.** The NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme has used CTC as an alternative follow-up pathway for FOBT-positive participants who are unsuitable for optical colonoscopy (due to comorbidity, incomplete colonoscopy, or patient preference) for over a decade. A published modelling study estimated that using CTC to follow up a proportion of FOBT-positive patients saved £776,283 per 100,000 individuals invited for screening over 10 years, primarily by avoiding approximately 1,700 optical colonoscopies. CTC is embedded in the NHS pathway, not treated as an exceptional circumstance requiring specialist approval.

Australia's NBCSP has no equivalent alternative pathway. The single-track colonoscopy requirement applies regardless of capacity, geography, patient risk profile, or wait time.

### 6. The Cost Economics: CTC Is Substantially Cheaper Per Procedure

The cost differential between CTC and optical colonoscopy in the Australian context is significant:

- **CTC Medicare rebate (existing): ~$532.55**
- **Optical colonoscopy total estimated cost: ~$1,300** (proceduralist MBS fee + anaesthetic fee + day hospital/facility fee)
- **Per-procedure saving from CTC: ~$767**

At a scale relevant to the NBCSP capacity shortfall (~50,000 procedures per year), converting even a third of the shortfall to CTC pathways would represent a system-wide saving in the order of **$12.8 million per year** — before accounting for the downstream cost of delayed diagnoses. Stage migration from delayed colonoscopy (early-stage to later-stage colorectal cancer) significantly increases treatment costs: the average cost of treating Stage IV colorectal cancer is many multiples of Stage I/II treatment costs, and Stage IV carries substantially higher ongoing systemic therapy and palliative care costs.

It is important not to overstate the savings. CTC-positive results — particularly polyps ≥6mm — still require optical colonoscopy for confirmation and polypectomy, generating a downstream endoscopy cost. A CTC used as an overflow alternative would generate a second colonoscopy for those with significant findings. Net cost savings depend on the false-positive rate and the proportion of CTC-screened patients who require follow-up colonoscopy.

### 7. Extracolonic Findings: The Double-Edged Sword

CTC has a clinical feature with no optical colonoscopy equivalent: it images not just the colon but the entire abdominopelvic field, generating **extracolonic findings** — incidental findings in the liver, kidneys, adrenal glands, ovaries, aorta, and other structures. Studies report extracolonic findings in 40–70% of CTC examinations, with clinically significant extracolonic findings in approximately 10–15% of cases.

These findings can save lives — incidental renal cell carcinoma, early ovarian cancer, or aortic aneurysm found on a bowel cancer CTC represents genuine added value. But they also generate cascade workup: additional imaging, specialist referrals, further procedures, and patient anxiety. The net clinical and economic value of extracolonic CTC findings has been debated for two decades without resolution.

In the Australian context, extracolonic findings would need to be managed within a healthcare system that already has specialist access constraints. A patient with a CTC extracolonic finding requiring urology or gynaecology review would join another specialist queue. This is a genuine clinical and health system burden that any NBCSP CTC integration proposal must address — through protocols for managing extracolonic findings, radiologist reporting standards, and clear clinical handover arrangements.

### 8. The GP Referral Question: Removing the Double Bottleneck

The most consequential structural reform for any CTC expansion would be extending GP referral rights. Currently, MBS item 56553 requires a referral from a specialist who performs colonoscopies. Under a reformed pathway designed to address NBCSP capacity failures, a GP would need to be able to:

1. Receive a positive NBCSP iFOBT notification from the NBCSP register
2. Order a colonoscopy, receive a wait time exceeding a defined threshold (e.g., 30 or 60 days)
3. Refer directly for MBS-funded CTC as an interim diagnostic step

This model — sometimes called a **stepped-care diagnostic pathway** — would require a new or amended MBS item that:
- Names positive NBCSP iFOBT as an explicit indication
- Allows GP referral
- Defines a colonoscopy wait time threshold that triggers CTC eligibility
- Specifies management protocols for CTC findings (polyps ≥6mm → optical colonoscopy; negative → NBCSP recall at next scheduled interval)

No such item exists, and no MSAC application for this specific pathway has been filed as of this report.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Pathway Integrity vs. Pragmatic Access

The NBCSP is built around the evidence that optical colonoscopy is the gold standard for detecting and removing pre-cancerous polyps. Inserting CTC into the pathway risks creating a two-tiered system: patients who wait for colonoscopy get one-step detection-and-treatment; patients diverted to CTC get detection-only, then wait again for colonoscopy if the CTC is positive. This sequential two-step pathway may leave patients with significant polyps in a more uncertain position for longer.

Against this, the current system produces a de facto two-step outcome for the 85% of patients who wait beyond 30 days: a positive NBCSP result (step 1) followed by a six-month wait (step 2). CTC inserted between step 1 and step 2 at least provides diagnostic certainty during the wait — allowing higher-risk patients (large polyps, suspicious lesions) to be triaged for urgent colonoscopy, and lower-risk patients (clean or minimal findings) to be managed with appropriate surveillance intervals.

### Tension 2: Radiologist vs. Gastroenterologist Scope

The existing MBS item 56553 and its referral restriction reflect a historical tension between radiologists (who perform CTC) and gastroenterologists (who perform optical colonoscopy) over procedural scope, volume, and income. A colonoscopy generates significantly more MBS revenue than a CTC — approximately $1,300 vs $533 per procedure. Gastroenterologists have historically been cautious about the expansion of CTC funding as a colonoscopy alternative, while radiologists have advocated for expanded access. MSAC Application 1789, filed by a radiologist, reflects this ongoing professional boundary dispute.

### Tension 3: The NBCSP Architecture Is Not Built for Alternatives

The NBCSP was designed as a binary pathway: positive iFOBT → colonoscopy. The program's clinical governance, data systems, and quality monitoring are built around this architecture. Integrating CTC as a genuine alternative would require changes to the National Cancer Screening Register (to record and track CTC-based follow-up), the NBCSP clinical guidelines, the MBS item structure, the GP referral workflow, and the extracolonic findings management protocols. This is a substantial system change, not a simple item expansion.

### Tension 4: The Radiation Question for a Repeated Surveillance Pathway

In the NBCSP context, CTC would primarily be used once — as a diagnostic follow-up to a positive iFOBT. But for patients who are ineligible for or who decline optical colonoscopy on a longer-term basis, CTC would represent an ongoing surveillance tool with repeated radiation exposure. The cumulative radiation dose from five-yearly or three-yearly CTC over a surveillance lifetime is non-trivial, particularly given that colorectal cancer screening begins at age 45 and surveillance may continue for decades. This consideration reinforces CTC's role as a complementary pathway rather than a replacement for optical colonoscopy.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Stool DNA testing (multi-target stool DNA) as an alternative NBCSP primary screening modality.** CTC is a diagnostic alternative; stool DNA tests like Cologuard (US) are primary screening alternatives to iFOBT with higher sensitivity for right-sided and sessile serrated lesions. Australia has no funded stool DNA pathway. How does it compare to iFOBT in the Australian context?

2. **Colorectal cancer screening equity for First Nations Australians.** The Northern Territory has the highest interval cancer rate in Australia at 8.4 per 10,000. First Nations Australians face lower NBCSP participation, lower colonoscopy follow-up rates, and severe NCSR data gaps. What targeted investments are being made?

3. **CADx: computer-aided characterisation for optical biopsy and the "resect-and-discard" strategy.** While CADe (Report 084) detects polyps, CADx systems characterise them in real time — potentially eliminating the need to send small polyps to histopathology. This has major implications for downstream costs of the NBCSP and for the surveillance burden created by CTC's detection of polyps that require colonoscopy follow-up.

4. **Private health insurance and colonoscopy: who pays what, and where the gaps fall.** Colonoscopy in the private setting carries significant out-of-pocket exposure: anaesthetic gaps, specialist gaps, and facility fees. The interaction between PHI coverage and NBCSP follow-up creates inequitable access based on insurance status.

5. **The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025: what it requires of health services and how compliance is measured.** The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care published a new Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard in 2025. What indicators does it mandate, and does it address the CTC-as-alternative-pathway gap?
