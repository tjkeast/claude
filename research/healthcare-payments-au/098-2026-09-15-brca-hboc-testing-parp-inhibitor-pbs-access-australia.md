# BRCA1/2 and Hereditary Breast-Ovarian Cancer in Australia: Testing Architecture, PARP Inhibitor Access, and the Under-Diagnosed Population

**Report 098 — 2026-09-15**

**Summary:** BRCA1/2-related hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) syndrome has received more sustained public investment in Australia than any comparable hereditary cancer condition — federal Medicare funding since 2017, a mainstream testing model that bypasses genetics referral bottlenecks, and a wave of PBS-listed PARP inhibitors that now spans ovarian, breast, and prostate cancer. Yet the January 2026 DNA Screen pilot found that 74.5% of Australians with pathogenic hereditary cancer variants — many of them BRCA carriers — would not qualify for government-funded testing under current criteria. The infrastructure investment is real, but it is targeted at people who already have cancer or a strong family history; the undiagnosed population that has never had a reason to present remains almost entirely beyond reach.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 097 documented Lynch syndrome's cascade of under-diagnosis — from unfunded secondary tumour tests to 49% referral gaps to overwhelmed genetics services — and flagged HBOC as the parallel hereditary cancer system that has seen more funding investment. This report examines what that investment actually looks like, how far it extends, and where the structural gaps remain.

---

## Background: BRCA1/2 and the Hereditary Breast-Ovarian Cancer Syndrome

HBOC is caused by pathogenic germline variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 — tumour suppressor genes that encode proteins essential for homologous recombination DNA repair. When BRCA function is lost, cells accumulate genomic damage and become susceptible to malignant transformation, particularly in breast, ovarian, fallopian tube, peritoneal, and prostate epithelium.

### Population Prevalence in Australia

Australian-specific prevalence estimates:
- **BRCA1**: approximately **1 in 500** Australians
- **BRCA2**: approximately **1 in 225** Australians
- **Combined**: approximately **1 in 150**, implying roughly **170,000 Australians** carry a pathogenic variant in one of these two genes

The population burden is comparable in magnitude to Lynch syndrome (~90,000 estimated carriers, Report 097), though the specific genes and cancer types differ. As with Lynch syndrome, the fraction of carriers who are diagnosed is a small minority of the total.

### Cancer Risks for Carriers

Lifetime cancer risks for BRCA1/2 carriers are substantially elevated:

| Cancer type | BRCA1 lifetime risk | BRCA2 lifetime risk |
|---|---|---|
| Breast cancer (female) | 40–80% | 40–70% |
| Ovarian / fallopian tube / peritoneal | 36–60% | 11–27% |
| Contralateral breast cancer | 35–60% at 10 years | 25–50% at 10 years |
| Male breast cancer | < 5% | 5–10% |
| Prostate cancer | Modest elevation | 5–8x elevated risk for aggressive disease |
| Pancreatic cancer | 2–3x | 3–6x |

Approximately **5–10% of all female breast cancers** in Australia and **15–20% of all invasive epithelial ovarian cancers** are explained by an inherited variant — predominantly BRCA1/2, with smaller contributions from other genes (PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, BRIP1, RAD51C, RAD51D).

## Key Findings

### 1. The MBS Testing Architecture: A Series of Expansions Since 2017

Before November 2017, germline BRCA testing in Australia was available only through state and territory-funded familial cancer services — a bottleneck that limited access to patients who could be referred to (and waited for) a genetic service appointment. Federal Medicare funding, introduced in November 2017 under Group P7 (Genetics) items, fundamentally changed the architecture by enabling any specialist or consultant physician to order funded germline BRCA testing.

**Current core MBS items for BRCA-related hereditary cancer testing:**

- **Item 73295**: Full BRCA1/2 germline sequencing for a patient with a personal diagnosis of breast or ovarian cancer who meets high-risk criteria (>10% prior probability of a pathogenic variant). Criteria were simplified in November 2023 — a quantitative algorithm is no longer required; a clinical and family history assessment of ≥10% likelihood is sufficient.

- **Item 73296**: Hereditary breast/ovarian cancer gene panel testing for a patient with a personal diagnosis who meets specific criteria. Amended in March 2025 to allow the treating clinician to select the most appropriate gene panel beyond BRCA1/2 alone (including PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, and others).

- **Item 73297**: Predictive (cascade) testing for a biological relative of a confirmed variant carrier. This is the critical population multiplier — once a family variant is identified, at-risk relatives are eligible for rebated confirmatory testing.

**Additional therapeutic companion test items:**

- **Items 73303/73304**: BRCA1/2 testing for patients with locally advanced or metastatic HER2-negative breast cancer to determine eligibility for PBS-listed olaparib (MSAC Application 1507.1; amended October 2024 and again with early breast cancer extension in 2024).

- **MBS item (mCRPC, effective April 2022)**: Somatic and/or germline BRCA1/2 testing for patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) to determine eligibility for PBS-listed olaparib.

- **New HRD testing item (2024)**: A new MBS item for **homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) testing** of patients with ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancer was introduced alongside the expanded PBS listings for olaparib and niraparib in ovarian cancer. HRD testing identifies tumours with the BRCA-like phenotype even when no germline BRCA mutation is present — approximately half of all high-grade serous ovarian cancers are HRD-positive.

**Mainstream testing model:** A key structural advance of the post-2017 architecture is that BRCA testing no longer requires a genetics service referral. Oncologists, surgeons, breast physicians, and gynaecological oncologists can order MBS-rebated germline BRCA testing directly. This mainstream model, pioneered at the Parkville Familial Cancer Centre from 2017, has substantially increased testing rates for diagnosed cancer patients — particularly in breast and gynaecological oncology settings.

**Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) shortcut:** TNBC carries a ≥10% prior probability of a BRCA1 pathogenic variant and qualifies directly for MBS-funded testing without additional family history criteria — a pragmatic, clinically sound simplification.

### 2. The PBS PARP Inhibitor Cascade: Australia's Expanding Therapeutic Pipeline

PARP (poly-ADP ribose polymerase) inhibitors exploit the "synthetic lethality" principle — cancer cells deficient in homologous recombination (BRCA-mutated or HRD-positive) cannot repair double-strand DNA breaks using either pathway, and die. Between 2022 and 2025, Australia listed PARP inhibitors for four distinct indications, each with a different biomarker requirement and patient number:

**Olaparib (LYNPARZA, AstraZeneca):**

1. **Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) + germline/somatic BRCA1/2:** PBS-listed from **1 April 2022**. MBS-funded companion testing simultaneously introduced.

2. **Advanced/recurrent ovarian cancer, HRD-positive (expanded from BRCA-only):** PBS-listed from **1 January 2024** (expanded; earlier BRCA-specific listing predates this). Companion HRD MBS item co-listed.

3. **HER2-negative high-risk early breast cancer (adjuvant), germline BRCA1/2:** PBS-listed from **1 July 2024** under the OlympiA trial indication. Reduces breast cancer recurrence by 42% and breast cancer deaths by 32% vs placebo in germline BRCA carriers with high-risk early disease. Companion test item expanded accordingly.

4. **HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer, germline BRCA1/2:** PBS-listed from **1 January 2025**. Patient cost: **$31.60/script** (concession $7.70). Without PBS subsidy, course cost is approximately **$72,000**. Expected to benefit approximately **300 patients annually**. MSAC Application 1507.1 confirmed that germline BRCA testing is separately funded for all metastatic breast cancer patients requiring it.

**Niraparib (ZEJULA, GSK):**

1. **BRCA-mutated advanced ovarian cancer:** PBS-listed earlier (BRCA-specific).

2. **HRD-positive advanced ovarian cancer, first-line maintenance:** PBS-listed from **April 2024** — the second PARP inhibitor for this indication, expanding access beyond BRCA-mutated to the broader HRD-positive population.

**Summary of PARP inhibitor landscape:** Rucaparib is not TGA-approved for use in Australia and has no PBS listing. The olaparib/niraparib duopoly covers the main clinical indications. The expansion from BRCA-specific to HRD-positive eligibility in ovarian cancer is significant — it doubles the eligible population from approximately 15–20% (BRCA-mutated) to approximately 50% (HRD-positive) of high-grade serous ovarian cancer patients.

**Total patient access:** Across ovarian, breast (early and metastatic), and prostate cancer, PARP inhibitors are now accessible to several thousand Australians annually who would otherwise pay $40,000–$100,000+ per year for these medicines — or go without.

### 3. The TRACEBACK Program: Reaching the Diagnosed But Ungenotyped

A critical population of potential BRCA carriers consists of women previously diagnosed with ovarian cancer who were never offered (or never received) genetic testing before current standards were in place. Many are now deceased. Their living relatives may carry the same pathogenic variant and remain unaware.

The **TRACEBACK program**, a collaboration between Ovarian Cancer Australia and Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre, addresses this gap by:
- Testing **archival tissue samples** from deceased ovarian cancer patients to identify pathogenic variants
- Notifying living family members so they can access cascade (predictive) testing
- Providing genetic counselling support through the testing process

Approximately **20% of ovarian cancer patients** carry an inherited pathogenic variant in a cancer risk gene (predominantly BRCA1/2, with contributions from BRIP1, RAD51C, RAD51D, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6). Each confirmed carrier in an archival sample creates eligibility for cascade testing of potentially 3–10 at-risk relatives. The program has government backing and represents a proactive — rather than reactive — use of the existing cascade testing MBS infrastructure.

A 2025 study (GRACE study, *Journal of Genetic Counseling*) evaluated cascade testing uptake from traceback programs and found uptake rates were notably higher when direct professional contact was made with relatives — reinforcing the importance of active outreach rather than leaving communication to affected families.

### 4. The DNA Screen Pilot: 74.5% of Carriers Ineligible for Funded Testing

The most significant recent evidence on the gap between HBOC's population burden and Australia's testing architecture comes from the **DNA Screen pilot** (Lacaze et al., Monash University, published *Nature Health*, January 2026), which was also cited in Report 097 for its Lynch syndrome findings.

Among the 10,263 participants who completed screening in the pilot (aged 18–40, general population):
- **202 participants (2.0%)** had a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome MMR genes, or familial hypercholesterolaemia genes
- Of those referred for clinical follow-up (189 participants), **97.9% accepted** and **87.3% attended appointments**
- **74.5% of those referred were ineligible for government-funded testing criteria** under current MBS rules — they would not have been offered a test through standard clinical pathways

For BRCA1/2 specifically, those not eligible for current MBS-funded testing under items 73295–73297 are, by definition, carriers who:
- Have not been personally diagnosed with breast or ovarian cancer
- Do not have a strong enough family history to trigger the ≥10% risk threshold
- Have no known family variant to access cascade testing

This is structurally the same problem as Lynch syndrome — but on a larger scale. Australia's MBS testing architecture is designed as a reactive system, triggered by cancer diagnosis or family history. The population of BRCA carriers who have neither (because their cancer hasn't happened yet, or their family's cancer was not genotyped) is invisible to the system until they themselves develop cancer — at which point the window for primary prevention has closed.

### 5. Genetic Counselling Bottleneck: The Same Constraint as Lynch Syndrome

Despite the mainstream testing model reducing reliance on genetics referral for the *initial test*, downstream consequences of a positive BRCA test — managing cascade testing, providing pre-operative risk counselling, coordinating surveillance and risk management — still require genetic counsellor capacity.

Australia and New Zealand had approximately **432 clinical genetic counsellors** in 2022 (workforce survey cited in Report 097). Two-thirds of respondents in that survey perceived a critical shortage, and wait times at public familial cancer services exceed 12 months at many sites.

For BRCA carriers, the downstream clinical load is substantial:
- Cascade testing notification to first-degree relatives (average 2–3 immediate at-risk relatives per index case)
- Pre-test counselling for each relative undergoing predictive testing
- Post-test counselling for variant-positive relatives
- Ongoing surveillance coordination (annual MRI and mammogram for BRCA1 carriers under 60; six-monthly alternating MRI/mammography for higher-risk patients)
- Risk-reduction surgery decision-making

The mainstream testing expansion increased throughput at the front end — but the back end, particularly post-test counselling and surveillance coordination, remains constrained by the genetic counselling workforce.

### 6. Risk-Reducing Surgery: The Prevention Opportunity With a 2–4 Year Wait

For women with confirmed BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants, two risk-reducing surgeries represent the most effective prevention strategies:
- **Bilateral risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO)**: Reduces ovarian cancer risk by ~96% in BRCA1 carriers; also reduces breast cancer risk by ~50% when performed before natural menopause
- **Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy (RRM)**: Reduces breast cancer risk by ~95% in BRCA1/2 carriers

Both procedures are MBS-rebated and publicly available. They represent some of the most cost-effective cancer prevention interventions in medicine — one Australian analysis estimated that providing prophylactic mastectomies to all eligible women with inherited high breast cancer risk could save the healthcare system **tens of millions of dollars annually** through averted treatment costs.

The access problem is surgical capacity and public system wait times. Women seeking RRSO or RRM through the public system have reported waits of **2–4 years** — a period during which cancer risk is unmitigated. Some turn to the private system to access surgery sooner, but the out-of-pocket costs for private surgical fees (above MBS rebates and PHI benefits) add a further financial barrier. The structural inequity — rapid access for the privately insured, 2–4 year waits for the public patient — is particularly acute given that BRCA carrier detection is now happening earlier and in younger women who are more likely to seek risk-reducing surgery.

### 7. BRCA in Prostate Cancer: A New and Underdeveloped Frontier

BRCA2, in particular, is the most clinically significant hereditary risk gene for aggressive prostate cancer. Men carrying a BRCA2 pathogenic variant face an approximately **5–8 times elevated risk** of developing aggressive prostate cancer compared with the general population, and at younger ages.

In prostate cancer, BRCA1/2 status is now therapeutically important: olaparib is PBS-listed for mCRPC patients with confirmed BRCA1/2 variants (April 2022), and MBS-funded companion testing was introduced simultaneously.

However, a 2025 guidance paper (Azad et al., *Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology*, 2025) described the clinical landscape as still developing:
- BRCA testing guidelines for prostate cancer were not formalised in Australia until 2025
- Many urologists and oncologists were uncertain about which prostate cancer patients to test, when to test, and how to manage positive germline results in men
- Cascade testing of male family members — particularly brothers of BRCA2 carriers — is rarely systematised

A consensus Delphi panel published in 2025 (*Familial Cancer*) found ongoing uncertainty among Australian clinicians about BRCA testing thresholds in prostate cancer. The prostate cancer BRCA pathway is approximately 5–8 years behind the breast/ovarian cancer pathway in terms of clinical infrastructure, referral protocols, and patient awareness.

## Comparison With Lynch Syndrome: What the Investment Advantage Looks Like

Report 097 documented Lynch syndrome's persistent funding and infrastructure failures. HBOC has received meaningfully greater investment, and the contrast is instructive:

| Dimension | HBOC (BRCA1/2) | Lynch syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Federal Medicare funding for germline testing | Since November 2017 | Later (and with ongoing gaps) |
| Mainstream testing (non-genetics ordering) | Yes — oncologists, surgeons can order | Limited — largely genetics service–dependent |
| Dedicated MBS items for companion diagnostics | Yes — items for therapeutic gating (PBS olaparib/niraparib) | No dedicated MLH1 methylation or MSI testing items |
| PBAC-approved targeted therapies | Multiple (olaparib, niraparib) across 4 indications | None specific (aspirin for Lynch is not PBS-listed as chemoprevention) |
| Traceback/cascade infrastructure | TRACEBACK program, government-backed | Cascade testing available but no systematic traceback program |
| Population screening investment | DNA Screen pilot (research); no clinical program | No program |

Yet even with this comparative advantage, the DNA Screen finding — 74.5% of carriers ineligible for current funded testing — demonstrates that HBOC's testing architecture, like Lynch syndrome's, was designed for the reactive clinical encounter, not for proactive population-level prevention.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The population screening decision.** The DNA Screen pilot has provided cost-effectiveness evidence and demonstrated feasibility for a national preventive genomic screening program covering BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome, and familial hypercholesterolaemia. The Monash research team has called for urgent government action. Modelling suggests combined population screening at AU$200/sample is cost-effective. But the political decision to fund a national program — requiring new MBS items, genetic counselling infrastructure investment, and clinical genetics capacity expansion — has not been made.

**2. HRD expansion without cascade follow-through.** Expanding PBS PARP inhibitor eligibility from BRCA-only to HRD-positive is clinically correct — it helps ~50% of high-grade serous ovarian cancer patients. But HRD-positive status without a germline BRCA variant does not trigger a cascade testing obligation. The subset of HRD-positive patients who have germline BRCA variants (and therefore at-risk relatives) requires careful clinical delineation — a gap that the HRD testing MBS item does not fully address.

**3. Cascade testing uptake remains low.** Despite MBS-funded cascade testing being available and rebated, studies globally find that only 30–50% of eligible first-degree relatives complete predictive testing within three years of an index diagnosis. In Australia, no national registry tracks cascade testing uptake for BRCA. The TRACEBACK program addresses one part of this problem (historical patients), but the broader cascade gap — affected individuals not contacting or not reaching family members — is not systematically measured or addressed.

**4. Risk-reducing surgery inequity.** The 2–4 year public hospital wait for risk-reducing mastectomy or salpingo-oophorectomy is an equity failure: prevention is available in theory but inaccessible in practice for public patients. The irony is acute — Australia has invested heavily in BRCA testing to identify carriers, but then leaves those carriers waiting years for the surgery that would prevent the cancer the testing was designed to detect. A carrier who develops ovarian cancer during a 3-year wait for RRSO has experienced a system failure.

**5. BRCA in men and prostate cancer.** Male BRCA carriers — principally those with BRCA2 — face significant prostate cancer risk but are systematically less likely to be tested, counselled, or enrolled in risk management programs than female BRCA carriers. Germline testing pathways were designed around breast and ovarian cancer. The prostate cancer cascade from familial cancer clinics remains nascent.

**6. The funding-diagnosis mismatch.** PARP inhibitors require confirmed BRCA or HRD status for PBS access. As PARP inhibitors expand to new cancer types (pancreatic cancer is emerging), the need for BRCA testing will broaden. But testing remains contingent on cancer diagnosis and family history. The structural mismatch — PBS-listed medicines require a test that the MBS funds only reactively — will become increasingly visible as more indications are approved.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) — Australia's third major under-diagnosed hereditary condition**: FH affects ~1 in 250 Australians (comparable prevalence to Lynch syndrome and HBOC), has a dedicated cascade testing register (FH Australia), and offers a comparative model for how Australia manages hereditary disease at scale — including what's worked and what hasn't.

2. **The National Genomics Strategy and population screening decision**: The Australian Government's National Genomics Strategy and the DNA Screen pilot's recommendations both point toward a national preventive genomic screening program. This report examines the policy architecture, funding ask, and what's actually been decided.

3. **Risk-reducing surgery access: the surgical capacity gap and PHI interface**: The 2–4 year public system wait for RRM and RRSO; how PHI coverage interacts with prophylactic surgery; whether a dedicated MBS pathway for faster access is feasible.

4. **PALB2, ATM, CHEK2 and other moderate-risk HBOC genes**: The current MBS items fund testing for these genes in some contexts, but surveillance and risk-management guidelines are less clear — a growing clinical population with no funded management framework.

5. **BRCA and pancreatic cancer: the emerging PBS frontier**: PARP inhibitors for germline BRCA-positive pancreatic cancer are progressing through PBS evaluation internationally; the Australian picture on testing, treatment eligibility, and access gaps.
