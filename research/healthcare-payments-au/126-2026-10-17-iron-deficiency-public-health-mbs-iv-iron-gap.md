# Iron Deficiency as a National Public Health Problem: The $6.6 Billion Productivity Loss, the 17-Fold IV Iron Surge, and the MBS Administration Gap

**Report 126 · 17 October 2026**

**One-line summary:** Iron deficiency affects up to one in five Australian women of reproductive age, costs the economy an estimated $6.6 billion a year in lost productivity, and has driven a 17-fold increase in IV iron infusions over a decade — yet there is still no Medicare item to fund GP-administered treatment, leaving women with a $250 out-of-pocket gap or a hospital admission they didn't need.

---

## Why this follows from yesterday

Report 125 on Von Willebrand Disease examined how Australian women with bleeding disorders face diagnostic delays of over 14 years and a payment system that has no joint haematology–gynaecology billing pathway. Iron deficiency — the commonest downstream consequence of chronic blood loss — is equally systematic in its neglect: poorly measured, routinely normalised, and trapped in a reimbursement architecture that shifts women from underfunded GP care to expensive hospital admissions.

---

## Background

Iron is essential for haemoglobin synthesis, immune function, cognitive development, and energy metabolism. Iron deficiency exists on a spectrum from depleted stores (low ferritin, normal haemoglobin) through iron-deficient erythropoiesis to frank iron deficiency anaemia (IDA), where haemoglobin falls below threshold. The distinction matters because symptoms — fatigue, brain fog, impaired concentration, reduced exercise tolerance — often appear well before anaemia is diagnosable, meaning the conventional "treat when haemoglobin is low" approach systematically undertreats a large proportion of affected people.

In Australia, iron deficiency is predominantly a women's health problem. The main drivers are menstrual blood loss (heavy menstrual bleeding is a recognised epidemic — covered in Report 124), pregnancy and lactation, and dietary inadequacy. For First Nations communities, particularly in remote settings, intestinal parasites and food insecurity create an additional burden that compounds these reproductive-cycle losses.

The payment system spans three layers: pathology testing (MBS-subsidised ferritin/iron studies); oral iron supplementation (PBS-listed and over-the-counter); and IV iron infusions (PBS-subsidised medicine but no Medicare item for the administration itself). It is the third layer where the structural failure is most acute.

## Key Findings

### 1. Prevalence: large, uncertain, and likely underestimated

There is no nationally representative, methodologically consistent prevalence study for iron deficiency in Australia — a striking gap for a condition of this scale.

The best available estimates suggest:

- **Women aged 18–39:** Approximately 35–37% have serum ferritin below 30 μg/L, the threshold commonly used in Australian clinical practice. This figure comes from a 2020 online-recruited study (NSW, VIC, QLD) and a March 2026 MJA Insight review; it may overrepresent health-engaged women but still points to a very large affected population. An older AusDiab cohort figure put iron deficiency at 20.3% of women aged 25–49.
- **Overall population:** NPS MedicineWise estimates 760,000 to 2 million Australians have iron deficiency; the Medibank Better Health Index (2016) found 1.1 million affected people — a figure likely to have grown with population increase and rising rates of heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Children under five:** The National Blood Authority's Patient Blood Management guidelines note that anaemia (mainly iron deficiency) affects about 8% of Australian children under five nationally.
- **First Nations children in remote communities:** Iron deficiency prevalence is estimated at 40–60% — five to seven times the non-Indigenous rate — driven by food insecurity, parasitic infections, and limited antenatal supplementation coverage.
- **First Nations pregnant women:** Anaemia in pregnancy rates of 48.7–54.5% in Far North Queensland cohorts (Cairns Hospital 2023; JCU retrospective 2006–2010) and up to 62% in some Northern Territory communities, compared with 28.9% in non-Indigenous women in the same regional settings.

**The ferritin threshold problem:** Australia's clinical practice commonly uses ferritin <30 μg/L as the cut-off for iron deficiency in women — but the WHO standard is <15 μg/L. A 2024 MJA review raised concerns about laboratory reference ranges using sex-based lower thresholds for women (as Lifeblood does), which may normalise iron stores that are functionally deficient. Women with ferritin 15–30 μg/L are often not diagnosed or treated, yet many experience symptomatic iron deficiency. This definitional inconsistency between laboratories, guidelines, and PBS restriction criteria creates a clinical grey zone that systematically disadvantages women.

### 2. Economic burden: $6.6 billion in lost productivity

An Evaluate Consulting report commissioned by the iron therapeutics sector (year of publication not confirmed in available sources, covered by Health Dispatch) estimated that iron deficiency in women aged 15–44 costs the Australian economy approximately $6.62 billion per year in productivity losses — an average of $2,846 per affected woman in foregone earnings. This figure encompasses absenteeism, presenteeism, and early workforce exit driven by fatigue and cognitive impairment.

While the methodology of private-sector health economic reports warrants scrutiny, the order of magnitude is plausible: iron deficiency is associated with significantly reduced aerobic capacity, impaired concentration, and increased susceptibility to infection — all pathways to reduced labour productivity.

### 3. The IV iron explosion: 17-fold in a decade, with a $82 million government spend

A landmark study by Flinders University and SAHMRI researchers, published in the *Medical Journal of Australia* in August 2026, traced IV iron dispensing via PBS data from 2013 to 2024. The findings are striking:

- IV iron use among women of reproductive age (18–44) rose from **0.3 to 5.0 per 100 women** — a 17-fold increase over 11 years. By 2024, approximately **one in 20 Australian women** in this age group received an IV iron infusion in a given year.
- Total estimated medication expenditure on IV iron grew from **$1.1 million in 2013 to $82.3 million in 2024**.
- The Australian government covered **90.8% of costs** in 2024 — the IV iron medicines (Ferinject/ferric carboxymaltose and Monofer/ferric derisomaltose) are PBS-listed as restricted benefit items, so the subsidy is substantial.
- **Ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject)** dominates, accounting for 94% of dispensings in 2024.
- **GPs write more than 60% of prescriptions**, confirming that primary care is the main delivery channel.
- There is a **greater than threefold variation in use across states and territories**, with Western Australia having the highest rates. This geographic variation — unexplained by disease burden differences alone — is likely driven by access, billing structures, and GP confidence in administering infusions.
- Only **7.5% of prescriptions** had a repeat fill, raising concern about widespread incomplete treatment courses and potential underdosing.

The PBS restriction for IV iron requires that "oral iron preparations are ineffective or cannot be used" — a clinical gate that GPs apply with considerable variation. The 17-fold increase suggests either a genuine epidemic of treatment-refractory or intolerant cases, or (more likely) a combination of appropriate clinical expansion from previous undertreatment plus increased GP confidence following the single-dose Ferinject formulation's availability.

### 4. The MBS administration gap: $250 out-of-pocket for treatment of a common condition

Here lies the core payment system failure. While the PBS subsidises the IV iron *medicine*, there is **no Medicare Benefits Schedule item** that covers the *administration* of an IV iron infusion in general practice. This creates a structural billing absurdity:

- A GP who administers a Ferinject infusion can only bill a standard consultation item (approximately $41–80 in Medicare rebate, depending on time and concession status). This does not cover:
  - The administration procedure itself
  - Nurse time for preparation, monitoring, and post-infusion observation (mandatory: minimum 30 minutes post-infusion observation for anaphylaxis risk)
  - Clinical resources (IV consumables, emergency equipment, blood pressure monitoring)

- In practice, **patients pay approximately $250 out-of-pocket** per infusion at GP clinics that offer the service. An MJA Insight opinion piece from September 2026 cited this as the real-world OOP cost at a standard GP clinic.

- Only **38% of GP practices** currently offer IV iron infusions, partly because the economics make it unviable without an adequate item number.

- Patients who cannot access or afford GP-administered infusions either go untreated or present to **hospital day-stay units for the same infusion, entirely free**. The RACGP cited more than **70,000 same-day hospital admissions in 2021–22** for severe iron deficiency anaemia — a substantial proportion of which could be handled in general practice if the payment architecture supported it.

**The RACGP proposal:** In February 2024, the RACGP submitted to the MBS Continuous Review a proposal for a new $200 item number covering GP-administered iron infusions. The structure proposed was: $90 for GP time (up to 15 minutes for dose calculation, patient education, consent); $50 for nurse administration and monitoring; $60 for clinical resources. Eligible if patient meets PBS restriction criteria; limited to once per 12 months with exceptions. The RACGP's modelling estimated potential government savings of **$53–124 million per year** by shifting infusions from expensive hospital day-stay settings to efficient GP settings.

As of September 2026, no dedicated MBS item has been approved. The gap persists.

### 5. The hospital-to-GP cost differential

The cost implication of the billing gap is significant. The government's own data suggests that a same-day hospital admission for IV iron costs far more than a GP administration would, primarily through facility costs and hospital overhead. The RACGP's per-patient savings estimate of $750–$1,800 per treated patient reflects this differential.

The paradox: the government subsidises the PBS medicine (90.8% of $82.3M = ~$74.7M in 2024) but does not subsidise the administration — so some patients end up in hospital at greater public cost because GP administration is not financially viable for practices. The system is paying for the drug at the cheaper site (PBS, community) but paying for the procedure at the more expensive site (hospital, inpatient/day-stay) because the GP billing gap makes primary care delivery unaffordable for many patients.

### 6. Oral iron: low cost but high intolerance

Oral iron supplements are PBS-listed (ferrous fumarate, ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate with folic acid) and are relatively low-cost — roughly $7.70 on concession. However:

- Gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, constipation, cramping) lead to significant non-compliance and treatment failure, particularly at therapeutic doses.
- Oral iron is less effective in patients with active gut inflammation, coeliac disease, or conditions affecting gut iron absorption.
- The PBS restriction for IV iron requiring oral iron "ineffective or not tolerable" creates a compliance documentation burden on GPs. In practice, the criterion is often met after one failed oral trial, but it adds an additional step — and sometimes an additional consultation — before IV access.

### 7. Children: cognitive stakes and remote access gaps

The cognitive consequences of iron deficiency in childhood are now well-established. A retrospective cohort of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in Far North Queensland found that early childhood anaemia **more than doubled the odds of developmental vulnerability at school age** (OR 2.2). The Kids Research Institute Australia (Perth) reports that **one in three one-year-olds** and up to **two in three three-year-olds** in WA are low in iron — figures that imply a substantial, invisible drag on developmental outcomes nationally.

For remote First Nations communities where prevalence reaches 40–60% in under-fives, iron deficiency represents a modifiable risk factor for the educational and developmental inequities that perpetuate disadvantage across generations. The National Blood Authority has paediatric dosing guides, and the Lowitja Institute funded a 2024 project to measure burden in NSW Aboriginal communities — but there is no national childhood iron deficiency prevention program.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Why hasn't the MBS administration item been approved?**
The RACGP submission has been pending in the MBS Continuous Review since February 2024. The case on cost-effectiveness grounds is strong — $53–124M in projected savings. The barriers appear to be concerns about: (a) induced demand (if GP administration is easier, more borderline cases will be treated); (b) safety — anaphylaxis risk from IV iron requires clear emergency protocols not universal in general practice; (c) the appropriate scope of a $200 item vs the full cost of safe service delivery. A 2026 federal health budget without this item looks like a significant policy miss.

**2. Is the 17-fold increase in IV iron appropriate or concerning?**
The RACGP and haematologists broadly support the trend as correcting historical undertreatment. But the low repeat-fill rate (7.5%) may indicate that single large-dose Ferinject infusions are being chosen over the recommended multi-dose protocols — not inherently wrong, but worth scrutiny. There is also limited pharmacovigilance data on long-term outcomes of repeated IV iron exposure.

**3. The ferritin threshold and sex-normalisation problem**
Women's "normal" ferritin ranges, as set by many Australian laboratories, are lower than men's — partly reflecting the realities of menstrual iron loss, partly reflecting a problematic normalisation of iron depletion as natural for women. Clinical guidelines (MJA 2024) are moving toward a more functionally-grounded <30 μg/L threshold, but this creates diagnostic inconsistency between laboratories. Until the RCPA or a national authority standardises thresholds, under-diagnosis will persist.

**4. The equity dimension**
The $250 OOP cost for GP IV iron is not a barrier only for the explicitly disadvantaged: for a part-time working mother with heavy menstrual bleeding, paying $250 for treatment of a condition she may not even know she has (since many women normalise fatigue) represents a meaningful access barrier. The alternative — a free hospital day-stay — requires time off work, childcare arrangements, and transport, which creates a different equity burden. The current system is not well-calibrated to the population it most affects.

**5. Remote and First Nations gaps: the most urgent and least addressed**
For First Nations pregnant women facing 60% anaemia rates in some NT communities, oral iron non-compliance (related to GI intolerance and cultural and practical barriers to supplementation) and IV iron access via remote community health services are critical issues. The National Blood Authority's guides exist; systematic delivery infrastructure does not.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Maternity services and antenatal care payment architecture** — The antenatal care system (Medicare-funded shared care, midwifery group practices, private obstetrics) intersects directly with iron deficiency management in pregnancy; the payment structures vary enormously between public and private, and by remoteness. Iron supplementation during pregnancy is guideline-recommended but systematically under-monitored.

2. **Women's health specialist out-of-pocket costs and the MBS gender gap** — Iron deficiency is the downstream consequence of conditions (HMB, VWD, endometriosis) covered in recent reports; this thread would aggregate the full OOP burden of women navigating a payment system not designed around the most common causes of their ill-health.

3. **Gynaecologist distribution and training** — The management of the underlying causes of iron deficiency (HMB, endometriosis, fibroids) requires gynaecological access that is highly geographically unequal; waiting times and specialist OOP costs are a parallel access problem.

4. **Peripartum haemorrhage and the payment architecture of maternity emergencies** — Postpartum haemorrhage is the leading cause of maternal mortality globally; it intersects with iron deficiency (pre-existing low iron stores are a major risk factor for severity), VWD (covered in Report 125), and the hospital maternity funding model.

5. **Patient Blood Management as a national program** — The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care runs the National Blood Agreement and the National Patient Blood Management Collaborative; the question of whether PBM is being systematically applied, and how the National Blood Authority's funding model works (Commonwealth subsidies, hospital allocations, blood product costs), has not yet been covered.
