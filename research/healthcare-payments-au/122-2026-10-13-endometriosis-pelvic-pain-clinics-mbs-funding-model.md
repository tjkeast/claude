# Endometriosis and Pelvic Pain Clinics: Australia's Condition-Specific Workaround for the Multidisciplinary Care Funding Gap

**One-line summary:** Australia has built a network of 33 government-funded, GP-led multidisciplinary clinics for endometriosis and pelvic pain — not through MBS item reform, but through a PHN block-grant model that bypasses the structural funding gap the Faculty of Pain Medicine has been fighting to close — raising the question of whether condition-specific grant programs are a durable alternative to systemic MBS reform or just a political workaround.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 121 documented that Australia's MBS cannot fund the multidisciplinary pain care that evidence shows works — because no MBS items exist for the allied health team or chronic pain management plans that a pain specialist and GP need to work together. Today's report examines the one area where the government *has* funded multidisciplinary pain care at scale: endometriosis and chronic pelvic pain, via a dedicated clinic network established outside the MBS entirely.

---

## Background

Endometriosis is a chronic inflammatory condition in which tissue resembling the uterine lining grows outside the uterus — on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, bowel, bladder, or peritoneum. It causes severe, cyclical and often chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhoea (painful periods), dyspareunia, and in many cases infertility. It affects approximately one in nine Australian women aged 40–44, and one in seven aged 44–49, based on Australian Institute of Health and Welfare data.

The condition has historically been defined by delay. Affected women wait an average of six to eight years between first reporting symptoms and receiving a diagnosis — a gap driven by clinician under-recognition, symptom normalisation, and a diagnostic architecture that required laparoscopic surgery (with its own waiting list) as the gold-standard test. A 2025 Monash University study using Medicare data found that median diagnostic delay in general practice has fallen to 2.5 years since 2011, reflecting increased awareness, but that one in four women still wait five or more years.

The economic and health burden is substantial. A 2019 cost-of-illness study (Armour et al.) estimated that endometriosis costs affected women approximately A$30,000 per year, with productivity losses — absenteeism and presenteeism — accounting for over 80% of that total. Healthcare costs were roughly A$5,700–$36,000 per woman annually depending on pain severity. At a population level, the same study extrapolated a national burden of approximately A$9.7 billion per year, making endometriosis one of Australia's most economically costly underdiagnosed conditions.

The AIHW recorded more than 44,000 hospitalisations related to endometriosis in 2022–23, more than double the 2012–13 figure of 28,700. Notably, more than two-thirds of those hospitalisations occur in private hospitals — a structural indicator that public system access to specialist care and surgery is constrained.

## Key Findings

### 1. The PHN Block-Grant Model: 33 Clinics, Not an MBS Item

Australia's response to endometriosis and chronic pelvic pain has not been a new MBS item enabling any GP to bill for coordinating a multidisciplinary team — the model the Faculty of Pain Medicine has been advocating for the entire chronic pain population. Instead, the government established a dedicated network of **Endometriosis and Pelvic Pain Clinics (EPPCs)** using grants distributed through Primary Health Networks.

The program began with $16.4 million over four years in the 2022–23 Budget, establishing 22 clinics. In February 2025, the government announced the addition of 11 further clinics, bringing the national total to **33** with at least one in every PHN region, at an additional $19.6 million over three years (2025–26 to 2027–28). Each new clinic receives approximately $770,000 (excluding GST) over three years. From July 2026, all clinics have expanded their remit to also provide information and support for menopause and perimenopause management.

The clinics operate within existing general practice settings. They are GP-led and supported by nurses, women's health physiotherapists, psychologists, dietitians, and, in some sites, nurse navigators who help patients move through specialist referral pathways. Initial appointments for new pelvic pain patients typically run over one hour — far longer than a standard GP consultation. The model deliberately integrates pharmacological and non-pharmacological care from the first visit.

The program sits within the government's broader **$87.2 million National Action Plan for Endometriosis** investment since 2018, across clinical care, education, and research.

**Why this matters for the broader MBS reform debate:** The EPPCs represent a government acknowledgement that the current MBS cannot fund multidisciplinary pain care for a condition affecting hundreds of thousands of Australians — and a decision to route around that problem with condition-specific grants rather than fix the underlying MBS architecture. The Faculty of Pain Medicine's recommended approach (create a chronic pain management plan item usable by any GP for any chronic pain patient) would generalise this model to 3.4 million Australians. The EPPC approach funds 33 specific sites for one condition.

### 2. The MBS Has Been Reformed — But Incrementally

Alongside the clinic network, the government has made targeted MBS changes for endometriosis care:

**New long specialist gynaecology consultation items (from 1 July 2025):** Items 125 and 126 provide Medicare rebates for specialist gynaecologist consultations of 45 minutes or longer — initial ($178.70) and subsequent ($89.40) attendances. Telehealth equivalents are items 127 and 129. These address a longstanding gap: complex endometriosis care routinely requires consultations far longer than standard specialist attendance items were designed for, but prior to July 2025 there was no higher rebate for that time. The government costed this measure at $49.1 million.

**Advanced pelvic ultrasound item (from 1 November 2025):** MBS item 55080 provides a dedicated rebate for specialist ultrasound assessment of deep infiltrating endometriosis — the severe form affecting the bowel, bladder, and other organs. Previously, this imaging had to be claimed under the generic pelvic ultrasound item 55065, which did not adequately compensate for the expertise and time a DIE assessment requires. A co-updated RANZCOG living guideline (May 2025) recommends transvaginal pelvic ultrasound as first-line investigation for suspected endometriosis, meaning that a better-funded diagnostic imaging pathway now exists before surgery is even discussed.

**PBS listing for Ryeqo (from 1 May 2025):** Relugolix with estradiol and norethisterone acetate (brand name Ryeqo) — a daily oral GnRH receptor antagonist with add-back hormone therapy — was listed on the PBS following a November 2024 PBAC recommendation. The cost fell from approximately $2,700 per year to $92–$380 per year depending on concession status. Around 8,500 Australian women are expected to benefit annually. This addressed a significant financial barrier: Ryeqo had been available as a private prescription at a cost most patients with endometriosis-related income loss could not sustain.

### 3. The National Action Plan's Impact: Limited on Treatment Uptake

A July 2025 study from the University of Queensland (Chinaka, Gannon, Doust) examined whether Australia's National Action Plan for Endometriosis, released in 2018, actually changed treatment utilisation. Using MBS and PBS claims data from 2012 to 2023, the researchers found that **five years after the plan was implemented, there had been no significant increase in the uptake of laparoscopic resection or nafarelin** — two primary treatments. There was a small and steady rise in laparoscopic resection and a short-term spike in nafarelin use at the plan's launch, but no durable improvement in the long-term treatment access trajectory.

This finding is consistent with what is now a well-established pattern in Australian health policy: an action plan that increases awareness and generates clinical education can move diagnosis rates (and the Monash data suggest diagnosis rates have improved) without necessarily fixing the structural barriers — surgical wait times, specialist access, and financial constraints — that determine whether a diagnosed patient can actually access treatment.

### 4. Surgery Access: A Private System By Default

The endometriosis surgery picture illustrates the broader two-tier dynamic that characterises Australian specialist care. In 2021–22, more than two-thirds of endometriosis-related hospitalisations occurred in private hospitals. Endometriosis Australia reports that public surgery waits can exceed one year; government data shows 7% of patients wait more than a year. Historically, endometriosis surgery has been classified as category 3 (lowest-priority) elective surgery in public hospitals — meaning it competes for theatre time against cataract surgery, joint replacements, and other common procedures.

For the uninsured or under-insured patient, out-of-pocket costs for laparoscopic excision surgery are prohibitive: roughly $2,000–$5,000 for stage 1–2 disease and $3,000–$8,000+ for stage 3–4, with private care for complex bowel or bladder resections reportedly exceeding $15,000–$20,000 in total costs. Without private hospital cover, costs can reach $8,000–$15,000 for a private admission.

The EPPCs' role in this context is to provide earlier, more thorough assessment and management in primary care — ideally reducing the number of patients who need surgery, improving patient readiness when they do, and optimising non-surgical management before the surgical wait list comes due.

### 5. The Regional Access Problem Has Not Been Solved

The ABC's April 2025 reporting found that regional and rural women with endometriosis face **unsustainable travel burdens** to access the EPPCs, many of which are concentrated in metropolitan centres or large regional cities. AIHW data show that the age-standardised rate of endometriosis-related hospitalisation is 1.5 times higher in major cities than in remote and very remote areas — a pattern advocates interpret as evidence of under-diagnosis and under-treatment in the bush, rather than lower prevalence.

Clinics have been "inundated" since opening, and some are not yet fully operational. The $770,000 per clinic over three years works out to approximately $256,000 per year — enough to fund perhaps half an FTE GP, nursing support, and some allied health sessions, but not a full multidisciplinary team operating at capacity.

### 6. The Endometriosis Management Plan: A New GP Tool

From June 2025, a new structured **Endometriosis Management Plan (EMP)** began piloting in general practices, funded by the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing under the National Action Plan framework. The EMP is designed to give GPs a structured clinical tool — analogous in concept to a GP Management Plan — for documenting endometriosis care, coordinating specialist referrals, and tracking treatment response. A national rollout to all primary care settings was planned for mid-2026, pending pilot evaluation.

The EMP is significant in a subtle way: it exists because no MBS item funds a chronic pain management plan for endometriosis (or any other condition). If the Faculty of Pain Medicine's recommendation to create a chronic pain management plan item were ever implemented, the EMP might become redundant — or become the template for it.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Block grants are not a scalable model.** The EPPC approach works for a single, high-profile condition with strong advocacy and political salience. Australia has hundreds of conditions associated with chronic pain — fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis, chronic widespread pain — none of which has a dedicated clinic network. The pain medicine sector's argument is that the MBS reform the FPM is seeking would reach all of them with a single structural change.

**2. The condition-specific grant creates equity problems within women's health.** Endometriosis has received dedicated funding; vulvodynia, adenomyosis, chronic pelvic pain without a laparoscopic diagnosis, and painful bladder syndrome largely have not, despite their similar burden. Clinicians in EPPCs report seeing patients with multiple overlapping pelvic pain conditions — not all of which fall within the clinic's official remit.

**3. The surgery access problem sits upstream of the EPPC model.** Earlier diagnosis in primary care does not shorten the surgical wait list. If patients identified earlier in the EPPC system then need laparoscopic excision, they join the same public queue — classified category 3, competing for constrained theatre time. The MBS consultation items and the PBS Ryeqo listing address pharmacological and diagnostic pathways; they do not address surgical capacity.

**4. The UQ evidence gap raises uncomfortable questions.** If five years of action plan investment in awareness and clinical education has not moved treatment uptake statistics, the question is whether the next round of investment — in clinics, new MBS items, and management plans — will fare better. The answer may depend on whether structural barriers (surgical wait times, PHI access, regional geography) are addressed alongside clinical capacity.

**5. The EPPC model needs evaluation infrastructure.** The closure of ePPOC (Report 121) removed the sector's benchmarking platform. There is currently no equivalent outcomes monitoring framework for the EPPC network that would allow the government to assess whether the $39.9 million invested in the clinics since 2022 has reduced diagnostic delay, reduced hospitalisation rates, or improved patient quality of life.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Adenomyosis — endometriosis' sibling condition and the current access gap**: adenomyosis (endometrial tissue embedded in the uterine muscle wall) affects a similar population but has received far less policy attention, no dedicated MBS items, and no equivalent clinic network — an equity question for the next stage of women's health reform
2. **PBS spending on hormonal endometriosis treatments — a trend analysis**: the trajectory of dienogest, leuprorelin, etonogestrel, and now Ryeqo on the PBS over the past decade; how the prescribing volumes and costs interact with surgical demand
3. **Women's health and the Medicare gap — the broader specialist OOP cost burden**: the 2025 RANZCOG report found that women face higher out-of-pocket costs than men for equivalent complexity of specialist care; the long gynaecology consultation items are one partial response
4. **Surgical pelvic pain services — the gynaecologist training and distribution problem**: how many laparoscopic excision-trained gynaecologists are there, where are they, and how does the training pipeline constrain access to care?
5. **Interoperability of the EPPC model and the MBS pain reform agenda**: whether the government intends the condition-specific clinic model to expand to other chronic pain conditions or whether it will eventually be superseded by MBS chronic pain management plan items — and what would need to change for either path to materialise
