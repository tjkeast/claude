# NBCSP's Unfinished Architecture: The Colonoscopy Follow-Up Gap

**Report 077 — 2026-08-22**

**Summary:** Australia's National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP) has the most cost-effective cancer screening test in the federal portfolio, but its architecture is broken at the follow-up stage. Roughly 16% of people who receive a positive iFOBT never complete a diagnostic colonoscopy — a proportion that barely moves year on year. Of those who do, only 14.6% receive their colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day window. Median wait times range from 132 to 202 days depending on jurisdiction. The gap is not random: it tracks the fault lines of public hospital capacity, private health insurance coverage, socioeconomic disadvantage, geographic remoteness, and Indigeneity — the exact same populations at highest bowel cancer risk are the least likely to close the loop.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 076 identified the NBCSP's colonoscopy follow-up stage as its most significant equity fault line, describing it as the program's "unfinished architecture." The postal iFOBT is universal and free; what happens after a positive result is not. This report examines the size of the follow-up gap in detail, its structural causes, which populations are most affected, and what reforms have been tested or proposed.

---

## Background: The Screening Pathway and Where It Breaks

The NBCSP's patient journey is, in theory, straightforward:

1. An iFOBT kit is automatically mailed to eligible adults aged 45–74 (extended to 45+ since July 2024)
2. The participant returns the kit, which is analysed by a NBCSP-contracted laboratory
3. ~6–7% of returned kits are positive (indicating microscopic blood in the stool)
4. A positive result triggers a letter advising the participant to see their GP
5. The GP refers the participant for diagnostic colonoscopy
6. Colonoscopy is performed; abnormality diagnosed or cleared
7. If polyps are found, a surveillance schedule is set; if cancer is found, treatment begins

Steps 1–4 are automated and Commonwealth-funded. Steps 5–7 are where the system fractures.

The NBCSP does not fund colonoscopy. It is funded separately — either through MBS item 32090 (standard colonoscopy) via a public hospital, or out-of-pocket for patients accessing the private system. The NBCSP has no mechanism to track what happens after it sends a positive result letter, except through retrospective MBS and hospital data matching. It cannot compel completion, ensure timeliness, or guarantee equity of access.

The NBCSP turned 20 in August 2026. The AIHW's monitoring report, published June 2026, recorded program participation at 42% — statistically unchanged from 2025's 41.7%. Participation has not materially improved in several years. Of the 6.4 million Australians aged 50–74 invited to screen between 2023 and 2024, 3.7 million (58%) did not return their kit. Since the program began in 2006, 17,378 bowel cancers have been detected through screening, and age-standardised mortality in the 50–74 cohort has fallen from 44 to 23 per 100,000 — a significant achievement built on an incomplete foundation.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Follow-Up Gap

The NBCSP Monitoring Report 2026 (AIHW, June 2026) provides the most recent performance data:

- **Positivity rate:** ~6% of returned iFOBTs in 2024 (up from ~5.9% in prior years), reflecting an eligible population that is on average older and higher-risk as age eligibility expanded
- **Follow-up colonoscopy completion (any time):** approximately **84–86%** of people with a positive result eventually receive a diagnostic assessment (colonoscopy or other assessment). This means **~14–16% never complete follow-up**
- **Timely follow-up within 30 days:** only **14.6% in 2024** (up marginally from 13.9% in 2023) — meaning fewer than 1 in 7 positive-test participants received colonoscopy within the clinically preferred window
- **Median time from positive result to diagnostic assessment:** approximately **62 days** nationally in 2023, with jurisdictional ranges of **132–202 days** in 2024 depending on location

The 30-day target reflects the clinical risk profile of people with positive iFOBTs: approximately 1 in 26 colonoscopies for a positive NBCSP result (3.8%) finds a confirmed or suspected bowel cancer, and 1 in 4 finds an adenoma (pre-cancerous polyp). For this group, every additional month of diagnostic delay carries meaningful cancer progression risk.

The ACSQHC's September 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard sets a **four-month (120-day) maximum** from positive FOBT to diagnostic colonoscopy — a standard that itself acknowledges the system cannot reliably deliver 30-day access. Even against this more pragmatic benchmark, a significant proportion of participants nationally do not meet it.

Bowel Cancer Australia has tracked colonoscopy wait times as a specific campaign issue. Its 2025 data flagged that John Hunter Hospital's Direct Access Colonoscopy (DAC) clinic had a median wait of 34 days — an outlier in the positive direction, yet still exceeding the 30-day target the DAC model was designed to meet.

### 2. The Public–Private Fracture

The central structural cause of the follow-up gap is the **bifurcation between public hospital colonoscopy access and private access**, which maps directly onto health insurance coverage:

**Public hospital colonoscopy:**
- Free at point of service (funded through state hospital budgets, not the NBCSP or Commonwealth MBS)
- Wait times in public outpatient gastroenterology queues can be extreme: at John Hunter Hospital (Newcastle, NSW), the waitlist for gastroenterology grew from 5,151 in 2021 to 7,872 in 2025; the maximum wait for a colonoscopy was 725 days. Median time to see a gastroenterologist in that system was 1.22 years
- The public system requires a specialist outpatient appointment before colonoscopy in most jurisdictions (though DAC models bypass this — see below)
- State-by-state variation is significant: jurisdictions with higher public hospital endoscopy capacity and lower lists deliver faster access

**Private colonoscopy:**
- Typically available within days to weeks with appropriate private health insurance (PHI)
- Full cost of a private colonoscopy ranges from approximately $2,500 to $5,000 all-up (procedure, anaesthetist, facility)
- With appropriate PHI: gap payments typically $0–$260 under known-gap arrangements; around 26% of private patients paid some gap in 2023–24, with an average gap of approximately $200
- Without PHI: the participant bears approximately 25% of the MBS fee, plus all facility costs not covered by Medicare — potentially $1,000–$3,000 out-of-pocket for the uninsured accessing a private facility
- Private gastroenterologists can book a colonoscopy within days to two weeks in most metropolitan areas

The result is a two-tier follow-up system that correlates directly with PHI coverage (42% of Australians and declining do not hold comprehensive hospital cover) and with geography (rural and remote areas have minimal or no private gastroenterology supply regardless of insurance status).

### 3. Geographic Variation: The ACSQHC Atlas (September 2025)

The ACSQHC released the Atlas Focus Report: Colonoscopy in September 2025, providing the most detailed national picture of colonoscopy access variation. Key findings:

- **Overall colonoscopy rates (MBS-subsidised) in 2023–24:** 2,550 per 100,000 in major cities versus **633 per 100,000** in remote areas — a **4:1 ratio**
- **Socioeconomic variation:** most affluent areas: 3,067 per 100,000; most disadvantaged areas: 1,675 per 100,000 — a **1.8:1 ratio**
- **Declining access in high-need areas:** the national rate of MBS-subsidised repeat colonoscopy before three years fell 8% between 2013–14 and 2023–24. In remote areas, the fall was **17%**. In the most socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, **26%**
- **Overuse counterpart:** in some metropolitan areas, rates of repeat colonoscopy in low-risk patients are higher than clinically indicated, consuming endoscopy capacity that would otherwise be available for high-risk patients and post-NBCSP follow-up

The ACSQHC's conclusion was explicit: overuse of colonoscopy in low-risk patients in major cities (surveillance colonoscopies at intervals shorter than clinical guidelines recommend) is diverting resources from people who most need timely access to diagnostic colonoscopy — including NBCSP positive-result participants.

SaferCare Victoria's March 2025 "Promoting Best Practice Colonoscopy" recommendations report identified the same dynamic at state level, proposing standardised referral and triage criteria to redirect endoscopy capacity toward higher-priority categories.

### 4. Equity: The Indigenous Bowel Cancer Survival Gap

The NBCSP's follow-up inequity is most acute for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples:

- **Kit return rate:** 31.3% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, compared with ~44% for non-Indigenous eligible Australians
- **Positivity rate among those screened:** approximately 9% — higher than the 6–7% national average, reflecting higher underlying bowel cancer risk and greater prevalence of risk factors (diet, tobacco, alcohol, lower colonoscopy surveillance history)
- **Follow-up diagnostic assessment rate:** lower than non-Indigenous participants; median time between screening and follow-up is longer
- **Five-year bowel cancer survival:** **54%** for Indigenous Australians versus **71%** for the broader population — a gap that reflects both later stage at diagnosis (partly driven by lower participation and delayed follow-up) and differential access to treatment once diagnosed

A 2025 study examining Aboriginal community perspectives in South Australia found strong support for lowering the NBCSP eligibility age for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from 50 to 40, given the earlier onset of bowel cancer in this population. The Northern Territory has the lowest NBCSP participation of any jurisdiction at 25.3% (2023–24). The DoHDA's "Closing the Gap" bowel screening initiative — which includes the health-care-provider-led alternative access model introduced in 2022 (allowing ACCHOs and primary care services to distribute kits directly) — has improved kit access in some communities but has not yet materially moved follow-up colonoscopy rates.

A 2024 publication in *Public Health Research & Practice* noted that the key bottleneck is not kit distribution but post-positive-result follow-up: remote Aboriginal communities frequently have no local colonoscopy service, requiring travel to a regional or metropolitan centre, with associated costs and logistics that create high dropout rates between receiving a positive result and completing the procedure.

### 5. The Direct Access Colonoscopy Model: Reform That Works, But Patchily

The most significant structural reform to the follow-up pathway has been the **Direct Access Colonoscopy (DAC)** model, pioneered in NSW and subsequently adopted (in varying forms) by other jurisdictions:

**How it works:** Under DAC, a GP can refer a patient with a positive NBCSP iFOBT directly to a public hospital colonoscopy booking service, bypassing the specialist outpatient queue. A nurse-led telephone triage assessment (replacing the face-to-face gastroenterologist consultation) determines suitability and schedules the procedure. There is no out-of-pocket cost to the patient.

**Evidence base:**
- DAC achieves wait times approximately 12 days shorter than standard public outpatient pathways
- Patients using DAC are less likely to wait >120 days (31.1% vs 37.5% via usual care)
- A 2026 economic evaluation published in *Public Health Research & Practice* found nurse-led virtual triage costs $115 more per patient upfront than usual care but projects $46 per patient in 10-year net healthcare savings (from earlier polyp detection and avoided cancer treatment costs) — net cost-effective
- DAC also reduces environmental costs: an estimated 4 kg CO₂ equivalent saved per in-person specialist attendance avoided

**Limitations:** DAC is operational in NSW, parts of Victoria, and South Western Sydney PHN; it is not uniformly available across Australia. Rural and remote patients still face geographic access barriers to colonoscopy even with a DAC referral in hand — a public hospital with a DAC pathway in Newcastle does not solve the absence of a colonoscopist in Katherine.

Victoria implemented new statewide referral criteria for Category 1 colonoscopy patients from February 2024, using a streamlined access model that shares some DAC features — patients can be placed directly on the wait list without an outpatient appointment under defined criteria.

### 6. The Colonoscopy Workforce Constraint

The supply-side constraint underpinning the follow-up gap is an **endoscopy workforce and capacity problem** that predates the NBCSP and that the program's design did not adequately anticipate:

- Australia currently trains approximately 50–60 gastroenterologists per year (from a base of approximately 700 practising gastroenterologists); the looming shortage has been flagged by the Gastroenterological Society of Australia (GESA) since the early 2020s
- The NBCSP generates approximately 55,000–65,000 positive iFOBT results per year requiring colonoscopy, based on 42% participation and ~6% positivity among ~2.2 million participants
- Public endoscopy lists are under pressure not just from NBCSP demand but from surveillance colonoscopy (following prior polyps), symptomatic referrals (rectal bleeding, change in bowel habit), and the growing backlog caused by COVID-19 disruption in 2020–21
- GESA's workforce modelling projects that demand for colonoscopy will grow significantly as the population ages and as the expanded 45–74 age range matures into the program — without a corresponding increase in training positions or endoscopy suite capacity
- The ACSQHC Atlas identified **overuse of repeat surveillance colonoscopy** in low-risk patients (surveillance intervals shorter than clinical guidelines recommend) as a significant capacity leakage: a 2021 study found that approximately 30–40% of repeat colonoscopies in Australia occur at earlier intervals than evidence-based guidelines recommend, representing a material diversion of endoscopy capacity

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The 30-Day Target Is Aspirational, Not Operational

Australia's clinical standard calls for colonoscopy within 30 days of a positive iFOBT. The data shows 14.6% compliance nationally. No credible policy has been funded to close this gap. The ACSQHC's September 2025 Clinical Care Standard revised the benchmark to four months — a pragmatic concession that the system cannot deliver 30-day access at scale. The question of whether the NBCSP should fund follow-up colonoscopy (as it funds the iFOBT test itself) has never been resolved: the structural separation between Commonwealth-funded test and state-funded procedure creates accountability diffusion that is a root cause of the timeliness failure.

### Tension 2: Overuse and Underuse Coexist in the Same System

The most striking finding from the ACSQHC Atlas is that the system simultaneously has too many colonoscopies in the wrong place (low-risk surveillance patients, short-interval repeats) and too few in the right place (post-NBCSP positive results, high-risk patients in remote and disadvantaged areas). This is a prioritisation failure, not purely a capacity failure. The solutions are partly clinical (guideline adherence on surveillance intervals), partly administrative (DAC-style triage to redirect lists toward NBCSP patients), and partly systemic (ring-fencing public endoscopy capacity for NBCSP follow-up).

### Tension 3: The Funding Boundary at the Most Critical Transition Point

The NBCSP's funding ends at the moment of maximum clinical urgency — the positive iFOBT. The test is free; the follow-up is not. For the 42% of Australians without comprehensive PHI who cannot access fast-tracked private colonoscopy, the path to follow-up runs through public hospital waiting rooms. State governments fund that pathway; the Commonwealth funds the screening program; neither has full accountability for the outcome. The 2025 RACS/GESA/CSSANZ joint position paper on bowel cancer screening in Australia and New Zealand explicitly called for a funded colonoscopy pathway within the NBCSP, arguing that the two-tier follow-up structure undermines the program's equity case.

### Tension 4: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People Face a Compound Gap

Indigenous Australians face a compound disadvantage: lower screening participation (31.3% kit return), higher underlying cancer risk and positivity rates (~9%), lower follow-up completion, longer follow-up wait times, and the worst 5-year survival outcomes (54% vs 71%). These gaps compound each other — a person who is less likely to participate, more likely to test positive, less likely to complete follow-up, and farther from treatment resources faces a mortality risk profile qualitatively different from the mainstream screening scenario. Yet the policy response — improved kit access via ACCHOs, an $3.5 million Deadly Choices extension in 2025–26 — is concentrated at the first step (kit distribution) rather than the critical second step (follow-up colonoscopy for positive results in remote or regional communities).

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Colorectal cancer treatment funding: surgical pathway, chemotherapy costs, and survival by stage.** The 1-in-26 positive colonoscopy finding of bowel cancer feeds into a treatment system with its own funding complexity — public vs private surgical access, MBS items for colorectal resection, chemotherapy PBS listings, and the stage-at-diagnosis survival curve. The NBCSP's follow-up gap directly affects which stage bowel cancer is treated at.

2. **Surveillance colonoscopy overuse: guideline adherence, MBS reform, and redirecting capacity.** The ACSQHC Atlas finding that 30–40% of repeat colonoscopies occur at intervals shorter than evidence-based guidelines represents a significant system inefficiency. What would a MBS reform targeting surveillance interval compliance actually look like, and what would it unlock for NBCSP follow-up?

3. **Early-onset colorectal cancer: rising rates under 50 and NBCSP age eligibility expansion.** The expansion of the NBCSP to 45–49 (July 2024) reflects growing evidence of rising colorectal cancer rates in younger adults globally. A 2025 modelling study (*ScienceDirect*) re-evaluated screening recommendations to address this trend. How does Australia's age eligibility compare internationally, and what are the economic and equity arguments for further lowering the age?

4. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander bowel cancer: colonoscopy access in remote communities as a specific infrastructure problem.** The follow-up gap for First Nations people is primarily a service supply problem — there are no colonoscopists in remote communities, and the logistics of travel create high attrition between a positive result and a colonoscopy. This thread would examine specific models (fly-in specialist services, telehealth pre-procedure triage, same-day visit models) and their funding.

5. **The MBS and colonoscopy: who pays, how much, and what rebate reform could look like.** A closer look at the MBS item structure for colonoscopy (items 32090, 32093, 32220), the gap between schedule fees and actual charges, the role of anaesthetist fees, and whether targeted MBS reform could improve access equity for uninsured patients with a positive NBCSP result.
