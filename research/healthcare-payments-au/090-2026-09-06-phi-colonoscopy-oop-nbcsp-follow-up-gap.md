# The NBCSP Follow-Up Gap: Private Health Insurance, Out-of-Pocket Costs, and the Two-Tier Colonoscopy Access Problem

**Report 090 — 2026-09-06**

**Summary:** When an Australian receives a positive result from the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program, the government's follow-up pathway is effectively bifurcated: public patients receive a free colonoscopy but wait a median of 132–202 days (only 14.7% are seen within the recommended 30 days); private patients with appropriate insurance get access within days to weeks, often at little or no out-of-pocket cost, but those on lower-tier policies or without insurance face out-of-pocket costs ranging from $180 gap fees up to $2,500–$5,000 for the full private package. The structure creates a screening system where the people most likely to defer costly follow-up are also those most likely to present later with advanced disease.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 088 documented that Australia's 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard governs the quality of colonoscopy where it is performed, but has no mechanism to address access where services are absent or unaffordable. Today's report examines one dimension of that access gap — the interaction between Australia's private health insurance (PHI) tiers, the MBS rebate structure, and the out-of-pocket costs that NBCSP-positive patients face when seeking timely follow-up.

---

## Background: The NBCSP Follow-Up Obligation

The National Bowel Cancer Screening Program sends a free immunochemical faecal occult blood test (iFOBT) to Australians aged 45–74 every two years. A positive result — blood detected in the sample — requires diagnostic follow-up, almost always colonoscopy, to determine whether cancer, pre-cancerous polyps, or other pathology is present. Critically, colonoscopy is not funded by the NBCSP itself: the program funds the test kit and the associated participant support infrastructure, but the follow-up diagnostic procedure is funded through Medicare (if performed in the public system or as a private outpatient), or through the combination of Medicare, PHI, and out-of-pocket payments (if performed privately).

This means a positive NBCSP result generates a referral — but the funding pathway for acting on that referral depends entirely on the patient's insurance status, geography, and willingness and ability to pay.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Follow-Up Rate Problem

In 2024, 73,724 Australians received a positive NBCSP result — approximately 5.8% of those who screened. Of these, 85% were recorded as having had a diagnostic colonoscopy. But of those who did proceed, only **14.7% received it within the recommended 30-day benchmark** (up marginally from 13.9% in 2023). The median actual wait ranged from **132 days in Western Australia to 202 days in the Northern Territory** — a four-to-seven month wait for a procedure where early cancer detection is the entire point (AIHW, National Bowel Cancer Screening Program Monitoring Report 2026).

The 30-day benchmark is clinically significant: cancer prognosis can worsen if diagnostic assessment is delayed beyond 120 days when a malignancy is present. Of NBCSP-positive patients who do receive a colonoscopy, approximately 1 in 29 are diagnosed with a confirmed or suspected cancer — a rate that underscores that follow-up deferral is not an academic concern.

### 2. The Two-Tier Access Structure

**Public pathway (free, slow):** Patients referred to public hospitals as public patients pay nothing for their colonoscopy. But wait times are long. Only 14.7% are seen within 30 days. The practical reality is that a NBCSP-positive result in the public pathway means months of waiting.

**Private pathway (faster, but complex costs):** Private patients — whether through PHI or self-funded — can access colonoscopy within days to weeks at most providers. But the cost structure is layered and opaque.

### 3. What Private Health Insurance Actually Covers — and Where It Doesn't

Colonoscopy falls under the **Gastrointestinal Endoscopy** clinical category in the PHI product tier framework. This clinical category is a **mandatory inclusion in Bronze tier hospital cover and above**, and in many Basic Plus policies. However, it is not required in Basic tier policies — the lowest tier of hospital cover.

This means Australians holding Basic hospital cover — typically younger, lower-income policyholders attracted by the lower premiums — may not have colonoscopy coverage at all. If they receive a positive NBCSP result, they face either the public wait or self-funding a private colonoscopy.

For those with appropriate PHI coverage, the picture is broadly positive for the procedure itself:
- **76% of privately insured colonoscopy patients had no out-of-pocket costs** for the specialist component of the procedure in 2023–24 (Medical Costs Finder, Department of Health)
- For the 24% who did pay a gap, the average gap was **approximately $180**
- Colonoscopy is typically a day procedure performed in a private day hospital; PHI "no-gap" and "known-gap" arrangements cover the majority of patients

However, several costs fall outside this relatively benign picture:

**Hospital excess/co-payments:** PHI policies typically carry an annual excess of $0–$750 per hospital admission (higher for couples and families). An excess applies each time a patient is admitted, even for a day procedure. If a patient's PHI excess is $500, their total out-of-pocket could be $500 + $180 average gap = $680 — paid in one hit for a single follow-up.

**Anaesthesia gap fees:** Colonoscopy requires procedural sedation or general anaesthesia. The anaesthetist bills separately from the proceduralist. Even in no-gap arrangements, the anaesthetist is not always covered by the same agreement. Anaesthesia gap fees — when charged — average several hundred dollars.

**Pathology:** If polyps are removed or biopsies taken (common in NBCSP-positive colonoscopies), histopathology is billed separately and may attract a gap, typically $40–$130 per specimen.

The average gap for all hospital treatment rose by **7.7% in 2025**, from $437 to $471, reflecting broader cost pressure across PHI-covered procedures.

### 4. The Uninsured Patient: A Structural Gap

Approximately 45% of Australians do not hold private hospital insurance. For an uninsured NBCSP-positive patient, the choice is stark:

- **Public patient:** Free, but 132–202 day median wait, with less than 15% chance of being seen in the recommended 30 days
- **Private self-fund:** Total cost depends heavily on provider. Fixed-price no-gap packages (specialist + anaesthesia + facility, bundled) are available from some providers at **$1,350–$1,950**. Most providers, however, charge **$2,500–$5,000** for the full episode, with MBS covering 75–85% of the schedule fee for each component

For a self-funded patient attending a non-bundled provider, the total out-of-pocket could reach:
- Specialist consultation: schedule fee ~$350, MBS rebates 75–85% → patient pays $50–$90
- Proceduralist colonoscopy: schedule fee ~$500, but specialist charges often $700–$1,200 → patient gap $200–$700
- Anaesthesia: schedule fee ~$450, specialist charges often above this → patient gap $100–$300
- Day facility fee: schedule fee ~$750, no insurance → patient pays most of this → $500–$1,000+
- Pathology (if applicable): $50–$130

**Total uninsured self-funded estimate: $850–$2,200+ out-of-pocket on top of Medicare rebates**, at standard private provider rates; up to $5,000+ at premium facilities.

### 5. Equity Implications: Who Bears the Cost?

The Colonoscopy Atlas Focus Report (September 2025) found that colonoscopy rates in the most affluent areas were **3,067 per 100,000** — nearly double the rate in the most disadvantaged areas at **1,675 per 100,000**. Socioeconomic disadvantage and lower PHI coverage rates are correlated: lower-income Australians are more likely to be in the public system, face longer waits, and are more likely to defer follow-up.

South Australian modelling (PLOS One, 2022) found that average costs per person for colorectal cancer treatment were **29% higher in the lowest socioeconomic quintile** than in the highest ($11,997 vs $9,281) — largely because later-stage cancer at diagnosis drives treatment intensity. This is the downstream cost of deferring the $180 gap or the $500 excess that prevented timely follow-up.

For First Nations Australians — documented in Report 087 to have lower follow-up colonoscopy rates than the general population — the combination of geographic remoteness, lower PHI coverage rates, and lower incomes compounds the financial access barrier with the supply barrier.

### 6. The No-Gap Mechanism: Partial Solution

The PHI no-gap and known-gap framework, introduced in the early 2000s, remains the primary policy tool for managing out-of-pocket costs in private hospital procedures. Under a "no-gap" agreement, both the proceduralist and anaesthetist agree to accept the combined Medicare rebate plus the PHI benefit as full payment.

For colonoscopy, no-gap arrangements are common — but not universal. Rural and regional areas have fewer no-gap providers (consistent with the overall colonoscopy access gap). Proceduralists who do not participate in gap cover arrangements are within their rights to charge above the schedule fee; the 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard's transparency provisions (Quality Statement 1 on referral and informed decision-making, and the Medical Costs Finder tool referenced in Report 016) address this partially, but compliance with fee disclosure requirements remains uneven.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: NBCSP creates the referral but not the funding pathway
The government funds the test but not the follow-up. A positive NBCSP result is a clinical obligation created by a government program — the ethical expectation is that follow-up should be accessible regardless of ability to pay. The current structure does not guarantee this.

### Tension 2: The PHI tier system creates a screening insurance gap at the bottom
Basic-tier PHI holders may screen positive through an NBCSP kit distributed by their GP, only to discover their hospital cover excludes the follow-up. The clinical category framework protects them for many acute conditions, but Gastrointestinal Endoscopy is not in the Basic tier floor.

### Tension 3: The no-gap framework is voluntary for providers
There is no regulatory requirement for colonoscopists to participate in no-gap arrangements. The Medical Costs Finder increases transparency, but price information without coverage closes the gap only for patients who can afford to shop.

### Tension 4: Public pathway improvement is stalled
The 30-day benchmark has barely moved despite awareness of the problem. Structural solutions — public-sector capacity, open access colonoscopy pathways, extended scope endoscopy by nurse practitioners — remain underfunded or poorly implemented at scale.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Open access colonoscopy in Australia's public system.** Direct GP referral to a public colonoscopy service (without specialist consultation first) is the central mechanism through which public-sector capacity can be increased. The 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard acknowledged open access pathways in Quality Statement 1 for the first time. How does this model work, what states have implemented it, and can it materially reduce the 30-day waiting time gap?

2. **PHI premium trajectory and the affordability spiral.** PHI premiums have risen faster than inflation for most of the past decade. The 2025 report card on PHI (AMA) showed further premium growth pressure. If PHI becomes unaffordable for lower-income earners and they drop to Basic or no cover, the NBCSP follow-up gap will widen.

3. **The 20-year NBCSP performance record: how much did it achieve?** Colorectal cancer mortality has fallen from approximately 44 to 23 per 100,000 since 2006. What can be attributed to the NBCSP, what to treatment advances, and does the follow-up lag mean a portion of the potential mortality benefit was never realised?

4. **Nurse practitioner and advanced practice colonoscopists.** International evidence suggests trained nurse endoscopists can perform colonoscopy at quality equivalent to gastroenterologists for surveillance and diagnostic purposes. Australia has not adopted this model at scale. Could expanding the colonoscopist workforce to include non-medical practitioners reduce both the cost and access gaps?

5. **CADx and the resect-and-discard strategy: reducing the pathology cost cascade.** Computer-aided characterisation (CADx) tools can optically diagnose small polyp histology in real time, potentially eliminating the cost and delay of routine histopathology for low-risk lesions. This would reduce the per-episode cost of NBCSP follow-up colonoscopy for uninsured patients.
