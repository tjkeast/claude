# Hospital Drug and Therapeutics Committees: Australia's Accidental Pre-PBS Subsidy System

**One-line summary:** State-based hospital Drug and Therapeutics Committees (DTCs) have become an unplanned, uncoordinated, and inadequately funded safety net for medicines stuck in the TGA-to-PBS gap — providing free access to some patients at major metropolitan cancer centres while the same medicines are inaccessible or unaffordable for patients at rural hospitals or in the community.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 110 documented the 726-day TGA-to-PBS access gap and identified the state hospital formulary system as one of the key mechanisms patients use to bridge it. This report examines how that mechanism works in practice — its governance, its funding, its limitations, and why it has evolved into a parallel medicine subsidy system that operates without national coordination or equity safeguards.

---

## Background

Every public hospital in Australia operates a **Drug and Therapeutics Committee** (DTC) — also called a Medicines and Therapeutics Committee (MTC) — that governs which medicines can be prescribed within that hospital, under what conditions, and at whose cost. These committees exist in every jurisdiction, from major metropolitan teaching hospitals to small rural district hospitals, and their decisions determine which medicines patients can access free of charge within a public hospital setting.

The PBS governs which medicines the Commonwealth subsidises in the community (outpatient pharmacy). Hospitals operate under a different framework: the state or territory government funds the medicines used in inpatient care, and increasingly in outpatient clinics, through the hospital system's own budget. In theory these are separate domains — PBS handles the community and hospitals handle admitted care. In practice, the boundary has dissolved. As the TGA-to-PBS access gap has grown, public hospital outpatient clinics have become the primary site where patients access medicines that are TGA-approved but not yet PBS-listed: the hospital's DTC decides whether to fund them, and the state government bears the cost.

No national framework governs how hospitals make these decisions. No national data collection tracks how much state governments spend on non-PBS medicines provided through hospital formularies. No equity mechanism ensures that patients at rural hospitals in Queensland receive the same access as patients at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre in Melbourne. The system has grown by accretion — each hospital, each state health system, responding individually to the access gap — producing a patchwork that is inequitable, expensive, and invisible to national health policy.

## Key Findings

### 1. How Hospital DTCs Work — and Why They Matter for the Access Gap

A DTC's core function is formulary management: deciding which medicines are "formulary" (available for routine prescribing at that hospital) and "non-formulary" (requiring individual approval). For PBS-listed medicines, DTCs primarily manage generic substitution, dose standardisation, and prescribing quality — the PBS listing decision has already been made federally. For non-PBS medicines, DTCs exercise de facto health technology assessment: evaluating whether a medicine is clinically appropriate, whether it should be funded from the hospital's own budget, and on what terms.

This function has expanded dramatically as the TGA-to-PBS access gap has grown. In oncology in particular, it is now routine for an oncologist to prescribe a TGA-approved, PBS-unlisted medicine for an outpatient and request that the hospital's DTC approve funding. If the DTC approves, the patient receives the medicine free. If the DTC declines, the patient pays out-of-pocket, joins a company-run Medicines Access Program (MAP) if one exists, or goes without.

The **Council of Australian Therapeutic Advisory Groups (CATAG)**, the national peak body for hospital DTC governance, released updated **Guiding Principles for Medicines and Therapeutics Committees** in June 2025. The principles provide a nationally endorsed framework for how DTCs should operate: their composition, decision-making processes, documentation standards, and communication obligations. A key message in the 2025 principles is that "standardisation of hospital medicines governance supports consistency in clinical care and equity in medicines access for patients both within those hospitals and at transitions of care." The existence of these principles — and the recognition that inconsistency is a problem — reflects how far the uncoordinated patchwork has grown.

### 2. The Architecture of Hospital Medicine Funding

**Activity-Based Funding (ABF)** is the primary mechanism through which state and territory governments fund public hospitals. Under ABF, hospitals receive payment for each episode of care — including inpatient admissions and, increasingly, outpatient clinic visits — based on a nationally set efficient price. Medicine costs are bundled into these episode-based payments, not reimbursed separately, creating a direct incentive for hospitals to use cheaper medicines where clinically appropriate.

ABF creates a structural problem for high-cost non-PBS medicines. If an oncologist prescribes a novel PBS-unlisted cancer medicine costing $30,000 per cycle in outpatient clinic, the ABF payment for that clinic visit may be $500–$1,000 — the cost of the medicine is not reimbursed. The hospital absorbs the difference. This is not a bug in the system; it was designed when PBS-unlisted oncology medicines were rare. As the access gap has grown and the volume of PBS-unlisted cancer medicines has expanded, this mechanism has imposed material costs on hospital pharmacy budgets that are not visible in any national accounting.

**Highly Specialised Therapies (HST) Program:** Recognising that some therapies are so expensive they cannot be absorbed into standard ABF, Australian governments created the HST pathway under the National Health Reform Agreement. Therapies assessed by the Medical Services Advisory Committee (MSAC) and costing above $200,000 per patient are funded jointly by the Commonwealth and states/territories on a 50:50 basis, delivered at designated public hospitals. CAR-T cell therapies funded under this model include:
- **Kymriah** (tisagenlecleucel): for relapsing/refractory acute lymphoblastic leukaemia — delivered at The Royal Children's Hospital, Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre, and Alfred Health (all Victoria)
- **Yescarta** (axicabtagene ciloleucel): for relapsing/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma — Peter Mac and Alfred Health
- **Carvykti** (ciltacabtagene autoleucel): for fifth-line relapsing/refractory multiple myeloma — Peter Mac and Alfred Health
- **Luxturna** (voretigene neparvovec): for inherited retinal dystrophies — Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital

The HST program provides a structured, nationally coordinated funding model. Its limitation is its scope: it covers only MSAC-assessed therapies above a high cost threshold, delivered at designated centres. The vast middle ground — cancer medicines costing $10,000–$150,000 per patient per year, TGA-approved but PBS-unlisted — is not covered by HST and falls entirely to hospital DTCs and state pharmacy budgets.

### 3. Medicines Access Programs: The Company-DTC Interface

Pharmaceutical companies operating in the TGA-to-PBS gap typically run **Medicines Access Programs (MAPs)**: structured arrangements under which the company provides medicines to patients at no cost, or at reduced cost, prior to PBS listing. MAPs are governed by CATAG's **Managing Medicines Access Programs: Guiding Principles** (updated 2025), which set out what DTCs should require from companies: application data, patient eligibility criteria, safety monitoring obligations, and reporting requirements.

MAPs create a three-way relationship between the company, the hospital DTC, and the patient. The DTC approves the patient's participation in the MAP, the company supplies the medicine free, and the DTC monitors safety outcomes. This arrangement suits companies because it generates real-world data that strengthens future PBAC submissions; it suits hospitals because it does not draw on the pharmacy budget; and it suits patients because it provides free access.

MAPs are not a satisfactory substitute for PBS listing. Research has documented **variability in MAPs across institutions** — some hospitals have access to programs others do not know exist; some large metropolitan centres operate dozens of MAPs while rural district hospitals operate none; equity of access is highly correlated with hospital size, specialist workforce, and geography. A study of MAPs in Australian and New Zealand hospitals found lack of clinical monitoring, inequity of access, and operational inconsistencies that make the MAP landscape "opaque and inequitable" rather than a systematic pre-PBS access mechanism.

CATAG's 2025 guiding principles require DTCs to maintain a register of MAPs and report patient numbers to companies as contractually required. The principles note that DTCs should consider "equity of access to programs offered by pharmaceutical companies" — an implicit acknowledgment that the current system does not deliver it.

### 4. The Geographic Equity Failure

The structural inequity of the hospital DTC system is not subtle. It produces dramatically different access outcomes based on where a patient receives care.

**Major metropolitan cancer centres** — Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (Melbourne), Royal Prince Alfred Hospital (Sydney), Princess Alexandra Hospital (Brisbane), Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital (Perth) — have dedicated oncology pharmacy teams, active DTC processes, established relationships with pharmaceutical companies, and the legal and logistical capacity to operate MAPs. When a TGA-approved cancer medicine is not on the PBS, a patient at one of these centres is likely to be offered hospital formulary funding or a MAP.

**Rural district hospitals** and regional base hospitals typically do not have: specialist oncology pharmacists with bandwidth to review MAP applications; DTC subcommittees with oncology expertise to evaluate non-PBS cancer medicines; relationships with the pharmaceutical companies operating MAPs; or hospital pharmacy budgets with capacity to absorb high-cost non-formulary medicines. A patient at Broken Hill Hospital, Mount Isa Hospital, or Kalgoorlie Health Campus — receiving care locally rather than travelling 1,000 km to a metropolitan cancer centre — may not be offered the same non-PBS medicine that would be provided free at the metropolitan centre.

The rural health spending gap compounds this. AIHW data establishes that in 2023–24, state and territory governments spent $82.0 billion on health — but the per-capita health spending shortfall for rural and remote Australians was estimated at $848 per person per year as of 2020–21 (National Rural Health Alliance analysis). Hospital pharmacy budgets are not exempt from the per-capita underfunding that characterises rural health more broadly.

CATAG's 2025 principles acknowledge that "access to high-cost medicines is currently inconsistent and frequently creates large variations in care" and call for "transparency and standardisation in decision-making, evaluation and review between jurisdictions." This framing treats geographic inequity as a governance problem amenable to process standardisation. But process standardisation alone cannot address the underlying resource disparity between a metropolitan cancer centre and a rural district hospital.

### 5. The Cost-Shifting Dynamic — Invisible to National Accounts

A central problem with the hospital DTC system as a pre-PBS access mechanism is that the costs are invisible in national health accounts. The AIHW's health expenditure reporting tracks hospital spending ($113.8 billion in 2023–24) and PBS spending ($23.1 billion in 2024–25) as separate line items. Medicines provided to hospital inpatients are embedded in hospital cost data and not separately identified. Medicines provided to outpatients through hospital pharmacy are increasingly in a grey zone: sometimes captured in hospital pharmacy data, sometimes not.

This invisibility has two policy consequences:

**First**, there is no national mechanism to track the total cost to state governments of medicines they are providing in lieu of PBS coverage. When tucatinib, for example, is prescribed for a patient with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer at a major cancer centre that has approved hospital formulary funding, the state health department is bearing the full cost of a medicine the Commonwealth would eventually fund through the PBS. This cost-shifting from Commonwealth to state is not intentional — it is a product of the access gap — but it is real and growing.

**Second**, the absence of national data means there is no political visibility for the costs states are bearing. Health ministers cannot easily calculate the total spend on non-PBS, hospital-funded medicines across their jurisdiction. The only partial measure is private prescription expenditure (AIHW estimates approximately $1.14 billion per year for 2023–24), but this does not capture state hospital pharmacy expenditure on non-PBS medicines at all.

Industry analyses have noted that with approximately **800 cancer medicines currently in research pipelines** globally, the volume of TGA-approved, PBS-unlisted medicines is expected to increase dramatically over the next decade. As the PBS access gap grows, so does the volume of medicines that fall onto hospital pharmacy budgets — without any national funding mechanism or equity assurance.

### 6. The 2025 Governance Update and Its Limitations

CATAG's June 2025 guiding principles for Medicines and Therapeutics Committees represent the most significant national governance update in the hospital DTC space in a generation. Key reforms recommended:

- **Equity considerations** must be formally included in every DTC approval process, including for access to non-formulary and non-PBS medicines
- **Cost-effectiveness analysis** should be incorporated into DTC decision frameworks, aligning hospital-level evaluations with PBAC methodology
- **Standardised documentation** of DTC decisions should be maintained and available for review
- **MAP governance** should be systematic and not dependent on individual relationships between pharmacists and company representatives
- **Rural and small hospital support:** larger health services and state-level therapeutic advisory groups should provide DTC governance support to smaller hospitals lacking internal capacity

These are genuine improvements. But they operate within a governance framework, not a funding framework. CATAG can mandate that a rural DTC follow a fair process for evaluating a MAP application; it cannot mandate that the company offering the MAP extends it to rural hospitals, or that the state government fund the medicine if no MAP exists.

The 2025 principles also acknowledge what they cannot fix: the absence of a national coordinating mechanism for decisions about pre-PBS access medicines in the hospital setting. The HTA Policy and Methods Review Recommendation 20 (a Commonwealth Bridging Fund) would, if implemented, provide a consistent national funding mechanism that bypasses the DTC patchwork — but as of September 2026, no Bridging Fund has been established.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Who should bear the cost of the access gap?** The current arrangement places the cost of medicines in the TGA-to-PBS gap partly on state governments (through hospital formularies), partly on companies (through MAPs), and partly on patients (for those who lack access to either). None of these is the appropriate long-term funder. The PBS is the appropriate mechanism, but it requires a PBS listing. A transitional Bridging Fund would place the cost on the Commonwealth — as it will eventually be in any case — but would require new federal budget allocation.

**Can process standardisation substitute for resource equity?** CATAG's governance approach assumes that better process will produce more equitable outcomes. This may be true at the margin — clearer DTC decision criteria may reduce arbitrary denials at under-resourced hospitals. But if a rural hospital lacks the pharmacy expertise, the company relationship, and the budget to approve a MAP or fund a high-cost medicine, process improvements produce consistent documentation of denial rather than consistent access.

**Is the HST program scalable?** The HST model — MSAC assessment, 50:50 Commonwealth-state funding, designated hospitals — works for a narrow class of extremely high-cost therapies. The question is whether it can be extended downward to cover the $10,000–$150,000 per-patient-per-year medicines in the access gap. Doing so would require either MSAC capacity expansion (it is already a bottleneck) or a new assessment pathway, and would require state governments to accept new jointly funded obligations that are not currently budgeted.

**Transparency as a precondition for reform:** No reform of the hospital DTC system is possible without knowing its true scale. The absence of national data on state hospital pharmacy expenditure for non-PBS medicines is a fundamental barrier. Without that data, the cost-shifting dynamic from Commonwealth to states cannot be quantified, the equity failure cannot be measured, and the case for a federal solution (Bridging Fund or HST extension) cannot be fully made.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The Medicines Australia Strategic Agreement 2022–2027 and the 2027 renegotiation:** The five-year strategic agreement between Medicines Australia and the Commonwealth governs price disclosure, PBS price reductions at listed milestones, managed entry obligations, and the broader commercial relationship. The 2027 renegotiation — in the context of the access gap crisis, Pfizer's disengagement, and HTA reform stalling — will be one of the most consequential negotiations in Australia's pharmaceutical policy history.

2. **Activity-Based Funding reform and hospital medicine pricing:** ABF's treatment of high-cost medicines as bundled into episode costs creates systematic underfunding of hospital pharmacy budgets. The Independent Health and Aged Care Pricing Authority (IHACPA) has worked on separate payment mechanisms for HSTs, but the gap between HST and standard ABF remains unaddressed for the mid-range cost medicines most commonly in the access gap.

3. **The private prescription market for PBS-unlisted medicines:** IQVIA and other market research firms have documented a growing private market in Australia where patients (and their private health insurers) pay full cost for TGA-approved, PBS-unlisted medicines. Characterising the size, composition, and equity profile of this market would quantify the true personal cost burden of the access gap.

4. **Parallel TGA-PBAC processing in practice:** The evidence from cancer medicines that have used the existing TGA-PBAC parallel process is mixed. A 2024 systematic review suggested the actual time saving was smaller than theoretical modelling predicted, largely because the parallel process does not eliminate sequential commercial negotiation. Exploring the real-world data would clarify what parallel processing can and cannot achieve.

5. **PHARMAC and the hospital formulary question:** New Zealand's PHARMAC controls both community and hospital medicine funding through a single national budget — eliminating the state-federal fragmentation that produces Australia's DTC patchwork. The trade-offs of that model (restricted access list, no managed entry for gene therapies) are directly illuminated by comparison with Australia's more fragmented but less restrictive system.
