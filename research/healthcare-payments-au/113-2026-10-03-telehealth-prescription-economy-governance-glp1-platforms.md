# Australia's Telehealth Prescription Economy: Platforms, Regulatory Gaps, and the GLP-1 Payment Architecture

**One-line summary:** A wave of direct-to-consumer telehealth platforms — led by Eucalyptus (acquired by Hims & Hers for up to US$1.15B in June 2026) — has built a billion-dollar private prescription economy in Australia outside the PBS and traditional GP system, filling the access gap for GLP-1 weight-loss medicines and raising acute questions about clinical safety, regulatory arbitrage, and what happens when commercial prescribing logic replaces continuity of care.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 112 documented that roughly 500,000 Australians are paying $395–$460/month for GLP-1 obesity medicines through private prescriptions while the PBS listing stalls — but it did not examine how those prescriptions reach patients. The answer is telehealth platforms operating outside the normal Medicare-GP ecosystem, and their governance is now Australia's #1 health provider compliance priority.

---

## Background

When the COVID-19 pandemic forced Australia to build a real-time telehealth infrastructure in March 2020, it also created a market entry opportunity that had not previously existed: the ability to deliver a consultation, a prescription, and a subscription product in a single digital transaction. Within two years of the pandemic, a cohort of venture-backed "digital health" companies had recognised that the most commercially attractive categories — weight loss, sexual health, hair loss, menopause, medicinal cannabis — were ones where patients were either underserved by the traditional GP system or had historically faced stigma seeking face-to-face care.

The result is Australia's telehealth prescription economy: a parallel healthcare financing system that runs almost entirely outside Medicare and the PBS, where the patient is both the consumer and the product, and where the consultation, the prescription, and the monthly medication supply are often vertically integrated in a single subscription.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Platform Market: Scale and Structure

The dominant player is **Eucalyptus Health**, founded in Sydney in 2020. Eucalyptus operates several direct-to-consumer brands:
- **Juniper** — weight management (Wegovy/semaglutide, tirzepatide) operating in Australia, the UK, Germany, and Japan
- **Pilot** — men's health (erectile dysfunction, hair loss, weight)
- **Kin** — women's reproductive health
- **Compound** — premium "medical concierge" service at up to A$1,495/month

By March 2025, Eucalyptus reported A$120.9 million in revenue over 18 months, with 55% year-on-year growth. By November 2025, it raised A$190 million in a Series funding round at a **A$1.37 billion valuation** — more than double its A$560 million valuation from 2023. Revenue growth was running at 130% year-on-year at the time of the raise. In February 2026, US-listed Hims & Hers Health announced the acquisition of Eucalyptus for **up to US$1.15 billion** — completed in June 2026 — making it Australia's largest digital health exit and bringing the country's dominant prescription platform under the ownership of a company under active US regulatory and Department of Justice scrutiny for its GLP-1 compounding practices.

Other significant players include: **Mosh** (men's health), **Hola Health**, **Abby Health**, **13SICK** (after-hours GP), **Updoc**, and **Medmate**. Most operate in one of four high-volume prescription categories: weight loss (GLP-1s), medicinal cannabis, peptides, and cosmetic injectables.

The Australian Telehealth Standards Consortium — convened by **Patients Australia** and including Eucalyptus, Medibank, Bupa, nib Group's Honeysuckle Health, Healthengine, MedAdvisor, Mosh, Medmate, 13SICK, and Wesfarmers Health — released a 34-page standards document in early 2026. The standards cover clinical governance, patient safety, informed consent, continuity of care, advertising, and technology. They are voluntary and self-accrediting. They are not endorsed by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners, by AHPRA, or by any formal regulatory body.

### 2. The Business Model: Where Medicare Ends and the Subscription Begins

The business model of these platforms creates a structural tension between Commonwealth health financing and commercial logic. There are three common variations:

**The full-private model**: The platform charges a monthly subscription bundled to include the online consultation, an ongoing weight management programme, and private GLP-1 medicine supply. No Medicare bulk billing. The patient pays $200–$700/month in full. This is the dominant model for Juniper's weight-management service. The medicine is not PBS-subsidised (for obesity indication), so the cost is unregulated — set at international list price benchmarks.

**The MBS consultation + private medicine split**: The platform bills Medicare for the consultation (typically using telehealth MBS items 91890/91899/23/36/91810 for video or phone attendances) while the patient separately pays for the private prescription. This model creates a subsidy asymmetry: the consultation is a public good, the medicine is not. It has attracted specific scrutiny from the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing (DoHDA) because it can function as a volume-driven consultation engine: the faster a doctor processes questionnaires and prescriptions, the higher the Medicare revenue.

**The questionnaire-only asynchronous model**: A patient completes an online health questionnaire; a doctor reviews and approves a prescription without a real-time consultation. This model, targeted at high-volume, time-sensitive prescriptions like GLP-1s and medicinal cannabis, became widespread between 2021 and 2025 and is now expressly prohibited under regulatory guidance. From a payment architecture perspective, it cannot legitimately claim a Medicare item — there is no consultation as defined under MBS requirements — but enforcement has been inconsistent.

### 3. The Regulatory Response: Slow, Partial, and Contested

Australia's regulatory response to the telehealth platform market has been slow and fragmented.

**Medical Board of Australia guidance (September 2023)**: The Medical Board introduced explicit telehealth prescribing guidelines requiring a real-time consultation (face-to-face, video, or telephone). The guidelines stated: "providing healthcare, including prescribing, via questionnaire-based asynchronous web-based tools in the absence of a real-time patient-doctor consultation is not good practice." This was targeted directly at questionnaire-only platforms. The guidelines do not apply to nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists, or other registered health practitioners — only to medical doctors.

**AHPRA guidance extension (October 2025)**: AHPRA extended the Medical Board's position across all **15 National Boards** — covering every regulated health profession — on 7 October 2025. This was a significant expansion: platforms that had shifted prescribing functions to nurse practitioners or other health practitioners to avoid the Medical Board's 2023 rules were now subject to the same standard.

**The 80/20 rule extended to telehealth**: Under Professional Services Review (PSR) thresholds, a GP who renders 80 or more services on 20 or more days in a 12-month period is automatically referred for investigation. From 2025, this calculation includes telehealth services — preventing the volume-prescribing models from being invisible to the PSR framework.

**PSR enforcement**: The PSR ordered over **$1.7 million in repayments** for inappropriate telehealth billing across five cases in the second half of 2024 alone, with the most common finding being failure to meet the "usual medical practitioner" or established relationship requirement for MBS telehealth items.

**Compliance letters**: More than **5,000 GPs** received compliance letters from DoHDA in May–June 2025 regarding overseas telehealth claiming — an issue where practitioners were billing MBS while conducting consultations from outside Australia.

**DoHDA Compliance Priorities 2026**: The Department of Health, Disability and Ageing placed on-demand telehealth platforms at the top of its five declared compliance priorities for 2026, specifically citing: "MBS servicing and requesting, and PBS prescribing, from on-demand telehealth platforms. The rise of these businesses raises concerns about opportunistic and/or inappropriate claiming of health program payments, as well as commercial incentives conflicting with clinical best practice, continuity of care and patient safety."

This is the first time the government has formally named the category as a compliance focus.

### 4. Patient Safety: The GLP-1 Eating Disorder Problem

The most acute patient safety concern to emerge in 2026 is the prescribing of GLP-1 weight-loss medicines to patients with active or historical eating disorders.

In August 2026, the ABC reported that a telehealth clinic had prescribed injectable GLP-1 weight-loss medicine to a patient with a documented history of anorexia nervosa. The patient's eating disorder history was not detected because the intake process relied on a questionnaire rather than a real-time consultation with clinical history-taking. Clinicians working in eating disorder medicine subsequently called for mandatory real-time screening for disordered eating before any GLP-1 weight-loss prescription.

The scale of the overlap is significant: global research indicates that over 30% of patients with eating disorders report having used GLP-1 medications. The appetite-suppression mechanism that makes GLP-1s effective for metabolic weight loss can reactivate restrictive eating patterns in patients with anorexia or ARFID history. Unlike a GP consultation — where a practitioner might notice physical signs of disordered eating, review a longitudinal medical record, or ask probing questions about eating behaviour — a questionnaire filtered for "contraindications" may not capture this history reliably.

The RACGP has been explicit: "asynchronous requests for medicines communicated by text, email, live-chat, online or based on the consumer completing a health questionnaire are unsafe outside of an existing and in-person therapeutic relationship." The college's position statement argues that continuity of care, not just the single consultation, is the safety mechanism — and that on-demand telehealth disrupts the entire longitudinal care relationship.

### 5. "Pesky Aussie Laws": The Marketing Problem

In late December 2025, a comment made by Eucalyptus/Juniper staff referencing "pesky Aussie laws" — apparently concerning TGA restrictions on direct-to-consumer advertising of prescription medicines to the public — attracted public scrutiny and ABC coverage. The TGA's Therapeutic Goods Advertising Code prohibits advertising prescription medicines directly to the public; social media promotion of Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Ozempic by telehealth platforms is an area of active TGA compliance monitoring.

This incident is emblematic of the deeper tension: telehealth platforms are built on consumer marketing logic (customer acquisition cost, retention, LTV) applied to prescription medicines that are legally regulated to prevent consumer-directed promotion. The business model requires reaching patients who want the medicine directly, but the regulatory framework requires clinical indication and prescriber-mediated access. The "workaround" — marketing weight management programmes rather than prescription medicines directly — is now under TGA scrutiny.

### 6. The Payment Architecture and Equity Inversion

The GLP-1 telehealth market has produced a payment architecture that inverts the equity logic of the Australian health system.

Obesity in Australia is highly correlated with socioeconomic disadvantage. The 2022–24 National Health Survey data shows that overweight and obesity rates are highest in the lowest-income quintile, in regional and remote areas, and in First Nations communities. These are precisely the populations who cannot access $395–$460/month private prescriptions, who are least likely to have established GP relationships that could support prescription and monitoring, and who are most likely to be seeking care through emergency departments or community health settings rather than telehealth apps.

The telehealth GLP-1 market, by contrast, serves primarily urban, higher-income patients with internet access, digital literacy, and disposable income for subscription medicine. This is not a structural failure of the platforms — they are operating rationally in a private market — but it is a structural failure of the public system to make effective obesity pharmacotherapy available to the population that needs it most.

The PBS listing of Wegovy (pending price negotiations as of October 2026) would, if completed, transform this access equation: PBAC has recommended listing for patients with established cardiovascular disease and obesity, with a projected co-payment of $7.70 (concessional) to $25.00 (general) per prescription. The gap between $460/month private and $7.70–$25/month PBS is the most acute access differential in current Australian medicine.

### 7. The Hims & Hers Acquisition: Regulatory Arbitrage Signal

The acquisition of Eucalyptus by Hims & Hers for up to US$1.15 billion requires careful reading in the context of US regulatory pressure. Hims & Hers was the subject of:
- A February 2026 FDA statement specifically identifying the company as marketing non-approved compounded GLP-1 drugs
- A February 2026 HHS referral to the Department of Justice for investigation of potential violations of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act

Hims & Hers' expansion into Australia, Japan, the UK, and Germany — via the Eucalyptus acquisition — occurred directly as its US compounded GLP-1 model came under regulatory closure. Australia's regulatory framework for telehealth prescribing, while tightening, remains substantially less restrictive than the US FDA framework for compounded medicines. The acquisition signals that international capital views Australia as a market with structural regulatory arbitrage potential — more permissive on direct-to-consumer prescription models than either the US or European equivalents.

Whether Australian regulators treat this signal as a reason to accelerate or to pause their response remains to be seen.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Enforcement vs. access**: Many patients who access mental health, sexual health, or weight management services through telehealth platforms would not have sought care through traditional GP channels. Tightening asynchronous prescribing rules protects some patients from harm but may simply exclude others from care. The net welfare effect is contested.

2. **The self-regulation credibility gap**: The Patients Australia-convened standards consortium is dominated by commercial participants (Eucalyptus, Medibank, Bupa, nib). Without RACGP endorsement, TGA backing, or AHPRA recognition, these "standards" have no more force than a code of conduct — and are easily characterised as industry-managed regulatory delay.

3. **The MyMedicare tension**: The November 2025 expansion of MyMedicare bulk-billing incentives (triple rate now available to all registered patients, not just concession holders) created a competitive distortion: traditional GP practices get the incentive for continuity-of-care registration, but on-demand platforms typically do not engage in MyMedicare registration — putting them at a structural disadvantage in price competition for bulk-billed consultations.

4. **Vertically integrated prescribing**: When the same entity employs the prescriber, sells the medicine, and provides the ongoing programme, the clinical independence of the prescriber is structurally compromised. Australia's professional indemnity insurers began flagging this conflict in 2025, and the RACGP's position statement refers directly to "commercial incentives conflicting with clinical judgement."

5. **The PBS listing race**: If Wegovy is PBS-listed in late 2026 or early 2027, the business model of telehealth weight-loss platforms changes overnight. A PBS-subsidised prescription dispensed by any community pharmacy eliminates the platform's medication-supply margin. Platforms would need to pivot to the programme, support, and monitoring services — which, unlike the medicine supply, cannot be easily commoditised.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Medicinal cannabis: the other telehealth prescription economy** — Australia's medicinal cannabis market ($1B+ annual private spend, 80%+ of SAS-B applications, now the dominant use case for the Special Access Scheme) shares the same telehealth platform architecture as GLP-1 and raises near-identical clinical governance questions, with a different product.

2. **PBS listing and commercial strategy: how Novo Nordisk is pricing Wegovy** — the SELECT trial framing was a deliberate strategy to get a cardiovascular-outcome listing rather than an obesity-indication listing, controlling the patient population size and the PBS price negotiation dynamics.

3. **Advertising restrictions and social media health promotion** — TGA Therapeutic Goods Advertising Code compliance on prescription medicines; how platforms use "programme" marketing to circumvent medicine advertising restrictions; the "pesky Aussie laws" issue in full.

4. **The compounding ban and its aftermath** — TGA's October 2024 ban on compounded GLP-1 preparations forced approximately 100,000 Australians off compounded products, redirected demand to commercial products at higher prices, and changed the economics of the private GLP-1 market.

5. **APRA and PHI reform for preventive medicines** — whether private health insurance should or could be reformed to fund GLP-1 obesity pharmacotherapy, given the cardiovascular risk-reduction evidence and the Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee's cardiovascular-indication recommendation.
