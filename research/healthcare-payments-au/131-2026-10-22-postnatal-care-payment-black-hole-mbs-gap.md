# Postnatal Care as a Payment Black Hole: What Medicare Funds — and Doesn't Fund — in the Weeks After Birth

**One-line summary:** After the birth itself, Australia's healthcare payment system largely withdraws: there is no Medicare rebate for postnatal physiotherapy, no public funding for lactation support, a midwifery MBS item set that reaches fewer than 1,400 practitioners nationally, and no standalone mental health screening item — leaving the six to twelve weeks after birth as the most medically dense and least publicly funded period of the maternity care continuum.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 130 examined the NSW Birth Trauma Inquiry's finding that birth trauma costs $17.5 billion per year and affects approximately one in three Australian women — but the inquiry's 43 recommendations focused primarily on what happens *during* labour. This report examines the parallel gap: what the payment system provides after women leave hospital.

---

## Background

Birth in Australia is one of the most extensively researched transitions in the healthcare system. The payment architecture around it has been studied in multiple MBS reviews, RANZCOG submissions, and women's health inquiries. Yet the postnatal period — roughly defined as the six to twelve weeks following birth, a window of acute clinical need for both physical recovery and mental health — is one of the most sparsely funded segments of the maternity continuum under the Medicare Benefits Schedule.

The consequences are well-documented: postnatal depression affects approximately 1 in 5 mothers (and 1 in 10 fathers); pelvic floor injury is present in up to 25% of women after vaginal delivery; breastfeeding discontinuation linked to inadequate support is associated with increased infant infection, hospitalisation risk, and maternal mental health deterioration. These are not rare edge cases — they are expected sequelae of a normal physiological event, and the healthcare system's payment architecture treats most of them as out-of-pocket problems.

## Key Findings

### 1. The MBS Postnatal Item Set: 1,355 Midwives for 310,000 Annual Births

The 1 March 2025 changes to the participating midwife MBS — part of the $56.5 million package announced in the 2024–25 Federal Budget — were the most significant reform to postnatal MBS items in many years. The changes (detailed in Report 130) included:

- **Item 82130** (short postnatal attendance): minimum 20 minutes, maximum duration removed
- **Item 82104** (new long postnatal attendance): minimum 90 minutes
- **Item 82140** (final postnatal appointment): minimum 60 minutes, includes birth debrief, mental health screening, and domestic violence screening; can be claimed between 4 and 8 weeks post-birth — extending the window from the previous 6 weeks
- Extended postnatal care window: the 2025 reforms extended MBS-funded postnatal midwifery care from 6 weeks to **8 weeks** post-birth

These are meaningful improvements. But they are available only to **endorsed midwives** — a specific regulatory category requiring an endorsement from the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia (NMBA) permitting independent practice and Medicare billing. As of late 2025, only approximately **1,355 endorsed midwives** nationally are eligible to bill these items.

With approximately **310,000 births per year**, the arithmetic is stark: there are roughly **one endorsed midwife for every 229 births annually**. The vast majority of Australian women giving birth in public hospitals — where midwifery care is provided by registered (not endorsed) midwives under public employment, not fee-for-service — do not interact with the MBS postnatal midwifery item set at all.

### 2. Home Visiting: The Public-Private Equity Fracture

Public hospital maternity services in most states provide postnatal home visiting — midwife-attended visits in the days to two weeks after hospital discharge. The Canberra Midcall service, Northern Sydney LHD's "Midwifery in the Home" program, and similar services across states offer state-funded midwifery care for up to 14 days post-discharge.

However, access is highly inequitable across sector and geography. A Queensland study found:
- **67.2%** of public maternity patients received a postnatal home visit from a nurse or midwife within 10 days of discharge
- **7.2%** of private sector patients received the equivalent service

Private patients — who by definition have paid for private health insurance — are almost entirely dependent on their obstetrician's outpatient appointments (which do not include home visiting) and whatever their midwife can provide via MBS items. The average postnatal visit cost from a privately-billing obstetrician is neither capped nor monitored; there is no requirement that it include the comprehensive physical and mental health assessment that public home visiting protocols mandate.

### 3. Pelvic Floor Physiotherapy: The APA's Unfunded Priority

Pelvic floor dysfunction following childbirth — including urinary and faecal incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse, and sexual health impacts — affects a substantial proportion of women who have given birth vaginally and a non-trivial proportion following caesarean. The Australian Physiotherapy Association (APA) estimates that approximately one in three women will experience urinary incontinence following childbirth.

There is **no MBS item** for postnatal pelvic health physiotherapy. As the APA stated in its November 2025 Physical Birth Trauma Position Statement: *"There is currently no public funding for pre-birth pelvic health assessment, individualised physiotherapy-led pelvic floor muscle training or the treatment of physical postnatal trauma."*

The APA's proposal — now over two years in advocacy — is for five individualised antenatal and postnatal pelvic health physiotherapy consultations funded through Medicare, covering screening, education, early treatment, and imaging referral. As of October 2026, there has been no government commitment to this item.

The only Medicare pathway for postnatal physiotherapy is indirect: via a GP Management Plan (chronic disease management, requiring an existing chronic condition), which does not map naturally onto the acute post-birth physiological context. Out-of-pocket cost for a postnatal pelvic floor physiotherapy consultation is typically $90–$160, with partial private health fund rebates depending on extras cover.

### 4. Lactation Support: No Medicare Rebate, Inconsistent PHI Coverage

Breastfeeding rates in Australia are among the weakest in comparable OECD nations for sustained exclusivity: the AIHW 2022 National Health Survey found 58% of infants were still being breastfed at 6 months, but only 16% exclusively. Inadequate lactation support is consistently cited in research as a primary driver of early discontinuation.

International Board Certified Lactation Consultants (IBCLCs) are the specialist workforce for breastfeeding support. There is **no Medicare rebate** for IBCLC consultations, with one narrow exception: if the lactation consultant is also an endorsed midwife with a Medicare provider number, the midwifery MBS items can be used.

Private health insurance coverage is inconsistent. PHI extras policies may provide partial rebates for "lactation consultant" services, but coverage and caps vary significantly between funds and policy tiers. Many Bronze and Basic level policies — increasingly common following the 2019 PHI reforms that enabled lower-cost tiers — do not cover lactation consultant services at all.

An initial IBCLC consultation costs approximately $200–$350 out of pocket. Women requiring multiple consultations, or home visits, face costs that accumulate rapidly in the first weeks after birth — a period when household income has typically just fallen and household expenditure risen sharply.

### 5. Perinatal Mental Health: The Block-Grant Workaround

Postnatal depression affects approximately 15–20% of women in the year following birth, with postnatal anxiety at similar rates. Birth-related PTSD, following traumatic birth experiences as documented in Report 130, affects a further sub-population.

The national response to perinatal mental health has been primarily through block-grant funded service models rather than MBS reform:

- **Gidget Foundation Australia Perinatal Mental Health Centres**: The Federal Government has committed $39.2 million (with a further $16.7 million election commitment for an additional 8 centres) to a national network of up to 20 Perinatal Mental Health Centres, operated by Gidget Foundation. These centres, which have been progressively opening since 2022 (with sites in SA, NT, WA, and outer Melbourne operational or announced by July 2026), provide free access to psychologist care via a GP referral and Mental Health Care Plan.

    Access model: free in-person or telehealth care with a psychologist. This sits within the Better Access architecture — the GP must establish a Mental Health Care Plan and the Medicare rebate flows through the MBS items 80000–80020 that the series examined in Report 011. The 20-session annual cap applies.

- **State-funded community health teams**: In most states, child and family health nurses provide postnatal welfare checks — not clinical mental health treatment — through community health centres and outreach services. These are state-funded, not MBS, and capacity is chronically constrained.

The **EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale)** — universally recommended in Australia's national perinatal depression guidelines — is administered by midwives, GPs, and child health nurses. There is no dedicated MBS item for this screening; it is bundled into standard consultation items.

The NSW Birth Trauma Inquiry's recommendation to the Commonwealth to extend Better Access from 20 to 40 sessions specifically for new parents experiencing birth-related PTSD had, as of October 2026, received no Commonwealth response.

### 6. RANZCOG and the 2026–27 Budget Gap

RANZCOG's response to the 2026–27 Federal Budget described it as having "fallen short of recognising women's health as a cost-of-living issue." Specific concerns included:
- No new dedicated postnatal MBS items for GPs providing postnatal care
- No commitment to the ongoing sustainability of private maternity services (where 25–30% of births occur)
- No funded gendered review of the MBS — only a commitment to undertake one

The 2024 *Cost of Birth Trauma in Australia* study (commissioned by Birth Trauma Australia) estimated that the annual cost of birth trauma is **$17.5 billion**, of which $1.4 billion represents direct health system costs — with the majority representing long-term productivity losses from undertreated physical and mental sequelae. If only a fraction of that $1.4 billion in direct costs is attributable to inadequate postnatal care coverage, the investment case for targeted MBS expansion is substantial.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Why is the MBS designed around the practitioner category rather than the need?** The postnatal care gap is not really about midwives — it is about funding care in the six to twelve weeks after birth. Whether that care is delivered by a GP, an endorsed midwife, a physiotherapist, a lactation consultant, or a psychologist, the underlying clinical need exists. The fragmentation of MBS items by practitioner type means that a woman's access to funded postnatal care is determined by what kind of practitioner she can access, not what care she needs.

2. **Does early hospital discharge create unfunded risk?** Most public maternity patients in Australia are discharged within 3–5 days; home visiting programs are of variable depth and duration. The mortality data — 59% of maternal deaths occur after birth — raises the question of whether early discharge combined with inadequate postnatal follow-up contributes to preventable deaths, particularly from sepsis, haemorrhage, and suicide.

3. **What is the right level of government for this problem?** Postnatal home visiting (state-funded), perinatal mental health centres (federal block grant), MBS items (federal), and PHI extras coverage (federal regulation, private delivery) operate in separate funding streams with no coordination mechanism. The result is care that is comprehensive in some public hospitals and almost absent in private settings and many rural areas.

4. **Is the 8-week window enough?** The 2025 extension of endorsed midwife MBS items to 8 weeks post-birth was welcomed, but the APA and RANZCOG have both argued for a longer postnatal care window — particularly for pelvic floor and mental health conditions that manifest or peak beyond 8 weeks. Birth-related PTSD may not be diagnosed until well into the first year.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Australia's national maternity strategy: the 2019 Woman-Centred Care framework and what comes after** — the federal policy architecture for the full maternity continuum and whether the birth trauma and postnatal care gaps create momentum for a comprehensive strategy update.

2. **Private maternity unit viability and the PHI policy downgrade epidemic** — how the shift of PHI members to Bronze/Basic policies without maternity cover is draining the private maternity sector of patients, with APRA and PHI regulatory implications.

3. **Endorsed midwife credentialing and hospital access rights** — the regulatory, legal, and professional barriers preventing endorsed midwives from exercising full-scope intrapartum care in hospitals, limiting both the MBS item reach and the continuity model that the evidence supports.

4. **First Nations maternal and perinatal health: the Close the Gap maternity targets** — the specific payment and access barriers facing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women, including Birthing on Country, IAHP/ACCHO funding for antenatal and postnatal care, and whether Close the Gap Target 2 (reduce under-5 mortality gap) is on track.

5. **The MBS gendered review: scope, methodology, and what a gender lens on the MBS would actually find** — RANZCOG and women's health advocates have called for a comprehensive audit of MBS items from a gender equity lens; what such a review might reveal about the structural underfunding of women's health across the lifecycle, not just in maternity.
