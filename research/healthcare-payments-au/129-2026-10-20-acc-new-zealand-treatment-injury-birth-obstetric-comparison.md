# New Zealand's ACC and the Treatment Injury Model: What Australia's Birth Injury Liability Crisis Could Learn from a No-Fault Neighbour

**One-line summary:** New Zealand's Accident Compensation Corporation eliminates nearly all birth-related litigation through a no-fault treatment injury framework, cutting obstetrician indemnity premiums to under $1,000 per year — roughly 1% of the Australian equivalent — but the scheme faces a looming $26.3 billion funding shortfall by 2030, raises unresolved questions about who pays when causation cannot be established, and has a significant coverage gap for babies who suffer birth brain injury without clear treatment causation.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Yesterday's report on Australia's medical indemnity and birth injury liability system documented how obstetric premiums of $65,000–$100,000+/year, a 21-year liability tail, and stalled NIIS medical treatment injury implementation are driving private maternity unit closures and pricing families out of private obstetric care; New Zealand's ACC is the most frequently cited policy alternative, and today's report examines how the scheme actually works — what it covers, what it costs the state, and what it leaves uncovered — as a concrete benchmark for Australian reform.

---

## Background

The Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) is one of the most distinctive institutions in Australasian public policy: a universal, no-fault injury compensation scheme that effectively abolishes personal injury litigation for almost all injuries, including those caused by medical treatment. New Zealand has operated some form of accident compensation since 1974, following the landmark 1967 Woodhouse Report, which recommended replacing the adversarial tort system with a state-funded rehabilitation-focused compensation model.

The medical treatment injury category — the part of ACC directly relevant to birth injury and obstetric liability — has evolved considerably since its introduction. The original "medical misadventure" framework proved difficult to administer and was replaced on 1 July 2005 with the "treatment injury" category, which has broader and more workable eligibility criteria. In October 2022, a further legislative extension added automatic coverage for a list of maternal birth injuries — the most significant recent reform to birth-related ACC coverage.

Understanding how ACC functions as an alternative to Australia's liability regime requires examining three distinct categories: treatment injury (the main pathway for medically-caused injury), maternal birth injury (the 2022 extension for the birthing parent), and babies' birth brain injury (the coverage gap that remains most contentious).

## Key Findings

### 1. The Treatment Injury Framework: What ACC Does and Doesn't Cover

Under sections 32–34 of the *Accident Compensation Act 2001* (NZ), a treatment injury is a personal injury caused by medical treatment, provided:

- There is a **clear causal link** between the treatment (or failure to treat) and the injury
- The injury is **not a necessary part or ordinary consequence** of the treatment (i.e., it goes beyond the expected risks)
- The injury is an **actual personal injury**, not merely a worsening of an underlying condition in the ordinary course of disease
- The treatment was given by, or at the direction of, a **registered health professional**

Critically, ACC is a **no-fault** scheme. The patient does **not** need to prove negligence. They must establish that treatment caused the injury — but they do not need to show that the clinician made a substandard decision or that the outcome was preventable. This is a substantially lower causation threshold than Australian negligence law, where the plaintiff must prove breach of duty (failure to meet the standard of a reasonable professional) and causation.

In exchange for ACC coverage, New Zealand patients effectively surrender their **common law right to sue for personal injury damages**. The *Accident Compensation Act 2001* bars personal injury claims for personal injury, with narrow exceptions — most notably for exemplary (punitive) damages where a defendant's conduct was reckless or outrageous. In practice, exemplary damages claims are rare and limited in quantum.

**The practical result for obstetricians:** The long-tail liability that drives Australian indemnity premiums to $65,000–$100,000+/year essentially does not exist in New Zealand. A New Zealand obstetrician who attends a birth in 2026 does not carry 21 years of personal liability exposure for that birth. Even if the baby or mother is injured, the claim routes through ACC, not through the practitioner's indemnity insurer. The practitioner still needs indemnity cover — New Zealand law does not exempt health professionals from all professional accountability — but the quantum of risk is a fraction of the Australian equivalent. Historical figures consistently put New Zealand doctor indemnity premiums at under NZD $1,000 per year regardless of specialty, compared with AUD $65,000–$100,000+ for full-scope obstetrics in Australia. Even accounting for currency differences, exchange rate fluctuations, and the likely inflation of NZ premiums since those figures were published, the structural difference is enormous.

### 2. Treatment Injury Claim Volumes and Acceptance Rates

ACC's treatment injury category processes approximately 17,000–20,000 decided claims per year, with an acceptance rate of approximately 78–80%.

Published OIA (Official Information Act) data gives the following lodgement-year figures for accepted and declined treatment injury claims:

| Financial Year | Accepted | Declined | Total Decided |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2018/19 | 11,813 | 5,785 | ~17,598 |
| 2019/20 | 12,221 | 5,144 | ~17,365 |
| 2020/21 | 14,189 | 5,359 | ~19,548 |
| 2021/22 | 13,951 | — | — |

*(Source: ACC OIA response GOV-031215, April 2024. Figures represent decided claims lodged in each year; the warning in the response notes figures may change if rerun.)*

A more recent OIA response (GOV-043297, October 2025) provides cost figures through 2024/25, reporting treatment injury costs of **NZD $411.5 million in 2024/25**. An older NZ Doctor article projected the scheme's total treatment injury outstanding liability at approximately **NZD $5.7 billion**, with a significant proportion of that — reportedly around $3.9 billion — attributable to a small number of neonatal encephalopathy (brain injury) claims. This illustrates a core tension: ACC's no-fault model resolves birth injury liability for practitioners but does not make it disappear — it shifts it to the state, and the per-claim costs for catastrophic birth brain injury are very similar to Australian tort settlement values.

For surgery-related claims specifically, a 2024 ACC OIA response reports that between 2019 and 2024, there were **53,070 decided surgery-related treatment injury claims, of which 42,251 (79.6%) were accepted and 10,819 (20.4%) were declined.**

### 3. Maternal Birth Injuries: The 2022 Legislative Extension

Before 1 October 2022, a mother injured during childbirth could only access ACC through the treatment injury pathway — meaning she needed to establish that a health professional's treatment caused her injury. Most obstetric birth injuries, including perineal tears, prolapses, and pelvic floor injuries, do not clearly flow from a specific intervention; they are often considered inherent risks of vaginal birth. As a result, many mothers were excluded from ACC coverage even when they suffered significant, disabling birth injuries.

The **Accident Compensation (Maternal Birth Injury and Other Matters) Amendment Act 2022** (effective 1 October 2022) added a **new category of automatically covered maternal birth injuries** — twelve injury types that are now covered simply because they occurred during birth, without the need to establish treatment causation. Covered injuries include:

- Perineal tears (including obstetric anal sphincter injuries — OASI)
- Tears or injuries to the labia, vagina, vulva, clitoris, cervix, rectum, anus, or urethra
- Levator avulsion (tearing of the levator ani muscle from the pelvic wall — a serious and common pelvic floor injury)
- Pubic ramus fractures
- Pudendal neuropathy
- Coccyx fractures
- Ruptured uterus during labour
- Prolapses (including prolapse of the uterus, bladder, bowel, or rectum)

Officials estimated the change would benefit approximately **17,000–28,000 birthing parents per year**, reflecting the reality that maternal birth injuries affect up to 80% of people who give birth vaginally. ACC's OIA data from October 2025 shows claims have been lodged from 1 October 2022 onward, with the numbers growing each calendar year as awareness of the coverage increases.

What the 2022 extension does **not** cover is the baby. Coverage for babies injured during birth requires establishment of the treatment injury pathway — that a registered health professional's treatment caused the infant's injury.

### 4. The Baby Coverage Gap: Neonatal Brain Injury and the Causation Barrier

The most contentious and consequential coverage gap in New Zealand's ACC birth injury framework is brain injury to babies. This is the category — hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), cerebral palsy, and related conditions — that drives Australia's birth injury liability crisis. How does ACC handle it?

**In theory:** A baby who suffers brain injury during birth can make a treatment injury claim if a registered health professional's intervention (or failure to intervene) caused the injury. The "ordinary consequence of treatment" exclusion requires that the injury goes beyond an expected risk of the treatment provided.

**In practice:** The causation test is difficult to satisfy for birth brain injury, for reasons that closely mirror the scientific uncertainty acknowledged in Australian litigation.

- A **2021 New Zealand Accident Compensation Appeal Court decision** (2021 NZACC 101) reviewed a declined birth brain injury claim. The court noted paediatric expert evidence that birth asphyxia accounts for **less than 10% of cerebral palsy cases**, and that a diagnosis of birth-asphyxia-caused cerebral palsy requires clear evidence including a moderate-to-severe neonatal encephalopathy.
- A **2023 Spinoff investigation** reported that approximately **360 babies per year suffer some form of birth brain injury** in New Zealand, while only around **100 ACC claims are even lodged** for birth brain injuries, and approximately **20 babies per year are accepted for ACC coverage** for birth-related brain injury.
- ACC itself disputed some of those figures and said it covers approximately **20% of babies injured at birth** — but acknowledged that the denominator includes only cases where an application is actually made, not the full universe of injured babies.

The result is a population of children with significant birth-related disabilities who — if their injury is not attributable to a treatment act — are **excluded from ACC compensation**. They may access NDIS-equivalent supports through New Zealand's disability support systems, but they do not receive the lifetime economic loss compensation, earnings replacement, or care-cost reimbursement that ACC-covered claimants receive. In Australia, the equivalent group has two pathways: prove negligence for very large tort damages, or access NDIS disability support. New Zealand's ACC provides a third pathway — no-fault compensation via treatment injury — but that pathway has a causation gatekeeping requirement that excludes precisely the ambiguous, uncertain cases that are most numerous.

The NZD $3.9 billion within the $5.7 billion treatment injury liability estimate attributable to neonatal encephalopathy claims reflects the subset that *did* get through the ACC gate — typically the clearest cases where clinical decision delays were documentable and causation was arguable. For the remainder, the New Zealand public system bears the disability support cost but not the compensation cost, much like Australia's NDIS.

### 5. ACC's Financial Sustainability Problem: Shifting Liability, Not Eliminating It

New Zealand's ACC scheme is not a costless solution to birth injury liability. The no-fault design eliminates the litigation system and its costs (legal fees, adversarial expert reports, court time, the luck-of-the-draw outcomes for plaintiffs), but the underlying care costs and lost earnings do not disappear. They are borne by the state.

ACC's financial position as at 30 June 2025:
- **Outstanding Claims Liability (OCL):** NZD $63.6 billion
- **Investment fund:** approximately NZD $51.1 billion
- **Underfunding gap:** approximately NZD $12.5 billion
- **Projected funding shortfall by 2030** (if trends continue): **NZD $26.3 billion**

In 2024/25, ACC recorded a **net deficit of NZD $1.5 billion** — an improvement from a NZD $7.2 billion deficit in 2023/24, but structurally concerning. Annual spending on rehabilitation, treatment, and compensation reached **NZD $8.1 billion** in 2024/25, up from $3.2 billion a decade earlier.

ACC attributes the growth in its outstanding claims liability to three drivers:
1. **Slower rehabilitation** — injured people are taking longer to return to work and independence
2. **Rising care costs** — the cost of services that ACC purchases has increased
3. **Judicial expansion** — court decisions have widened the scheme's boundaries beyond its original legislative intent

The third driver is particularly relevant to the treatment injury category. When courts take a broad view of what constitutes a treatment injury — attributing more birth brain injuries to health professional acts, or accepting more tenuous causation chains — ACC's treatment injury liability increases. There is structural pressure within the scheme toward broad coverage (advocates, families, and sometimes courts push for coverage in ambiguous cases), but the fiscal consequence is that ACC bears large long-tail claims similar to those that are driving the Australian liability crisis.

### 6. The Accountability Trade-Off: No Litigation, No Negligence Signal

New Zealand's no-fault system achieves its premium reduction by removing the negligence finding from the compensation process. This has a fiscal benefit and a practitioner welfare benefit, but it trades away the accountability mechanism that litigation (however imperfectly) provides.

In Australia, a $9.5 million birth injury settlement comes with a finding (at least implicit in settlement) that care was below standard. The settlement establishes a public record. The practitioner faces AHPRA notification obligations. The hospital may face coronial or ministerial scrutiny. The outcome is visible.

In New Zealand, an ACC treatment injury claim establishes that a treatment caused an injury — but not that the treatment was negligent. ACC does not make negligence findings. Notification to the Medical Council of New Zealand or the Health and Disability Commissioner (HDC) occurs through separate quality and safety reporting mechanisms, not through the ACC process. A 2006 Commonwealth Fund study by Marie Bismark found that **fewer than 5% of eligible patients even filed a treatment injury claim** — suggesting that the ACC system, while protecting practitioners from litigation, may also suppress the feedback loop that informs quality improvement.

New Zealand's Health Quality and Safety Commission (HQSC) and the HDC serve some of the quality accountability functions that litigation serves in Australia. But neither produces the volume of case-specific public findings that Australian litigation generates.

### 7. The Levy Structure: How New Zealand Pays for ACC

Australia's medical indemnity system is funded by practitioner premiums plus government subsidies (PSS, HCCS, ROCS). New Zealand's ACC treatment injury category is funded by levies imposed on a different set of parties:

The **Treatment Injury Account** within ACC is funded primarily by the **Non-Earners' Account levy** paid by the Crown on behalf of non-earners, and by the **Earners' Account** levy paid by employed earners. Health professionals do not pay a specific levy for treatment injury — unlike Australian practitioners who pay individually priced indemnity premiums. The diffusion of cost across all earners and taxpayers is what makes the NZD <$1,000 practitioner premium possible: the liability exists, but it is collectively borne rather than individually priced.

This structure means that New Zealand society as a whole cross-subsidises medical treatment injury, including obstetric injury. The levy-based funding model is inherently resistant to risk signals — because practitioners do not face premium increases for adverse outcomes, the financial incentive for quality improvement that insurance pricing theoretically provides is absent.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Can Australia afford the ACC model?** New Zealand's treatment injury scheme costs roughly NZD $411.5 million/year for treatment injuries alone. Scaling to Australia's population (5x New Zealand's) suggests a minimum cost of approximately AUD $2.75–$3.5 billion per year for a comparable scheme — not including birth injury liability already held by the government via HCCS and ROCS. The Productivity Commission's 2011 NIIS proposal did not attempt a comprehensive Australian ACC equivalent; it targeted only catastrophic injuries, recognising the fiscal boundary.

2. **The baby gap is unresolved in both countries.** ACC covers mothers (automatically since 2022) and covers babies via treatment injury if causation can be established — but the ambiguous majority of birth brain injury cases fall through in both jurisdictions. The difference is that in Australia they may receive large tort damages if negligence is established; in New Zealand they get neither compensation nor a negligence finding.

3. **Is the ACC scheme making practitioners less accountable?** With no litigation risk, New Zealand practitioners may face less pressure to adopt defensive-but-standard practices (like the thorough fetal monitoring protocols that drive much Australian birth injury litigation). Or they may be liberated to practice more rationally, without litigation-driven over-intervention. The evidence is mixed.

4. **ACC's financial trajectory is not sustainable on current settings.** A $26.3 billion projected funding shortfall by 2030 means New Zealand will face either levy increases, benefit reductions, or structural reform. Scope restrictions on treatment injury — including birth injury — are a potential cost-control lever. This would bring New Zealand's system closer to Australia's, not further from it.

5. **The tort bar and exemplary damages:** New Zealand's ACC bars personal injury suits. A family with a birth-injured child cannot sue the obstetrician for a $10 million settlement even if the injury was clearly caused by negligent care. They receive ACC compensation at standard rates — rehabilitation, care support, earnings replacement — but not the uncapped future care packages that Australian birth injury litigation produces. For the most severely affected families, Australian tort outcomes may produce substantially more resources than ACC coverage provides.
