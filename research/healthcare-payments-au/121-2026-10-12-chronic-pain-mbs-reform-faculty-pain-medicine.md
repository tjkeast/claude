# Chronic Pain and the MBS: The $144 Billion Problem Australia Is Medicating Rather Than Managing

**One-line summary:** Australia spends an estimated $144 billion per year on chronic pain's economic burden, yet the Medicare Benefits Schedule lacks dedicated items for multidisciplinary pain care — creating a system where opioid prescribing is easy, funded, and monitored, while the non-pharmacological alternatives that would reduce that prescribing are largely unaffordable, inaccessible, and outside the MBS.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 120 documented that Australia's Real-Time Prescription Monitoring (RTPM) system has demonstrably reduced doctor-shopping for opioids while failing to reduce opioid-induced deaths — because monitoring a problem and providing an alternative pathway to treat it are not the same thing. Today's report examines the structural funding failure that sits behind that gap: the MBS's inability to fund multidisciplinary chronic pain care, despite a detailed reform blueprint that has sat largely unimplemented since 2019.

---

## Background

Chronic pain is Australia's largest hidden health crisis. At least one in five Australians over 45 live with persistent pain. Painaustralia, drawing on a 2020 Deloitte Access Economics analysis, estimates the total economic cost — direct healthcare costs plus productivity losses and reduced quality of life — at $144.1 billion per year, rising to a projected $215 billion by 2050.

These are extraordinary numbers for a condition that barely registers in Australia's healthcare payment architecture. There is no dedicated Medicare rebate for a chronic pain management plan. There is no MBS item that funds the allied health team — physiotherapists, psychologists, occupational therapists — who work alongside pain physicians in multidisciplinary clinics. And pain medicine specialists face inequities in which consultation items they can access compared to other specialist groups.

The result is a system that funds opioid prescriptions readily — through the PBS, at low cost, indefinitely — and monitors those prescriptions through an increasingly sophisticated national surveillance infrastructure, while systematically underfunding the clinical alternatives that could reduce the need for those prescriptions in the first place.

## Key Findings

### 1. The MBS Review Knew the Problem — in 2019

The MBS Review Taskforce's Pain Management Clinical Committee, which reported in 2019, identified the funding gap clearly. Its 32 recommendations included a specific group (Recommendations 26–30) targeting the infrastructure of non-pharmacological chronic pain care:

- **Recommendation 26:** Create a new MBS item for a chronic pain management plan — analogous to the GP Management Plan (GPMP) and Team Care Arrangement (TCA) framework for other chronic conditions.
- **Recommendation 27:** Enable multidisciplinary case conferencing for complex chronic pain, allowing GPs to coordinate allied health input.
- **Recommendations 28–30:** Create new items for multidisciplinary team review, group-based pain programs, and telehealth-delivered pain consultations.

The committee was explicit about the rationale: pain management outcomes are demonstrably better when delivered through multidisciplinary teams combining pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches. The current MBS item structure — built around individual specialist consultations and PBS-subsidised opioids — does not support this model.

As of June 2025, the Faculty of Pain Medicine (FPM) at the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists (ANZCA) wrote to Health Minister Mark Butler confirming that these core multidisciplinary reforms remained unimplemented. Some procedural and safety-related item revisions had been adopted, but the structural changes enabling coordinated, non-pharmacological pain services had not been made.

### 2. The $40 Million Cut With No Corresponding Benefit

The path from the 2019 MBS Review to the present is not a story of benign delay — it is a story of savings captured without reform delivered.

ANZCA documented that the Australian Government made approximately $40 million in cuts to chronic pain funding following the MBS Review process, by rationalising or removing items the committee identified as low-value. The offsetting investment — the new items that would have redirected that funding to multidisciplinary care — was not made.

The result was a net reduction in MBS-funded pain services. Community-based multidisciplinary pain services, which might have been viable with the new item numbers, did not become viable without them.

### 3. Items 132 and 133: A Persistent Inequity for Pain Physicians

A specific and longstanding grievance for the FPM concerns MBS items 132 and 133 — consultation items covering complex initial assessments of up to 60 minutes. These items are accessible to specialists enrolled in the Royal Australasian College of Physicians (RACP) but are not accessible to FPM fellows who are not RACP members.

The Pain Management Clinical Committee included this as Recommendation 29: that FPM fellows should have access to items 132 and 133 as a recognition of the complexity of initial chronic pain assessments. Pain physicians regularly see patients with multi-system comorbidities, complex psychosocial histories, and extensive prior medication trails — assessments that routinely require more than 40 minutes and that are inadequately compensated through current specialist pain items 2801 and 2806.

In January 2026, the Department of Health responded to the FPM's review request regarding items 132 and 133, leaving the Faculty with ongoing concerns about the government's position. Sports and Exercise Medicine physicians gained access to Group A4 items including 132 and 133 during the same period — a comparison the FPM characterises as an inequity. As of mid-2026, the item access reform remains unresolved.

### 4. The Five Recommendations the Minister Received in June 2025

In a June 2025 letter from the FPM Dean to Minister Butler, the Faculty laid out five specific priorities for the government's Strengthening Medicare agenda:

1. **Implement MBS Review Recommendations 26–27** — chronic pain care planning and multidisciplinary case conferencing, enabling GPs to coordinate allied health input through the MBS in the same way they currently coordinate care for diabetes or cardiovascular disease.
2. **Formally recognise chronic pain as a Strengthening Medicare priority** — so that it receives investment in the same reform envelope as general practice, aged care integration, and mental health.
3. **Fund medically led public hospital pain services**, specifically in rural, regional and First Nations communities, where there are no pain physicians and where GP opioid prescribing is heaviest.
4. **Support a genuine multidisciplinary approach** — acknowledging that physiotherapy, psychology, occupational therapy and social work are not optional add-ons to pain care but central to its evidence base.
5. **Create a rural generalist training pathway** for GPs managing chronic pain in low-resource settings, recognising that the vast majority of chronic pain in Australia is managed in general practice, not specialist pain clinics.

As of this report's publication date, no comprehensive government response to these recommendations has been publicly announced.

### 5. The Public Pain Clinic System: Median 150-Day Waits, No Funding Reform

Australia's publicly funded multidisciplinary pain services operate under sustained capacity pressure. The most widely cited evidence — the Waiting in Pain surveys — found that the median wait from GP referral to initial assessment at a publicly funded adult pain service was 150 days, compared to 38.5 days in the private sector. Some services recorded wait times exceeding three years.

A 2020–21 update found that adult public services had expanded modestly and median waits had declined at some sites, but lengthy waits of two years or more persisted at services in high-demand metropolitan areas. The fundamental mismatch between demand and capacity was not resolved by the update period's service growth.

The structural problem is that multidisciplinary pain services are primarily funded through state hospital budgets, not the MBS. This creates several perverse incentives:

- **Pain specialists in public clinics** can bill the MBS for their individual consultations but cannot bill MBS items for the allied health team working alongside them — because no such items exist.
- **Community-based pain programs** that could reduce hospital dependency are financially unviable without MBS item support for coordinated allied health.
- **Private pain clinics** are accessible only to patients with private health insurance covering outpatient specialist services, or to patients who can pay out-of-pocket for allied health input. This creates a two-tier system: well-resourced patients can access timely multidisciplinary care; others wait.

The AIHW's 2020 chronic pain report found that people with chronic pain were significantly more likely to be hospitalised, to have longer hospital stays, and to report limitations to daily activities — all indicators of inadequate community-based management. The MBS's failure to fund community multidisciplinary care likely contributes directly to these hospitalisation patterns.

### 6. RTPM's Unintended Signal: The Opioid–Pain Access Trade-Off

Report 120 documented that RTPM has reduced doctor-shopping but not opioid-induced deaths. Emerging evidence suggests one reason: for patients with legitimate chronic pain needs, RTPM monitoring is generating prescriber anxiety without providing an alternative clinical pathway.

A 2025 MJA qualitative study of rural GPs managing chronic non-cancer pain found that prescribing behaviour was shaped less by clinical knowledge gaps than by structural constraints: short Medicare-funded consultation times, limited access to multidisciplinary pain services, and fear of regulatory scrutiny. Rural GPs reported feeling more pressure to justify *deprescribing* opioids than to justify prescribing them — in part because deprescribing without access to an alternative pathway (a pain clinic, a physiotherapist, a psychologist) creates a clinical problem the GP cannot solve within their available resources and funding.

This finding is consistent with the broader pharmacovigilance literature. Opioid monitoring systems that flag high-dose or multi-prescriber use do not, by themselves, create the clinical infrastructure to manage the patients they identify. Without MBS support for multidisciplinary alternatives, a monitoring system's primary effect may be to redistribute prescribing — from flagged patients to unflagged ones, from high-prescribing GPs to low-prescribing ones — rather than to improve patient outcomes.

### 7. The ePPOC Closure: Losing the Ability to Measure What Isn't Working

In June 2026, the Faculty of Pain Medicine, Australian Pain Society, New Zealand Pain Society, College of Pain Australia, Painaustralia, and the Australian Pain Society's Research Alliance issued a joint statement warning that the end of the electronic Persistent Pain Outcomes Collaboration (ePPOC) presented a critical risk to the sector.

ePPOC, operated by the Australasian Health Outcomes Consortium at the University of Wollongong and established in 2013 with NSW Ministry of Health funding, was Australia's only national platform for benchmarking patient-reported outcomes across multidisciplinary pain services. It enabled services to measure whether their interventions worked, compare their performance against peers, and identify practice improvement opportunities.

ePPOC is being decommissioned on 31 October 2026. As of the FPM's July 2026 board update, no decision had been made on a replacement platform. The closure means that at the same time as the sector is advocating for expanded MBS investment in pain services, it is losing the national infrastructure to demonstrate whether those services deliver value — a direct blow to the evidence case for reform.

The joint statement characterised the ePPOC closure as threatening "a nationally and internationally significant capability to measure, understand and improve pain care." It asked for government intervention to ensure continuity of benchmarking, common data standards, and a sustainable national outcomes framework.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The "opioid-first" architecture won't self-correct.**
The current MBS makes it straightforward to prescribe an opioid and monitor that prescription through RTPM. It makes it very difficult to fund the non-pharmacological alternatives. This asymmetry is not ideologically deliberate — it is a legacy of how the MBS developed, item by item, without a chronic pain policy framework. But it produces opioid-first care at scale, and monitoring without alternatives cannot reverse it.

**2. Strengthening Medicare has a chronic pain blind spot.**
The government's Strengthening Medicare investment since 2022 — bulk billing incentives, longer consultation items, MyMedicare enrolment — has been focused primarily on general practice access. Chronic pain has not featured as an explicit priority despite affecting 3.4 million Australians. The FPM's June 2025 call for formal recognition within the Strengthening Medicare agenda reflects a concern that the program will conclude without touching the MBS pain architecture.

**3. The care planning analogy is powerful but contested.**
The FPM's Recommendation 26 asks for a chronic pain management plan analogous to the GPMP/TCA framework. But the GPMP/TCA framework itself has been subject to criticism — of paperwork burden, low actual coordination impact, and GP co-claiming concerns (separately addressed in a 2025 policy change). The pain sector's proposal may need to be designed differently to avoid replicating those problems, while still enabling the allied health access that multidisciplinary care requires.

**4. The equity dimension is stark.**
Rural and regional Australians with chronic pain are least likely to have access to a pain specialist, most likely to be managed in general practice alone, most likely to be in occupations (mining, agriculture, construction, transport) with high injury and opioid exposure risk, and most likely to wait years if referred to a public pain service. The National Strategic Action Plan for Pain Management (2021) identified rural and regional access as a priority area. The $4.3 million rural outreach investment in its initial implementation phase is orders of magnitude smaller than the structural problem it was asked to address.

**5. Without ePPOC, reform advocacy loses its evidence base.**
The closure of ePPOC removes the sector's primary data infrastructure for demonstrating outcomes. Any future bid for MBS investment in multidisciplinary pain care will need to be supported by evidence of effectiveness. If the benchmarking capability is lost during the years that the advocacy campaign is most active, the political case for reform becomes harder to make at the exact moment it needs to be made.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Chronic pain and the opioid prescribing review item**: whether a structured MBS item requiring a GP to review opioid prescribing (with RTPM checking, deprescribing planning, and allied health referral) could serve as a transitional bridge between the current monitoring architecture and the multidisciplinary care model the sector is seeking
2. **Endometriosis and pelvic pain clinics — a successful multidisciplinary model**: the federal government's 2024-25 investment in 20 new endometriosis and pelvic pain clinics represents one example of multidisciplinary pain service funding outside the MBS item architecture — examining how that funding model works and whether it can generalise
3. **Chronic non-cancer pain and the PBS — opioid spending volumes**: what the PBS actually spends on opioids for non-cancer pain patients; which items, what volumes, what trend, and what the implied public subsidy of the opioid-first architecture amounts to annually
4. **Pain medicine training pipeline**: the FPM's recommendation for a rural generalist GP pathway intersects with the broader specialist training pipeline problem (covered in Report 28); how many pain medicine specialists does Australia have, where are they, and what is the attrition rate?
5. **Long COVID and chronic pain — emerging MBS pressure**: long COVID has introduced a new population of patients with complex chronic pain, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms that sit poorly within existing MBS categories; how the payment system is adapting (or failing to)
