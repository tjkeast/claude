# Von Willebrand Disease in Australian Women: The Diagnostic Delay, the Haematology–Gynaecology Funding Gap, and the Case for Joint Care

**One-line summary:** Von Willebrand disease — the most common inherited bleeding disorder in Australia — is systematically undiagnosed in women and girls, with diagnostic delays averaging more than 14 years, because heavy menstrual bleeding is normalised or attributed to gynaecological causes rather than a coagulation defect, leaving thousands of women without the coordinated haematology–gynaecology care that could prevent unnecessary surgery and iron deficiency.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 124 on heavy menstrual bleeding identified that roughly one in three young women with HMB may have an underlying bleeding disorder — most commonly von Willebrand disease — and that diagnosis can take up to 16 years. Today's report examines how that gap is structured: who VWD affects, how Australia's clinical and payment systems are (and are not) organised to catch it, and what the combined haematology–gynaecology funding architecture currently provides.

---

## Background

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder in the world. Unlike haemophilia A and B, which predominantly affect males due to X-linked inheritance, VWD affects males and females equally in terms of genetics — but women and girls bear a substantially greater symptomatic burden because heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) is the disorder's most common and disabling manifestation in this population. This asymmetry means that VWD has historically been framed as a "men's disease" in clinical culture, research investment, and healthcare system design, even though women are the majority of those who suffer its consequences.

In Australia, VWD sits at the intersection of three funding and care domains: haematology (the specialty responsible for bleeding disorders), gynaecology (the specialty most women first present to), and primary care (where most women first report their symptoms, if they report them at all). The payment system — the Medicare Benefits Schedule, the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, and the National Blood Authority's supply framework for clotting factor products — is designed primarily around each of these domains in isolation. Joint haematology–gynaecology care, which international evidence identifies as the most effective model for women with bleeding disorders, is not systematically structured or funded in Australia.

## Key Findings

### 1. Scale: VWD Is Australia's Most Common Inherited Bleeding Disorder — and Is Vastly Under-Diagnosed

The global estimated prevalence of VWD is approximately 1% of the population — roughly one in 100 people carry a VWD variant. Clinically significant VWD (symptomatic disease requiring treatment or management) affects approximately 1 in 1,000 people, while severe VWD (Type 3) is rare, affecting approximately 1 in 250,000.

The Australian Bleeding Disorders Registry (ABDR), maintained by the National Blood Authority, had **8,487 active patients** as at 30 June 2025 (up from 8,034 at 30 June 2024). Hereditary VWD is one of the two most prevalent conditions in the ABDR, alongside hereditary haemophilia A — and based on historical breakdowns (VWD constituted approximately 35% of ABDR patients in 2011–12), there are likely around **2,800–3,000 VWD patients** currently on the registry.

Even taking the more conservative population prevalence of clinically significant VWD at 1 in 1,000, Australia's 27 million population implies approximately **27,000 people** with clinically meaningful VWD. The gap between ~3,000 on the ABDR and ~27,000 estimated to have the condition is a measure of profound under-diagnosis — particularly among women, who are less likely to be referred to a haematology treatment centre.

### 2. Three Types, Asymmetric Complexity

VWD is divided into three broad categories with significantly different clinical presentations, diagnostic pathways, and treatment requirements:

- **Type 1 VWD** (partial quantitative deficiency of von Willebrand factor): the most common form, affecting approximately 70–75% of VWD patients. Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is structurally normal but present at reduced levels. Many patients have mild symptoms; presentation in women is typically HMB. Desmopressin (DDAVP) is often effective in Type 1.

- **Type 2 VWD** (qualitative defects in VWF structure or function): approximately 20–25% of VWD patients. Four subtypes (2A, 2B, 2M, 2N) with different clinical profiles. Type 2B is associated with thrombocytopenia and worsens with desmopressin — an important contraindication that requires correct typing before treatment.

- **Type 3 VWD** (virtual absence of VWF): approximately 1–5% of VWD patients. The most severe form. Desmopressin is ineffective; VWF/FVIII concentrate replacement is required. Many Type 3 patients present in childhood with spontaneous bleeding.

Correct typing is clinically important: prescribing desmopressin to a Type 2B patient can be harmful. Yet the diagnostic pathway — involving specialist VWF assays, VWF activity measurement, VWF collagen binding, and sometimes genetic testing — is not routinely available in general practice and requires specialist haematology referral and laboratory testing.

### 3. The Diagnostic Delay Problem

Evidence from multiple international cohorts consistently shows that women with VWD wait far longer for diagnosis than men. Studies published in *Haematologica* (2026) and other recent journals report that **median diagnostic delay in women can exceed 14 years**, often with multiple episodes of heavy menstrual bleeding, iron deficiency, and in some cases surgery (including hysterectomy) occurring before the coagulation defect is identified.

The mechanisms driving this delay are well characterised:

- **Normalisation:** Heavy periods are frequently accepted as normal within families, by women themselves, and by healthcare professionals. Women with a VWD family history may see their bleeding as characteristic of "how the women in our family are," rather than as a disorder.

- **Attribution to gynaecological causes:** When HMB is severe enough to prompt medical help, it is typically managed within a gynaecological paradigm — fibroids, endometriosis, hormonal imbalance — without a coagulation workup. Tranexamic acid and hormonal management suppress bleeding sufficiently to remove urgency without resolving the underlying disorder.

- **GP knowledge gaps:** Studies and advocacy organisations consistently note that general practitioners in non-specialist settings may have limited training in bleeding disorders and may not apply bleeding assessment tools systematically to women presenting with HMB. Professor Sonia Grover and Dr Briony Cutts (Australian paediatric and gynaecological specialists) have specifically called out under-diagnosis in girls and young women as a significant problem, and the value of Bleeding Assessment Tools (BATs) in structured history-taking.

The Haemophilia Foundation Australia (HFA) marked **World Haemophilia Day 2025** (17 April) under the global theme "Access for all: Women and girls bleed too" — explicitly acknowledging that women and girls with bleeding disorders remain systematically underdiagnosed and underserved, in Australia as internationally.

In March–April 2026, HFA ran the **Women Bleed Too survey** — the first Australian survey specifically examining how women and girls with bleeding disorders experience healthcare settings, which tools help them communicate their symptoms, and what gaps they face. The survey had 100 participants and its findings were published in the June 2026 issue of *National Haemophilia* and presented at the WFH 2026 World Congress. Full results were not publicly available at the time of writing, but the survey signals a shift toward treating women's diagnostic experience as an explicit policy and advocacy priority.

### 4. The Treatment Architecture: Haemostatic Agents, Desmopressin, and Factor Concentrates

Women with VWD have access to a tiered set of treatments, delivered through different payment mechanisms:

**Non-specific haemostatic agents (PBS-listed):**
- **Tranexamic acid** (antifibrinolytic, available as tablets and IV): PBS-listed and used across a range of HMB causes. For VWD patients with HMB, tranexamic acid during menstruation can reduce blood loss significantly. This is accessible via GP prescription at standard PBS co-payment (~$25 general, $7.70 concession).
- **Combined oral contraceptives** and **progestogen-only pills**: PBS-listed; suppress endometrial proliferation and reduce HMB in VWD. Widely accessible but not suitable for all patients (particularly those with Type 2B where oestrogen may affect platelet count dynamics).
- **LNG-IUS (Mirena)**: PBS-listed under restricted benefit for idiopathic menorrhagia (as discussed in Report 124). Effective in reducing HMB in VWD patients; the PBS restriction requiring prior failure of oral therapies applies here too.

**Desmopressin (DDAVP / Minirin):**
Desmopressin stimulates release of stored VWF from endothelial cells and can temporarily raise VWF levels two- to fivefold. It is effective in Type 1 VWD and some Type 2 variants (with testing to confirm responsiveness required before clinical use). Desmopressin nasal spray (Minirin) and subcutaneous injection are TGA-registered in Australia. Access typically requires a haematologist or haemophilia treatment centre (HTC) involvement for the initial responsiveness trial. The product is PBS-listed for certain indications including perioperative haemostasis and diabetes insipidus; PBS access specifically for VWD/HMB management is primarily coordinated through specialist prescribers.

A responsiveness trial — showing VWF activity rises at least twofold above baseline — is required before desmopressin is used clinically. This trial must be done at a haematology facility with laboratory capacity to measure VWF levels: it cannot be performed in primary care.

**VWF/FVIII concentrates (National Blood Authority supply):**
For Type 2B and Type 3 VWD, and for Type 1/2A patients undergoing surgery or experiencing major bleeds, VWF/FVIII concentrates are required. In Australia, clotting factor products are **not** supplied through the PBS. They are procured centrally by the **National Blood Authority (NBA)** through national supply contracts and distributed through Haemophilia Treatment Centres (HTCs) to patients.

Under new national contracts effective **1 July 2025**, the NBA established arrangements with CSL Behring, Takeda, Pfizer, Sanofi-Aventis and Roche covering recombinant Factor VIII, Factor IX, and emicizumab products. VWF/FVIII concentrate products — including **Wilate** (Octapharma, TGA-registered in Australia) and plasma-derived VWF/FVIII concentrates — are supplied through this centralised NBA framework. Patients access these products through HTCs at no direct out-of-pocket cost, but access requires registration with an HTC and specialist oversight.

**The access gap:** Women with milder VWD who have never been referred to an HTC — and who manage their HMB through GP-prescribed tranexamic acid and hormonal agents — may never access the concentrated products or desmopressin that could more effectively control their bleeding, simply because they have not been diagnosed, or because their GP has not referred them. The NBA/HTC model is effective for patients who are in the system; the problem is the structural underrepresentation of women in that system.

### 5. The Haematology–Gynaecology Funding Interface

The optimal management of VWD in women requires coordination between two specialties that have no structured joint MBS billing pathway:

- A **gynaecologist** assesses HMB, diagnoses structural causes (fibroids, adenomyosis, polyps), manages surgical options (ablation, hysterectomy, UAE), and prescribes hormonal treatments.
- A **haematologist** diagnoses the coagulation defect, performs a desmopressin trial, manages prophylaxis and treatment around major bleeds and surgery, and coordinates VWF concentrate supply through the NBA/HTC network.

In **practice**, women with VWD need both specialists. Yet the MBS does not fund a joint consultation, and neither specialist can bill for coordinating care with the other. A patient seeing both a gynaecologist and a haematologist privately faces **two gap payments** (both specialties frequently do not bulk-bill) plus GP referral costs.

International evidence consistently recommends **joint haematology–gynaecology clinics** as best practice. A 2025 UK survey of haemophilia treatment centres found that only **8 of 29 comprehensive care centres** offered joint haematology–gynaecology clinics, and fewer than half had a dedicated clinical lead for women and girls. In Australia, no national data on the number of HTCs offering joint or coordinated women's clinics is publicly available, though advocacy and nursing literature signals that the concept is emerging rather than established.

An October 2026 article in *National Haemophilia* reported that a World Federation of Haemophilia congress session on nurse-led women and girls' clinics stressed the historical underdiagnosis and under-treatment of women, with one Australian HTC nurse planning to propose a nurse-led multidisciplinary model for girls and young women. This is a bottom-up advocacy signal, not a funded system change.

HFA established a **Women and Girls Advisory Group** in 2024, which at its February 2025 meeting identified three key goals centred on underdiagnosis, underrepresentation in the ABDR, and the need for treatment approaches tailored to hormonal changes across the female lifespan (menarche, pregnancy, menopause).

### 6. Pregnancy and the Peripartum Risk

Women with VWD face significant peripartum haemorrhage risk, particularly postpartum haemorrhage (PPH). During pregnancy, VWF levels typically rise in the third trimester (especially in Type 1 VWD, where they may normalise temporarily), but fall sharply after delivery — creating a PPH window. Women with Type 2 and Type 3 VWD do not have this protective rise.

Nationally, PPH rates in Australia have been rising (the AIHW reported in 2024 that PPH affected approximately 4% of births). For women with undiagnosed VWD, the PPH risk is substantially elevated. Routine antenatal haematology review for women with known or suspected VWD is part of best-practice care, but requires that the diagnosis exists before the pregnancy — which, given the 14+ year diagnostic delay, frequently means VWD is first diagnosed during or after a complicated delivery rather than proactively.

The peripartum context is one of the most consequential consequences of the diagnostic gap: a woman who might have been diagnosed in adolescence on the basis of HMB enters pregnancy without optimal prophylaxis or peripartum planning.

### 7. First Nations Women and Equity

No Australian data specifically tracking VWD prevalence or diagnostic delay among First Nations women was identified. However, the broader patterns documented in Reports 012, 013, and 124 apply here: First Nations women have higher rates of HMB complications including hysterectomy, lower access to specialist services, greater distance from HTCs (which are concentrated in major teaching hospitals in capital cities), and face compounding disadvantages in navigating the haematology–gynaecology interface.

The ABDR's underrepresentation of women generally is likely to be compounded for First Nations women, meaning the registry — the primary national data source on VWD — does not adequately capture this population's burden.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **PBS restriction vs optimal haemostatic management:** The LNG-IUS PBS restriction requires prior failure of oral therapies. Yet for a woman with confirmed VWD, a haematologist's first-line recommendation may be the LNG-IUS — not because oral therapies have failed, but because reducing endometrial thickness is the most effective long-term haemostatic strategy. The PBS framework was not designed with VWD-specific management in mind.

2. **ABDR as a registry of the system, not of the disease:** The ABDR captures patients who are known to and managed by HTCs. Women with mild VWD who have never been referred to a haematologist — perhaps the majority of women with clinically significant VWD — are invisible to the ABDR. National policy is shaped by registry data; systematic underrepresentation of women distorts the evidence base.

3. **Desmopressin responsiveness testing capacity:** The desmopressin trial (measuring VWF activity at baseline, one hour, and four hours post-dose) requires laboratory capacity not available in most GP or gynaecological settings. This creates a structural dependency on HTC access before one of the most effective treatments can be prescribed — a dependency that delays access for women in rural and regional areas.

4. **Bleeding Assessment Tools and MBS time:** Structured bleeding assessment using a validated BAT (such as the ISTH-BAT or the Canadian Hemophilia Society's Self-BAT) is endorsed as best practice in primary care for identifying women with possible underlying coagulation disorders. But completing a BAT properly takes consultation time beyond the standard MBS item 23 consultation. The absence of a dedicated HMB or bleeding assessment MBS item (noted in Report 124's discussion of MBS review gaps) means that thorough bleeding history-taking is financially disincentivised at the primary care level where most women first present.

5. **Haemophilia carrier designation and symptomatic status:** Women who carry haemophilia A or B gene variants (estimated 20–30% of female carriers have reduced factor levels causing symptoms) are sometimes referred to as "obligate carriers" rather than patients — a framing that historically denied them the same access to HTC services as symptomatic males. The framework has been shifting, with WFH guidelines now recommending all carriers be registered with an HTC regardless of factor levels, but clinical culture change lags behind guidance.
