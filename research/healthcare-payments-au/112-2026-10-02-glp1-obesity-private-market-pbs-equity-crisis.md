# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists for Obesity in Australia: A Half-Million-Patient Private Market, a Stalled PBS Listing, and a Growing Equity Crisis

**One-line summary:** Over one million Australians were dispensed a GLP-1 medicine in a single month in 2026, almost half of them paying $200–$700 per month out of pocket — and Australia's obesity-indication PBS listing remains pending, after Novo Nordisk's Wegovy recommendation awaits price negotiations and Eli Lilly walked away from the PBS entirely for tirzepatide, calling the government's offer the world's lowest price.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 111 on the Special Access Scheme documented the pre-PBS access gap, noting that Wegovy is available at $460/month via private prescription while awaiting PBS listing, and that Mounjaro costs $395/month. The GLP-1 obesity market is now the single largest driver of Australia's expanding non-PBS pharmaceutical sector — and the pricing standoff between the government and manufacturers is one of the most consequential healthcare financing disputes in Australia today.

---

## Background

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) were developed primarily as treatments for type 2 diabetes. Their ability to produce sustained, clinically significant weight loss was established in large-scale trials through the early 2020s: the SELECT trial showed semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 20% in people with obesity and established cardiovascular disease; the SURMOUNT and SURPASS-CVOT trials demonstrated tirzepatide's superiority. These results shifted GLP-1 RAs from diabetes medicines that happened to cause weight loss, to cardiovascular and metabolic medicines with transformative population-level potential.

The problem for Australian patients is structural: PBS listing requires a successful PBAC assessment, price negotiations, and a government decision. Until listing occurs, medicines are available only on private prescription at internationally benchmarked private prices — prices calibrated to markets far wealthier than the typical Australian household. The result is a rapidly growing two-tier market in which effective obesity pharmacotherapy is a subscription product for people who can afford it.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of Australia's Obesity Problem

Obesity is now Australia's leading preventable health risk, overtaking tobacco in 2024. Key statistics:

- **67% of Australian adults** (approximately 13 million people) live with overweight or obesity, based on 2022–24 data
- **1 in 4 children** (approximately 1.4 million, aged 2–17) are classified overweight or obese
- Between 1995 and 2022–24, abdominal obesity in adults nearly doubled
- Overweight and obesity account for **8.3% of all preventable ill health** in Australia
- Annual healthcare spending on obesity-related conditions was approximately **$10 billion** in 2023–24
- Projected costs to the Australian community reach **$87.7 billion by 2032**

These figures explain both the medical urgency of effective obesity pharmacotherapy and the fiscal terror it induces in government: medicines that work will attract a very large patient population.

### 2. The GLP-1 Market Has Already Arrived — Privately

The most striking feature of Australia's GLP-1 situation is that the market is not waiting for PBS listing. It has grown organically through private prescriptions.

By May 2026, more than **one million unique Australians** — approximately 5% of all adults — were dispensed at least one GLP-1 prescription in a single month, growing at a four-year compound annual growth rate of approximately 60%. Of those, roughly **500,000 are regular users**, and almost **half are paying $200–$700 per month** out of pocket for obesity indications.

IQVIA data shows the private share of total GLP-1 dispensing reached **43.8% by April 2025**, up from effectively zero in 2019. PBS-subsidised access (for type 2 diabetes) grew from 58,000 units in May 2020 to 280,000 units in April 2025 — but private dispensing grew from zero to match that level in the same period.

The current private market prices for obesity-indication use are:

| Medicine | Dose | Monthly cost (private) |
|----------|------|------------------------|
| Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg) | Maintenance dose | ~$460 |
| Ozempic (semaglutide) off-label | 1 mg | ~$150–$170 |
| Mounjaro (tirzepatide) | Initiation dose | ~$395 |

Approximately **400,000 Australians** are currently paying up to **$5,000 per year** for GLP-1 weight-loss medicines.

### 3. Wegovy (Semaglutide): PBAC Recommended, But PBS Listing Not Yet Done

At its November 2025 meeting, the PBAC recommended PBS listing of semaglutide (Wegovy 2.4 mg) for **adults with established cardiovascular disease (CVD) and obesity**. The eligibility criteria are narrow:

- **Established CVD** — defined as having experienced a cardiovascular event (heart attack, stroke) or having symptomatic peripheral arterial disease
- **BMI ≥35 kg/m²** (or ≥32.5 for people of Asian, Aboriginal, or Torres Strait Islander ethnicity)

The PBAC's recommendation was conditional on a price reduction and risk-sharing arrangement with Novo Nordisk — consistent with standard PBS practice for high-cost, high-volume medicines. The Ministry confirmed in January 2026 that the listing had been recommended and that price negotiations with Novo Nordisk would begin in 2026.

As of October 2026, **PBS listing has not yet taken effect**. The Minister for Health, Mark Butler, has publicly committed to listing Wegovy, framing it as both a transformative health intervention and a significant fiscal challenge:

> *"This is going to be a very big bill for taxpayers, so it's incumbent on us as the government to negotiate a good price."*

A PBS listing would cut the cost from approximately $460/month to **$25 per script for general patients** (approximately $50/month for two-weekly injections) or **$7.70 for pensioners and concession cardholders** — a reduction of more than 90%. The delay in finalising pricing means that every month of negotiation is a month in which 400,000 private-payers continue paying full price.

### 4. The Tirzepatide Standoff: Eli Lilly Walks Away

The Mounjaro (tirzepatide) story is more dramatic. After three previous rejections, the PBAC finally recommended tirzepatide for listing on the PBS at its **March 2026 meeting** — for type 2 diabetes, not obesity — after the sponsor submitted a significantly revised cost-effectiveness case.

However, in **April 2026**, Eli Lilly announced it would **not proceed with PBS listing**, rejecting the government's price offer. Lilly's public statement described the government's offered price as "unrealistic and unviable" and, specifically, "significantly lower than any other reimbursed price globally — including comparable healthcare systems like the UK and countries with lower GDP per capita, such as China."

The rejection denies subsidised access to tirzepatide for approximately **450,000 Australians with type 2 diabetes** who could benefit from it. It also effectively removes tirzepatide from the obesity PBS pathway — a future obesity-indication PBAC application is contingent on first establishing PBS listing for type 2 diabetes.

The standoff illustrates a deeper structural tension: Australia's PBS uses a cost-effectiveness framework and lowest-cost comparator approach that systematically generates low prices — which is by design, since the PBS's fiscal sustainability depends on strong price discipline. But for companies with globally dominant market positions (both Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly had market capitalizations in the hundreds of billions of dollars through this period), accepting the PBS price for a mass-market medicine creates a reference price problem: it risks being used by other payers worldwide to justify equivalent price reductions.

### 5. The Regulatory Interlude: Compounded GLP-1s Banned

In the period before Wegovy's Australian launch and during supply shortages of Ozempic (which was PBS-listed for diabetes but rationed due to demand), a compounding pharmacy market emerged. Pharmacies began producing unlicensed compounded versions of semaglutide and tirzepatide.

The TGA identified safety concerns — compounded products are not assessed for safety, quality, or efficacy — and took enforcement action, including a high-profile raid on a Melbourne compounding pharmacy. The federal government formally banned compounded GLP-1 RAs effective **1 October 2024**, affecting at least 20,000 known patients (with actual numbers likely higher).

The compounding market illustrated demand suppression in action: when people cannot afford brand-name products and no generic market exists (GLP-1s are still on-patent), some proportion will seek alternatives. The ban eliminated one safety valve without creating another — either PBS access (still pending) or price reduction.

### 6. What Happens After PBS Listing: A Massive Fiscal Event

Multiple analyses have examined the potential PBS cost of listing GLP-1s for obesity. A June 2024 Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) analysis estimated that listing GLP-1 RAs on the PBS for obesity could cost **billions of dollars per year** depending on eligibility criteria and prescribing volumes — which is why the PBAC has recommended narrow cardiovascular-risk-based criteria rather than broad obesity criteria.

The PBAC November 2025 advice on equitable access to GLP-1 obesity treatments addressed the Minister's referral explicitly. The PBAC recommended that PBS access be initially restricted to the cardiovascular disease indication — essentially the SELECT trial population — rather than a broader obesity indication. MJA analysis suggests that applying clinical trial-equivalent eligibility criteria could mean **4 in 10 Australian adults** meeting some threshold for GLP-1 treatment, which would represent a fiscally unmanageable patient population under any realistic pricing scenario.

The government's strategy is therefore to:
1. List Wegovy for a narrow high-risk cardiovascular population first
2. Use the resulting real-world data to inform any future expansion
3. Negotiate aggressively on price, with Wegovy's PBS listing price likely to include confidential rebates under a managed entry agreement

### 7. The Equity Paradox

The private GLP-1 market has created a striking equity inversion. Obesity's distribution in Australia is not random: it is concentrated in lower socioeconomic quintiles, in regional and remote areas, and disproportionately affects First Nations peoples. Yet the private obesity pharmacotherapy market is accessible primarily to those who can afford $460/month.

IQVIA data shows the private market is heaviest in metropolitan high-income areas, served by vertically integrated telehealth platforms (Eucalyptus and its competitors) that market directly to consumers. When pharmaceutical companies began running disease-awareness campaigns for obesity medicines, analysis published in the *MJA* in 2026 found that many campaigns featured women of colour, including First Nations women — raising questions about disease-awareness versus commercial intent in communities where systematic health inequity, not individual pathology, is the primary driver of obesity.

The PBS listing of Wegovy will help the cardiovascular-risk population — which includes a higher-risk cohort than the average private payer. But it will not extend to most of the 13 million Australians living with overweight or obesity, who will remain in a private market with $200–$700/month costs or no treatment at all.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**The pricing war sets precedents.** Eli Lilly's rejection of the PBS price offer for tirzepatide is being watched globally. If Australia's monopsony purchasing model generates prices that manufacturers reject, other companies may consider the same path, undermining the PBS's sustainability model. Medicines Australia has described the tirzepatide rejection as evidence of a "broken PBS system." But if the government capitulates on price to secure listing, it creates a different precedent — that rejection is a viable negotiating strategy.

**The equity arithmetic.** At $460/month, three years of Wegovy treatment costs approximately $16,560 — nearly a quarter of median annual income. For low-income households, welfare recipients, and most First Nations communities, private GLP-1 pharmacotherapy is simply inaccessible. Narrow PBS criteria mean that the NHS-level of access will reach only a fraction of those who could benefit medically.

**Once listed, the demand will be hard to contain.** The experience in the US (where Medicare Part D GLP-1 spending quintupled between 2021 and 2024) suggests that even narrow initial criteria expand rapidly under clinical and political pressure. Australia's PBS safety net — designed around medicines used by tens of thousands, not potentially millions — may face unprecedented fiscal pressure once GLP-1s are formally listed.

**The follow-on question of maintenance.** GLP-1 medicines require indefinite use; weight returns rapidly on cessation. Unlike a short-course antibiotic or even a one-time surgical procedure, PBS listing for GLP-1 obesity treatment is an ongoing cost commitment. The PBAC's awareness of this permanence partly explains the caution embedded in narrow initial eligibility criteria.

**Generic entry.** Semaglutide's primary patent expires in the late 2020s in some markets. A generic market would transform the cost structure — but the TGA registration of biosimilar GLP-1s is likely to be contested by originators, and Australia has no established pathway for "biosimilar GLP-1s" comparable to the existing biosimilar interchangeability framework.
