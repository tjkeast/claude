# The Medicare Levy and Health Hypothecation: Australia's $28 Billion Fiscal Fiction and the Case for Reform

**Report 054 — 2026-07-30**

**Summary:** The Medicare Levy raises approximately $25–28 billion a year from Australian workers, but almost none of it is genuinely earmarked for healthcare — it flows into consolidated revenue and covers only a fraction of what the government actually spends on health. This "fiscal fiction" has persisted for fifty years, punctuated by brief experiments with quasi-hypothecation (the 2014 DisabilityCare Australia Fund, the 2017 Medicare Guarantee Fund) that were accounting constructs rather than genuine dedications. Meanwhile, Victoria and Queensland have built genuine, ring-fenced mental health levies that are now raising over $1.4 billion a year combined. As the CPSP 2027 cliff approaches and the foundational supports architecture remains unfunded, the question of whether Australia could — or should — use a dedicated levy to finance the missing middle in mental health is more live than at any point since the NDIS debate.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 053 identified that $1 billion in adult psychosocial foundational supports funding sits contingent on state matching agreements that haven't been struck, with 25,000 CPSP clients facing a potential support cliff in July 2027. That funding gap exists against a backdrop of a levy mechanism that has historically been used precisely for this kind of structural health system financing challenge — when there is political will to dedicate it.

---

## Background

### The Whitlam Origins: A Levy Rejected

When Gough Whitlam introduced Medibank to Parliament in 1973, the financing design included a 1.35% income levy, explicitly hypothecated to fund universal health insurance. The Senate rejected it. Medibank launched on 1 July 1975 funded from consolidated revenue — and the principle that the levy's revenue would be genuinely linked to healthcare spending was lost at the very foundation.

The Medicare Levy in its current form was introduced by the Hawke Labor government in 1984, at an initial rate of 1%, as Medicare replaced Medibank. It has been increased several times since:

| Year | Rate | Context |
|------|------|---------|
| 1984 | 1.00% | Introduction with Medicare |
| 1986 | 1.25% | Increased to fund costs |
| 1993 | 1.40% | State hospital funding supplementation |
| 1995 | 1.50% | Various health cost increases |
| 2014 | 2.00% | 0.5% increase explicitly for NDIS (DisabilityCare Australia Fund) |

Through all these changes, the revenue has nominally entered consolidated revenue — spending on health is decided separately through the budget process, not mechanically linked to levy receipts.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Levy — and the Size of the Fiction

The Medicare Levy, at 2% of taxable income, raises approximately **$25–28 billion per year** in current revenue (the 2026-27 Budget projected a slight decrease in receipts due to the 2.9% uplift in low-income thresholds, which reduces the number of lower-income earners subject to the full levy). The Medicare Levy Surcharge — an additional 1–1.5% applied to higher-income earners who lack adequate private health insurance — raises a further $1.5–2 billion.

Combined, these mechanisms raise approximately **$26–30 billion annually**.

Australia's total government health expenditure in 2023–24 was **$188.2 billion** (69.6% of the $270.5 billion total), of which the Commonwealth Government's direct health spending was approximately **$106+ billion** when grants to states are included. The Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) alone cost around $27 billion; the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme approximately $19 billion.

The arithmetic is striking: the Medicare Levy covers, at most, **roughly 25–30%** of what the Commonwealth actually spends on health — and it is not even specifically directed at those costs. The levy funds general revenue; health is funded from general revenue; but the two flows have no direct legal or mechanical connection.

This is the "fiscal fiction" identified by the Centre for Independent Studies as early as 2005: the Medicare Levy creates the political impression that Medicare is funded by a modest 2% contribution, when in reality the scheme's total cost is several times the levy revenue. The levy is better understood as a partial health income tax — a label that influences public willingness to pay — than as an actual funding mechanism for any particular service.

### 2. The Medicare Guarantee Fund: Hypothecation in Name Only

In the 2017-18 Budget, the Turnbull government announced the creation of the **Medicare Guarantee Fund (MGF)**, designed to "guarantee" future funding for the MBS and PBS. The mechanism was explicit: the MGF would be credited with all Medicare Levy revenue (excluding the NDIS component) plus a top-up from personal income tax receipts sufficient to cover estimated MBS and PBS costs each year.

On paper, this looked like hypothecation. In practice, it was an accounting construct. The MGF operates as a special account within the consolidated revenue framework — money credited to it still comes from and returns to general government accounts. The "guarantee" is a political commitment, not a legal ring-fence. Parliament can change it at any time through the appropriations process.

The MGF has not been a significant point of policy debate since its establishment, precisely because it changed nothing structural about how health funding is decided. The amounts credited to it each year are set by the Budget process; the process itself determines health spending rather than the other way around.

### 3. The Exception That Proves the Rule: The DisabilityCare Australia Fund

The most significant use of the Medicare Levy as a genuine (if quasi-)hypothecated funding mechanism was the 2014 increase from 1.5% to 2%.

The 0.5% increase was explicitly legislated to fund the NDIS through the **DisabilityCare Australia Fund (DCAF)**. Established on 1 July 2014 under the DisabilityCare Australia Fund Act 2013, the DCAF is managed by the Future Fund Board of Guardians and can only be used to meet NDIS expenditure.

As at 31 December 2025, **$46,903 million ($46.9 billion) had been credited to the DCAF** since its inception. This represents the accumulated 0.5% levy component over eleven years.

However, even this mechanism is not true hypothecation in the UK National Insurance sense:
- The DCAF credits flow to the Fund, but the NDIS is funded through separate Commonwealth and state appropriations; the DCAF reimburses governments for expenditure already incurred
- The NDIS now costs approaching $50 billion annually — far exceeding what the 0.5% levy component raises (approximately $6–7 billion per year at current income levels)
- States did not have matching levy increases; the DCAF mechanism only covers the Commonwealth's share of a cost that has grown well beyond original projections

In 2017, the Turnbull government proposed raising the Medicare Levy from 2% to 2.5% to fund the growing NDIS cost. The proposal was ultimately abandoned after the government identified alternative budget savings — demonstrating that the levy remains a political instrument deployed opportunistically rather than a stable, rules-based health financing mechanism.

### 4. State-Level Genuine Hypothecation: Victoria and Queensland

While the Commonwealth has maintained its "not really hypothecated" approach, two states have built genuine, ring-fenced mental health levies in recent years.

**Victoria's Mental Health and Wellbeing Levy** commenced 1 January 2022 as a payroll tax surcharge on employers with national payrolls exceeding $10 million per year. Revenue is legislated to flow directly to mental health services and cannot be redirected to general government purposes. The levy is raising approximately **$900 million per year** by 2024-25. Total projected receipts over four years significantly exceeded initial estimates.

**Queensland's Mental Health Levy** operates similarly, as a payroll tax surcharge applied from 1 January 2023:
- 0.25% on Queensland taxable wages for employers with total Australian taxable wages above $10 million
- An additional 0.50% for employers with wages above $100 million

In 2023-24 (the first full collection year), Queensland's levy raised approximately **$494.8 million** — higher than initial forecasts. In 2024-25, it is estimated to raise approximately **$533 million**. The levy was initially projected to raise $1.6 billion over five years; revised figures suggest $2.3 billion — a 44% underprediction.

Combined, Victoria and Queensland's genuine mental health levies are raising over **$1.4 billion annually** and directing it to services.

The contrast with the Commonwealth approach is instructive: state governments facing immediate constituent pressure around mental health service quality adopted genuine hypothecation because it:
- Provides a political narrative ("your payroll levy funds your mental health system")
- Insulates the mental health budget from general fiscal trade-offs
- Creates automatic escalation as payrolls grow
- Enables sector planning on multi-year horizons

The Commonwealth has shown no appetite to adopt a similar approach at the federal level.

### 5. The Case for a New Levy — and Its Limits

The recurring gap between what the Medicare Levy raises and what Australia needs to spend on the unmet demand in mental health, aged care, and psychosocial support has prompted periodic advocacy for levy reform.

**The progressive levy proposal (Australia Institute):** The Australia Institute proposed redesigning the Medicare Levy as a progressive tax (different rates at different income levels), arguing the flat 2% is regressive as a share of effective income for lower earners. A progressive levy could raise more revenue while improving equity.

**The sector argument for a dedicated psychosocial levy:** Peak bodies including Mental Health Australia and RANZCP have not formally proposed a new levy for foundational supports, but the framing is present in the debate. If adult psychosocial foundational supports are to be funded at the scale of unmet need (230,500+ people), a dedicated, non-contestable revenue stream would insulate the sector from the cycle of serial two-year extensions that has characterised the CPSP.

**The case against:** The standard economic argument against hypothecation is that it reduces allocative efficiency — governments should fund the most valuable services regardless of where the revenue comes from. Ring-fencing one expenditure category prevents reallocation when priorities or costs change. The Productivity Commission has historically been sceptical of health hypothecation for this reason.

A second objection: at the Commonwealth level, a new psychosocial levy would require the Commonwealth-state matching architecture to be resolved. If the Commonwealth raises a new levy but states don't provide the matching services, the money risks being absorbed into general Commonwealth revenue while the service gap persists at state level.

A third objection: payroll tax levies (the state mechanism) affect labour costs and may reduce employment in affected industries. The incidence of these taxes falls partly on workers through lower wages; the mental health benefit accrues to the whole population; the distributional effects are not obvious.

### 6. The Foundational Supports Funding Equation

The most immediate policy relevance of the hypothecation debate is the foundational supports funding cliff now visible for July 2027.

The $3 billion Commonwealth foundational supports commitment includes:
- $2 billion for **Thriving Kids** (children's developmental supports)
- $1 billion for adult foundational supports — held in **contingency** pending state matching

For adult psychosocial foundational supports to serve even the CPSP's 25,000 existing clients at a modestly improved per-person rate, the required annual investment is approximately $150–200 million. Serving even 10% of the 230,500 with severe unmet need (23,000 additional people) at similar rates would require an additional $130 million per year. Scale to 20%? $400–500 million additional annually.

The contingency structure and state-matching requirement means the Commonwealth's $1 billion may not unlock at all, or may unlock only partially. Against this background, a dedicated hypothecated revenue stream — even a modest 0.1% employer payroll surcharge — could generate approximately $2–3 billion per year at the national level if designed like the Victorian mechanism.

No government has proposed this. The political challenge is real: a new health-related levy on business runs into employer opposition; expanding the Medicare Levy faces resistance from those who see it as a tax on employment income; and the government's stated preference is for "matching" from states rather than a new Commonwealth levy.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The fiscal fiction serves a political function.** The Medicare Levy's nominal link to Medicare creates public support for the system and provides a political constraint on cutting health funding. Genuine hypothecation might be economically more honest but would require governments to raise the rate as costs grow — creating a more visible and contestable health tax debate each budget. Some economists argue the current arrangement is politically optimal even if technically misleading.

**2. State-level hypothecation has worked — can the Commonwealth learn from it?** Victoria and Queensland's mental health levies demonstrate that a targeted, ring-fenced payroll surcharge can generate substantial, stable, and growing revenue for a specific health system priority. The Commonwealth's reluctance to adopt the same approach is partly constitutional (the Commonwealth cannot impose payroll taxes under the current federation structure) but mainly political.

**3. The DCAF has $46.9 billion in it — why?** The accumulation of credits in the DisabilityCare Australia Fund raises a governance question. The NDIS costs $50 billion annually; the DCAF has $46.9 billion accumulated. The Fund's purpose is to reimburse governments for NDIS expenditure, but governments have generally funded the NDIS through appropriations rather than drawing heavily on the DCAF. Whether this accumulated balance represents a genuine buffer, an accounting fiction, or a deferred fiscal liability is not clearly communicated to the public.

**4. A 0.5% increase in 2014 raised the NDIS spectre.** The only time the Medicare Levy rate was explicitly increased for a specific health purpose, the cost outran the mechanism within a decade. Any new levy designated for mental health foundational supports would face the same structural challenge: the cost of meeting unmet need is orders of magnitude larger than any politically feasible levy rate.

**5. Does the Commonwealth need a new instrument?** The more pragmatic path, advocated by some economists, is not a new levy but rather a stronger version of the Medicare Guarantee Fund — one that commits a specific share of the existing levy to psychosocial foundational supports, with a legislated floor and automatic annual escalation. This would not raise additional revenue but would make it harder for future governments to deprioritise the sector.
