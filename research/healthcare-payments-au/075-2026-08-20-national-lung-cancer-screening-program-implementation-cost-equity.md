# Australia's National Lung Cancer Screening Program: Implementation, Cost Model, and Equity Design in Year One

**Report 075 — 2026-08-20**

**Summary:** Australia's National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) launched on 1 July 2025, backed by $263.8 million in Commonwealth funding. In its first five months, approximately 37,000 low-dose CT scans were performed and 426 high-risk nodules referred for investigation. The program's MBS architecture is elegant but incomplete: radiology bulk billing is mandatory, but GP consultations to assess eligibility and manage results are not — a structural gap that risks replicating inequity within the program designed to address it. Rural and remote access is being partially addressed by mobile CT trucks, and NACCHO is co-designing culturally safe pathways for First Nations communities, but workforce, stigma, and radiology capacity constraints remain material risks.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 074 established that lung cancer is Australia's deadliest cancer, that five-year survival has tripled from 9.7% to 27.3% largely due to immunotherapy and targeted therapies, and that the National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP), commencing July 2025, had a modelled ICER placing it within cost-effective range. This report examines what the NLCSP actually looks like on the ground: its payment mechanics, eligible population, early outcomes, equity architecture, and the tensions that have emerged in year one.

---

## Background: From Evidence to Program

The case for lung cancer screening using low-dose CT (LDCT) was established by two landmark trials: the US National Lung Screening Trial (NLST, 2011) which showed a 20% mortality reduction versus chest X-ray in high-risk smokers, and the Dutch-Belgian NELSON trial (2020) which showed a 24–33% reduction versus no screening. The combination of this mortality evidence and Australia's improving lung cancer treatment landscape — where earlier-stage detection would allow more patients to access surgery or less costly systemic therapy — made a population-level program economically defensible.

The Medical Services Advisory Committee (MSAC) assessed the evidence under Application 1699 and provided positive advice in October 2022. The Albanese Government announced funding on 2 May 2023: **$263.8 million over four years** from 2023–24. New MBS items came into force on 1 July 2025.

## Key Findings

### 1. Eligibility: Who Qualifies and How Many Is That?

The NLCSP targets a narrow high-risk window:

- **Age:** 50–70 years (inclusive)
- **Smoking history:** At least **30 pack-years** of tobacco cigarette smoking (a pack-year = 20 cigarettes/day for one year; 30 pack-years = e.g. 30/day for 20 years, or 20/day for 30 years)
- **Currency of exposure:** Currently smoking, or have quit within the **past 10 years**
- **Asymptomatic:** No signs or symptoms suggestive of lung cancer at referral

This criterion set is deliberately conservative relative to some international programs (the US Preventive Services Task Force lowered its threshold to 20 pack-years in 2021), reflecting MSAC's cost-effectiveness assessment. The trade-off: a tighter eligible population means lower cost but some at-risk individuals are excluded.

Modelling published in *medrxiv* (2024) estimated the eligible population in year one (2025) at approximately **930,500 Australians** (95% prediction interval 852,200–1,019,000), representing roughly 12.8–14.1% of the 50–70 age group. This is the pool; actual uptake in year one has been far lower — ~37,000 scans in five months suggests an annualised rate of approximately 89,000, well under 10% of those eligible.

### 2. The MBS Payment Architecture

Two new MBS items underpin the radiology component:

| MBS Item | Description | Fee | Benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **57410** | Screening LDCT (approximately every 2 years) | $338.75 | 85% = $287.95 |
| **57413** | Follow-up/interval LDCT after prior screen | Not separately published | Comparable |

**Critical design feature:** Both items are **mandatorily bulk-billed**. Patients pay nothing for the CT scan. The radiology fee is entirely met by Medicare. This is the same model as BreastScreen (mobile mammography) but different from diagnostic imaging generally, where gap payments are routine.

**The GP gap:** There is **no dedicated MBS consultation item** for the eligibility assessment, shared decision-making discussion, or results management that a GP undertakes in the NLCSP pathway. GPs use standard attendance items (items 3, 23, 36, 44 or 123) for these consultations, and **bulk billing of those consultations is not mandatory**. In a healthcare system where GP bulk billing rates vary dramatically by region and population (see Report 009 on GP practice economics), this creates a meaningful out-of-pocket cost risk for patients who need GP access to enter the screening pathway — particularly disadvantaged patients who may have the highest tobacco-attributable lung cancer risk.

### 3. Program Infrastructure: From Fixed Sites to Mobile CT Trucks

Approximately 97% of NLCSP scans will be delivered through accredited fixed-site radiology providers — clinics and hospital imaging departments that meet RANZCR-developed technical and reporting standards including mandatory use of the **NLCSP Nodule Management Protocol** (standardised LDCT lung nodule identification, classification, and reporting). The RANZCR established a dedicated ANZSTR (Australia and New Zealand Society of Thoracic Radiology) NLCSP working group, attracting over 60 radiologists to support quality-assured implementation.

For rural and remote communities without ready access to fixed radiology services, **Heart of Australia** was contracted to operate **five mobile CT screening trucks** from November 2025. The first deployments cover:
- Heart 7: Kimberley and Pilbara regions of Western Australia
- Heart 8: Northern Territory

Heart of Australia already operates mobile specialist cardiac, respiratory, and general medical services to rural and remote Queensland, NT, and WA communities, making it a logical vehicle for mobile CT. The mobile service integrates with local primary care, ACCHO clinics, and WA Country Health Service infrastructure.

### 4. Early Results: First Five Months

Between 1 July and approximately late November 2025:
- **~37,000 LDCT scans** were performed nationally
- **426 high-risk or very high-risk nodules** were referred for further investigation

The 426/37,000 ratio (~1.2% high/very high-risk finding rate) is broadly consistent with international prevalence-round screening rates. Clinicians are beginning to report seeing earlier-stage, potentially curable cancers — the central clinical rationale for the program. No final outcomes data (resection rates, stage-shift, mortality) will be available for years, but the early nodule detection rate is functioning as predicted.

The uptake rate — roughly 37,000 in five months against ~930,000 eligible — reflects the program's ramp-up phase, awareness building among GPs and consumers, and the inherent friction in identifying eligible patients from practice records (see the MedicineInsight study on GP data quality for smoking history).

### 5. Equity Architecture: First Nations and CALD Communities

**First Nations co-design:** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people have disproportionately high lung cancer incidence and mortality — a product of higher smoking rates (substantially above the general population average), later-stage diagnosis, and reduced access to specialist treatment. NACCHO is leading co-design of the program's First Nations implementation in partnership with the ACCHO sector.

A specific implementation challenge: many First Nations people — particularly those in remote communities with fragmented healthcare relationships — lack documented smoking histories sufficient to calculate a 30 pack-year history in a standard clinical record. NACCHO developed a **proxy eligibility assessment approach** (published June 2025) allowing clinicians to use contextual clinical judgement and patient self-report where records are incomplete, reducing the risk that record-keeping gaps exclude eligible people.

**Culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities:** A 2026 qualitative study (PMC 12813417) found that CALD community members face compounding barriers including cancer stigma, language barriers, low health literacy around screening concepts, and financial barriers at the GP access point.

**Stigma as a structural barrier:** A 2025 commentary (Rose et al., *Health Promotion Journal of Australia*, PMC 11799825) identified stigma as an underappreciated barrier to NLCSP participation. Eligible participants are current or recent smokers — a group that has internalised social blame for their tobacco use, and who may experience healthcare interactions as judgmental. Research showed 21% of surveyed Australians believed lung cancer patients were "their own worst enemy" and 14% felt patients "had only themselves to blame." These attitudes, if expressed or implied in clinical consultations, will suppress participation in the highest-risk eligible population.

### 6. Cost-Effectiveness: How the Numbers Changed

The MSAC assessment used a modelled ICER of approximately **$65,663 per QALY gained** — above Australia's informal threshold of $50,000/QALY but considered acceptable by MSAC given the severity of lung cancer and the equity rationale. The MSAC recommended the program.

Subsequent modelling, published in the *Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific* (2024, Chen et al., PMC 11626775), incorporated the modern immunotherapy-era treatment landscape and NELSON trial long-term mortality data, yielding a considerably more favourable **ICER of AU$39,250 per QALY** (95% CI: $18,150–$108,300). The key driver: earlier-stage detection shifts patients from high-cost, long-duration immunotherapy (for metastatic disease) to curative surgery — a downstream cost saving that was not fully captured in the original MSAC modelling. If the modern ICER estimate better reflects reality, the NLCSP is among the more cost-effective interventions funded in recent health policy history.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The GP Consultation Gap Undermines the Program's Equity Rationale

The program's equity ambition — to reach disadvantaged populations who carry the highest tobacco burden — is at risk from its own payment architecture. Entering the NLCSP requires a GP encounter. That encounter is not mandatorily bulk-billed. In communities with low bulk billing rates (regional, rural, lower socioeconomic status), the prospect of a GP gap fee acts as a deterrent. PHNs have been tasked with awareness campaigns, but awareness alone does not remove financial barriers. The logical fix — a dedicated, mandatorily bulk-billed NLCSP eligibility and results consultation MBS item — was not funded in the initial program design. This is the same structural gap identified in breast and bowel cancer screening, where GP engagement is assumed but not paid for.

### Tension 2: Radiology Workforce Capacity

Adding ~89,000+ scans per year in 2025 (growing to full capacity as awareness builds) to an Australian radiology system already under strain creates capacity risk. The RANZCR working group attracted 60+ radiologists, but radiologist demand nationally is growing faster than training pipelines. The mandatory nodule management protocol — which requires specialist thoracic radiology interpretation — is more labour-intensive than routine chest CT reporting. AI-assisted LDCT nodule detection tools (several are TGA-registered) may partially address this, but have not been formally integrated into the NLCSP protocol.

### Tension 3: Uptake Below Eligible Population — a Normal Ramp or a Warning Sign?

The ~37,000 scans in five months against ~930,000 eligible (roughly 4% uptake in the first half-year) is lower than the program's long-term targets require. It may simply reflect the early ramp-up of any new national program — BreastScreen took years to reach target participation rates. But it may also reflect structural barriers: low GP awareness, absence of a registry-driven recall system (unlike BreastScreen and bowel screening, NLCSP does not proactively invite eligible patients — GP referral is required), and stigma. The absence of an active call-recall system is a deliberate design choice, partly reflecting data availability, but it is the single biggest structural driver of participation inequity in cancer screening internationally.

### Tension 4: The ICER Question and PBS Interaction

If the NLCSP successfully shifts lung cancer stage distribution — detecting more Stage I/II cancers curable by surgery — the downstream effect is reduced immunotherapy and targeted therapy utilisation at the PBS. But this PBS saving is not returned to the NLCSP budget or to the broader oncology funding envelope. The separate budgeting of the NLCSP (DoHDA capital investment) and the PBS means that a program success — lower stage-at-diagnosis, lower PBS expenditure per patient — does not generate a visible dividend within the health accounts that funded the program.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Nodule management after lung cancer screening: the downstream costs of incidental findings.** LDCT is highly sensitive — it detects nodules, ground-glass opacities, and incidental non-lung findings that require further investigation. What is the cost of the investigative cascade downstream of a screen, and how is it managed in the MBS? This is the "NLCSP cost iceberg" that sits below the scan itself.

2. **Australia's four national cancer screening programs compared: BreastScreen, NBCSP (bowel), NCSP (cervical), and NLCSP.** How does the NLCSP's equity design, call-recall mechanism, and cost model compare to the more mature programs? Bowel screening has active registry-driven mail-out; BreastScreen has mobile services; the NLCSP relies on GP referral. What are the participation equity implications?

3. **Pulmonary rehabilitation and COPD chronic disease management: the MBS gap.** Report 074 flagged that one million Australians have undiagnosed COPD and that spirometry item 11506 is under-utilised. The NLCSP will identify many COPD-positive patients through the LDCT pathway. What is the funded pathway for those patients beyond the screen?

4. **Smoking cessation integration in the NLCSP: the "teachable moment" opportunity.** The scanning appointment is a known "teachable moment" for cessation — international programs that integrate cessation support into the screening visit show higher quit rates than standard care. Is cessation support integrated into the NLCSP pathway, or is it a missed opportunity?

5. **The lung cancer nodule surveillance market: private radiology and out-of-pocket costs for follow-up.** Not all nodule follow-up fits within the MBS 57413 item. Some intermediate-risk nodules require follow-up CT at intervals that may not be MBS-covered under the program's protocol, or may require PET, bronchoscopy, or biopsy that attracts significant gaps.
