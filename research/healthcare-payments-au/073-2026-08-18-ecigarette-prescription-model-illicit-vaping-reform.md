# Australia's E-Cigarette Regulatory Collapse: The Pharmacy-Only Model, the 95.7% Illicit Market, and What Reform Looks Like

**Report 073 — 2026-08-18**

**Summary:** Australia has implemented the world's most restrictive framework for e-cigarettes — first prescription-only, then pharmacy-only — with the explicit goal of preventing youth vaping initiation while channelling nicotine-dependent smokers toward cessation. The result is paradoxical: the 2025 NDSHS shows daily smoking has fallen to a historic low of 5.8%, youth vaping is declining, and the pharmacy model is showing early signs of success — yet simultaneously, the Illicit Tobacco and E-cigarette (ITEC) Commissioner estimated that **95.7% of all vapes sold in Australia are illicit**, the illegal market is worth approximately $1.6 billion, and the same distribution networks serving illicit tobacco are saturating the country with unregulated vaping products. The regulation that protects the legal market is also, by making it inaccessible, sustaining the black market.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 072 examined Australia's illicit tobacco economy and found the ITEC Commissioner's finding that 95.7% of the e-cigarette market operates outside health and regulatory safeguards — cited as one of the three central tensions of the illicit tobacco crisis. This report examines the vaping regulatory framework in depth: how Australia arrived at its unique pharmacy-only model, what early evidence says about its effects, and what the 95.7% illicit market means for the public health rationale.

---

## Background: How Australia Arrived at the World's Most Restrictive Vaping Framework

Australia's approach to e-cigarettes has been shaped by a distinctive framing: vaping is a therapeutic good, not a consumer product. This framing has its origins in a 2017 TGA decision to schedule nicotine as a poison, making e-liquids containing nicotine available only by prescription (Schedule 4). For the seven years between 2017 and 2024, a smoker seeking vaping as a cessation aid in Australia needed a GP consultation, a prescription, and access to a pharmacy stocking imported therapeutic vaping goods — a process with sufficiently high friction that most simply did not complete it.

The predictable result was the growth of a large informal supply network. Vape shops operating in a legal grey area sold products without nicotine, while Chinese-manufactured disposable vapes (containing nicotine in breach of Australian law) flooded the country via online orders, informal retail, and later through the same distribution channels as illicit tobacco.

The legislative response, when it came, maintained the therapeutic goods framing while attempting to reduce the friction enough to compete with the illicit market.

### The Vaping Reforms Act 2024

The **Therapeutic Goods and Other Legislation Amendment (Vaping Reforms) Act 2024** commenced **1 July 2024** and represents the most significant structural overhaul of vaping regulation in Australia's history. Key elements:

**Stage 1 (from 1 July 2024):**
- All vapes — including disposable single-use devices — were reclassified as therapeutic goods
- Tobacconists, vape shops, and convenience stores were prohibited from supplying any vapes
- Commercial importation of non-compliant vaping goods became an offence
- Advertising of vapes banned across all media, including social media

**Stage 1B (from 1 October 2024):**
- Adults aged 18 and over could purchase therapeutic vapes from participating pharmacies **without a prescription**, subject to a pharmacist consultation and state/territory requirements
- Vapes with nicotine concentrations ≤20 mg/mL were rescheduled to **Schedule 3 (pharmacist-only)**; higher concentrations remained Schedule 4 (prescription-only)
- The pharmacist consultation required: confirming the patient is 18+; confirming other cessation methods have been tried; providing brief counselling; supplying a maximum of one month's stock

**Product Standards (from 1 July 2025 under TGO 110):**
- Maximum nicotine concentration: 20 mg/mL (Schedule 3) or 50 mg/mL (Schedule 4/prescription)
- Permitted flavours restricted to three: **mint, menthol, and tobacco**
- Child-attractive product names, colouring, and packaging prohibited
- Child-resistant packaging required
- Prohibited ingredients: vitamins, minerals, colourings, sweeteners beyond specified limits

The **Pharmaceutical Society of Australia (PSA)** trained pharmacists in the Schedule 3 vaping consultation, and the model positioned community pharmacy as the primary access point for nicotine vaping products — the same infrastructure used to dispense smoking cessation pharmacotherapy (varenicline, NRT) under the PBS.

## Key Findings

### 1. The 95.7% Illicit Market: Scale and Source

The headline finding from the **ITEC Commissioner's inaugural 2024–25 Annual Report** (December 2025) is striking: an estimated **95.7% of all vapes purchased in Australia are illicit**, leaving only 4.3% bought through legal pharmacy channels. The estimate was derived from a consumer survey of **4,000 nicotine product users** conducted in March 2025 — approximately nine months after the legal pharmacy supply channel opened on 1 October 2024.

The illicit vaping market's estimated value is approximately **AUD $1.6 billion** — roughly as large as, or larger than, the legal market for nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and smoking cessation pharmacotherapy combined. The Commissioner also found:

- The **Australian Border Force** seized over **6 million illicit vapes** in FY2024–25, with more than **3 million intercepted in Q3 2025 alone** — the largest single quarter on record — representing approximately 120 border detections every day of the year
- Despite these seizures, the illicit market operates at near-saturation levels: seizures capture significant volume but clearly not the majority of illicit supply
- 95.7% of the entire e-cigarette market operates outside health and regulatory safeguards — meaning without plain packaging, approved ingredients lists, child-resistant safety requirements, age verification at point of sale, or any of the quality controls that the therapeutic goods framework mandates

The illicit vaping market shares its distribution infrastructure with the illicit tobacco market documented in Report 072. Many retailers selling illicit tobacco also sell illicit vapes; the same transnational supply chains (primarily from Chinese manufacturing, via Southeast Asian transit points) service both product categories.

### 2. Why the Pharmacy Model Struggled to Compete

The 95.7% illicit market share reflects a structural mismatch between the legal supply channel and consumer behaviour.

**Access friction:** The Schedule 3 consultation model, while simpler than the prior prescription pathway, still requires:
- A physical visit to a participating pharmacy
- A consultation with a pharmacist during pharmacy opening hours
- Disclosure of prior cessation attempts
- Purchase of a maximum one month's supply at a time

By contrast, illicit vapes are available:
- Through informal retail premises (convenience stores, tobacconists continuing to sell in defiance of the law, market stalls)
- Via social media ordering and postal delivery
- At far lower prices than the pharmacy-stocked products

**Price differential:** Pharmacy-stocked therapeutic vapes, compliant with TGO 110 standards, retail at approximately $25–$45 per device (with ongoing e-liquid cost). Illicit disposable vapes — Chinese-manufactured, nicotine-containing, non-compliant — sell for $5–$15 per device. The price differential, while not as extreme as the illicit tobacco market ($50 legal cigarettes vs $10–$20 illicit), is sufficient to drive most price-sensitive consumers to the cheaper, more accessible option.

**Flavour restrictions:** TGO 110's restriction to three permitted flavours (mint, menthol, tobacco) eliminated the vast majority of the existing vape product market, which was built around hundreds of flavour variants. Youth-targeted illicit products continue to use fruit, candy, and dessert flavours — precisely the flavours that the standards were designed to eliminate from the legal market.

**Pharmacist participation:** Not all pharmacies elected to stock and supply therapeutic vapes. The requirement to conduct a consultation, maintain product compliance records, and restrict supply to one month at a time was seen by some pharmacists as creating professional liability without adequate professional service remuneration. There is no separate MBS rebate or professional services payment for the pharmacist vaping consultation; pharmacists are reimbursed only through the product margin.

### 3. NDSHS 2025: The Paradox of Falling Rates and a Dominant Illicit Market

The **National Drug Strategy Household Survey 2025** (AIHW, published July 2026) presents data that appears in tension with the ITEC Commissioner's findings — but on examination, they describe two different parts of the market.

**Historic smoking low:**
- Daily smoking among Australians aged 18+ fell to **5.8%** in 2025 — a historic low, and well below the National Tobacco Strategy's 2025 target of 10%
- Among all Australians aged 14+, daily smoking fell to **5.6%**
- Australia has approximately **500,000 fewer daily smokers** than three years previously
- Smoking among 14–17 year olds fell to **1.2%**, down from 3.2% in 2019

**Vaping rates stabilising:**
- Daily vaping among Australians 18+ stabilised at **3.6%** in 2025, the first time the rate has not risen year-on-year since comprehensive measurement began
- Current vaping prevalence (any frequency) fell from **7% (2022–23)** to **6% (2025)**
- Daily vaping among 18–24 year olds: **8.3%** in 2025, down from 9.8% in 2022–23
- Approximately **40,000 fewer young people** are vaping compared to the peak in 2022–23

**Youth vaping peak passed:**
- Vaping among 14–17 year olds has declined from its 2022–23 peak
- The reforms appear to have reduced youth initiation — particularly among young people who would not have otherwise smoked

**The paradox resolved:** The NDSHS captures legal and illicit behaviour together. The *decline* in reported vaping prevalence reflects genuine reduction in uptake — particularly among youth, where the retail channel disruption (closing tobacconists and vape shops) removed the most common access point for underage users. But the *continued use* among adults — including the 8.3% daily rate in 18–24 year olds — is predominantly served by the illicit market, not the pharmacy channel. The pharmacy channel captures 4.3% of purchases; the other 95.7% are the adult vaping market that has shifted to illicit supply.

A parallel data point: **1 in 4 young Australians** (Quit Victoria, 2026) is still using some form of nicotine product — a figure that encompasses both vapes and tobacco. The reform has not eliminated youth nicotine use; it has shifted its composition.

### 4. Australia vs New Zealand: A Natural Experiment in Regulatory Philosophy

The starkest evidence in the regulatory debate comes from comparing Australia and New Zealand — two countries with similar demographics, healthcare systems, and baseline smoking rates that made fundamentally different regulatory choices about vaping.

A peer-reviewed study by Mendelsohn et al. (*Addiction*, 2025) analysed diverging trends between 2016 and 2023:

| Metric | Australia | New Zealand |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Annual rate of smoking rate decline | 5% per year | 10% per year |
| Annual decline in lowest socioeconomic group | 4% per year | 12% per year |
| Annual decline, Indigenous/Māori population | 6% per year | 16% per year |
| Youth vaping peak (18–24 daily) | 9.8% (2022–23) | ~8.7% (2024, now declining) |
| Evidence of illicit vaping market | Substantial (95.7% illicit) | No significant evidence |

New Zealand adopted a regulated consumer model for vaping in 2020 (Smokefree Environments and Regulated Products Amendment Act 2020), allowing adult retail sale through general retail with age verification. Australia maintained the prescription-only (later pharmacy-only) therapeutic goods model.

The Mendelsohn findings are contested. Critics (Greenhalgh et al., *Addiction*, 2025) argue the study confounds the impact of vaping policy with other tobacco control measures, including New Zealand's smoke-free generation legislation (since repealed), and that methodological choices inflate the apparent effect. The policy community remains divided.

However, the New Zealand evidence on *illicit markets* is less disputed: under a consumer retail model with age verification, no significant illicit vaping trade developed in New Zealand. The illicit market is, in part, a policy artefact of the prescription/pharmacy-only approach — it exists because the regulated supply channel is too inaccessible to compete.

### 5. The Cessation Evidence Base: Vaping's Contested Role

Within the pharmacy model, the evidence for vaping as a cessation tool is strong:

- A 2026 study (*MJA InSight+*, Mendelsohn) found that vaping was approximately **three times more effective than NRT** for smoking cessation among disadvantaged Australians: **28.4% continuous abstinence** in the vaping group vs **9.6%** in the NRT group
- PBS-funded smoking cessation pharmacotherapy (varenicline/Champix, bupropion) achieves approximately 15–20% six-month abstinence rates in clinical trial settings, but lower rates in real-world populations

The public health challenge is that the most effective cessation tool (vaping) is largely delivered through the illicit market, where it operates without clinical guidance, standardised products, or connection to the PBS cessation pharmacotherapy pathway. A smoker accessing illicit vapes as a quit aid is not connected to a pharmacist who could also offer Quitline referral, brief advice, or varenicline support.

The South Australia repeated cross-sectional study (University of Adelaide, published 2025) found early signals that the reformed framework corresponded with reductions in e-cigarette prevalence driven *mainly by declines among youth who had never smoked* — suggesting that the primary public health benefit of the reforms so far has been prevention of youth nicotine initiation, rather than adult smoking cessation.

### 6. The Healthcare Payments Dimension

The vaping regulatory architecture sits awkwardly within Australia's health payments framework:

**No Medicare rebate:** The pharmacist Schedule 3 vaping consultation attracts no MBS item payment. Pharmacists are reimbursed only through product margin. Unlike the PBS-funded NRT or the MBS-funded GP cessation consultation (MBS item 4605 — the Level B consultation for chronic condition management that would often cover smoking), the pharmacy vaping consultation sits entirely outside Commonwealth funding. Pharmacists bear professional liability for an increasingly complex consultation without dedicated remuneration — a structural disincentive to participation.

**PBS cessation pharmacotherapy:** Varenicline (Champix) was PBS-listed (General Schedule), removed following a global recall, relisted as Champix is replaced by generic varenicline preparations. Nicotine patches/gum are available both PBS and OTC. Vaping is not PBS-funded and is not reimbursable through private health insurance extras in most funds.

**Illicit market healthcare cost:** Unregulated illicit vapes present healthcare risks that the legal therapeutic goods framework was designed to eliminate — unknown ingredient quality, heavy metals from unregulated heating coils, vitamin E acetate (implicated in EVALI, e-cigarette or vaping product use–associated lung injury in the US), and high concentrations of undisclosed additives. Australia has not yet documented the same scale of EVALI cases as the US, but with 95.7% of the market operating outside quality controls, this is a prospective healthcare payment risk rather than a current measured one.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Pharmacy Model's Commercial Viability

The pharmacy-only model depends on pharmacies electing to participate, maintaining compliant stock, and conducting professionally appropriate consultations without dedicated MBS payment. Early evidence suggests participation is uneven. The Lung Foundation Australia's October 2025 position statement on online dispensing raised the question of whether online pharmacy supply — with prescriber-authenticated consultations — might expand access without requiring physical visits. The TGA has been working through the policy framework for online therapeutic vape supply, but this remains unresolved as of mid-2026.

### Tension 2: The Harm Reduction vs. Abstinence Debate

The deepest fault line in Australian vaping policy is not about enforcement or product standards — it is about whether vaping is better understood as a harm reduction tool for smokers or as a new nicotine addiction risk for non-smokers. The public health community is genuinely divided:

- **Abstentionists** (Cancer Council Australia, Heart Foundation, Australian Medical Association): vaping creates new nicotine dependence and is a gateway to smoking for youth; any liberalisation risks undermining 30 years of tobacco control gains; the 5.8% historic smoking low vindicates the restrictive approach
- **Harm reductionists** (Dr Colin Mendelsohn, NNA Australia, Harm Reduction Australia): vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking; the fastest way to reduce smoking-related disease burden is to make the most effective cessation tool easily accessible; the NZ comparison shows the cost of over-restriction; the illicit market is the real harm

Neither camp has a consensus policy solution to the 95.7% illicit market finding.

### Tension 3: Flavour Restriction and Youth Access

The TGO 110 flavour restrictions (mint, menthol, tobacco only) represent one of the most aggressive flavour policies in the world. Public health advocates argue that flavour diversity drives youth initiation and must be eliminated from the legal market. Industry and harm-reduction advocates argue that flavour variety is one of the primary reasons adult smokers find vaping an effective cigarette substitute, and that restricting flavours in the legal market pushes adult vapers to illicit products (where hundreds of flavours remain available).

The empirical question — whether flavour restriction in the legal market reduces youth initiation, or simply shifts it to illicit supply — cannot yet be answered from Australian data alone.

### Tension 4: The Online Supply Gap

A key market segment largely unaddressed by the current framework is online supply of illicit vapes — ordered via social media (Instagram, TikTok, WeChat) and delivered by post. Social media platforms have advertising restrictions but do not effectively police vape sales to Australian users. Australia Post and private courier companies are not resourced or mandated to detect vape shipments. The TGA's online enforcement is consumer-facing; the supply networks operate from overseas with little consequence.

### Tension 5: Vaping as a Gateway to Tobacco

The NDSHS 2025 found that use of multiple nicotine products has doubled in the last year. Among young Australians, the pattern of co-use (vaping and smoking concurrently) rather than substitution complicates the harm reduction narrative. If a youth who would not otherwise have smoked uses illicit vapes for two years and then adds cigarettes, the net harm calculation is unfavourable regardless of the cessation efficacy data for existing adult smokers.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Tobacco-related disease burden in 2026: COPD, lung cancer, and cardiovascular disease costs.** Smoking causes approximately 22,000 deaths per year in Australia and is the leading preventable cause of disease burden. Quantifying the hospital, MBS, and PBS costs of tobacco-related disease frames the economic stakes of both the illicit market and the vaping cessation debate. (Directly flagged as a thread in Report 072.)

2. **The MJA/clinical community's position on vaping as cessation therapy.** The 2026 *MJA InSight+* evidence that vaping is 3× more effective than NRT for disadvantaged smokers puts pressure on the PBS not listing vaping, and the MBS not funding the consultation. Is there a clinical pathway toward subsidised vaping-as-cessation?

3. **Online pharmacy supply of vaping products: TGA consultation and regulatory design.** The Lung Foundation's October 2025 position statement and the TGA's unresolved online supply framework represent the next structural reform moment. What does a workable online model look like, and what are the safeguards?

4. **First Nations youth vaping: the 22% lifetime use figure from the 2022–23 NATSIHS.** Previously flagged in Report 072 — whether vaping is substituting for tobacco or layering new dependence in Indigenous youth communities.

5. **E-cigarette product safety and EVALI risk in Australia.** With 95.7% of the vaping market unregulated for ingredient quality, the EVALI experience in the United States (2,807 hospitalised, 68 deaths, 2019–2020) is a prospective risk for Australia. The TGA's surveillance and regulatory response architecture for this risk deserves examination.
