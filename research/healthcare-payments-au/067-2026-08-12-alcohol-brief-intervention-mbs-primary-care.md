# Alcohol Screening and Brief Intervention in Australian Primary Care: The Missing MBS Item

**Report 067 — 2026-08-12**

**Summary:** Australia's general practice system has no dedicated Medicare Benefits Schedule item for alcohol screening and brief intervention — one of the most evidence-based, cost-effective preventive health services in existence. Despite 6.6 million Australians drinking at hazardous levels, $66.8 billion in annual social and economic costs from alcohol use, and a Cochrane evidence base demonstrating that a 5–15 minute GP conversation reduces alcohol consumption by approximately two standard drinks per week, brief alcohol interventions remain structurally unfunded in mainstream Australian primary care. The House Standing Committee Inquiry into the health impacts of alcohol and other drugs in Australia (re-referred August 2025, final hearings May 2026) has exposed the full scale of this gap. The absence of a dedicated MBS item is not a technical oversight — it is a political economy failure driven by the alcohol industry's lobbying power, the low clinical status of addiction medicine, and the persistent underfunding of prevention across Australia's payment architecture. Fixing it would be among the lowest-cost, highest-return interventions in the health system.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 066 documented that Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder — a 100% preventable condition affecting an estimated 3.64% of Australians — is the direct downstream consequence of hazardous and dependent alcohol use going unidentified in primary care. The report identified the absence of a dedicated MBS brief intervention item as "the single most tractable policy change that would reduce FASD incidence over time." This report examines exactly what that item would look like, why it does not exist, and what it would cost to create one.

---

## Background

### The Scale of the Problem

Alcohol is the most widely used psychoactive substance in Australia and, by most measures, the most harmful. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) reports that in 2022–23:

- **31% of Australians aged 14 and over** (approximately **6.6 million people**) consumed alcohol at levels that put their health at risk — defined as more than 10 standard drinks per week or more than 4 standard drinks on a single occasion at least once a month. This hazardous drinking rate has remained essentially unchanged since 2019 (32%).

- **Alcohol was the principal drug of concern** in 41% of all treatment episodes for Australians seeking help for their own drug use in 2024–25 — the largest single category, ahead of all illicit drugs combined.

- **84,300 alcohol-related hospitalisations** occurred in 2023–24, representing approximately 3 in 5 drug-related hospital admissions (58%). These are resource-intensive, often preventable episodes.

- Approximately **1 in 5 Australians** (4.6 million people) over the age of 14 reported being verbally or physically abused, or put in fear, by someone under the influence of alcohol in the 12 months to 2022–23.

The most recent comprehensive economic analysis of alcohol harms, published by the National Drug Research Institute (NDRI) at Curtin University in 2021, estimated the **total social and economic costs of alcohol use in Australia at $66.8 billion per year** (for the 2017–18 financial year). The major cost components include:

- Intangible costs of premature mortality: **$25.9 billion** (lost years of life)
- Lost quality of life from alcohol dependence: **$20.7 billion**
- Workplace costs (absenteeism, injury): **$4.0 billion**
- Crime: **$3.1 billion**
- Direct healthcare costs: **$2.8 billion**
- Road traffic crashes: **$2.4 billion**

A more recent multistate life table modelling study published in *International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction* (2024–25; the TAP model, led by Griffith University researchers) provides a forward-looking estimate: maintaining current drinking patterns will generate **25.9 million new incident disease and injury cases** and **$55 billion in healthcare costs** over the next 25 years. Reducing consumption to low-risk levels (as defined by NHMRC guidelines) would prevent 4.4 million years of healthy life being lost and save **$49 billion** in healthcare costs over the same period.

A landmark 2026 review article in the *Internal Medicine Journal* (Palmer, Connor and Clark) summarises the current state of evidence: alcohol accounts for **4.1% of the national disease burden** but "remains under-prioritised in health policy, prevention and treatment," with "most harm being downstream of hazardous alcohol consumption and untreated alcohol use disorder (AUD), a common but stigmatised condition that is under-recognised and rarely treated with evidence-based therapies." Alcohol-related harm disproportionately burdens First Nations peoples, rural communities, and socioeconomically disadvantaged Australians.

## What Is Alcohol Screening and Brief Intervention (SBI)?

### The Clinical Pathway

Alcohol screening and brief intervention (sometimes abbreviated SBI or ASBI, or simply 'brief intervention') refers to a structured, time-limited approach to identifying patients with hazardous alcohol use in a clinical setting and delivering a short, evidence-based behavioural intervention to reduce consumption or facilitate referral to treatment.

The recommended pathway follows a sequential logic:

**Step 1 — Screen:** Administer a validated, brief screening instrument. The most widely used globally is the **Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT)**, a 10-item self-report questionnaire developed by the World Health Organization. The **AUDIT-C** is a validated 3-item abbreviated version, used in Australian general practice software systems. In Australia, the **RACGP recommends universal screening** of all patients aged 15 and over every 2–4 years using the AUDIT or AUDIT-C.

**Step 2 — Assess:** Score the screening instrument. AUDIT scores distinguish:
- Zone I (score 0–7): low risk or abstinent
- Zone II (score 8–15): hazardous drinking → brief advice
- Zone III (score 16–19): harmful drinking → brief counselling
- Zone IV (score 20–40): probable dependence → referral for specialist assessment

**Step 3 — Intervene:** For patients in Zones II–III, deliver a brief intervention. The most widely taught framework in Australia is the **5As** (Ask, Assess, Advise/Agree, Assist, Arrange), supported by the RACGP and South Western Sydney PHN. The FRAMES model (Feedback, Responsibility, Advice, Menu of options, Empathy, Self-Efficacy) is an alternative international framework, based on motivational interviewing principles. A brief intervention is typically **5–15 minutes** in a single consultation, though evidence also supports brief multi-contact formats over 2–4 visits.

**Step 4 — Refer:** For patients in Zone IV (probable dependence), provide appropriate referral to specialist addiction services, community drug treatment, or opioid/alcohol treatment programs (as covered in Reports 058 and 065).

### Evidence Base

The evidence for alcohol brief interventions in primary care is among the strongest in preventive medicine. The **2018 Cochrane systematic review** (Kaner et al., *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*) remains the definitive synthesis:

- **64 randomised controlled trials** with 27,546 participants
- Brief interventions resulted in an average **reduction of 20g of alcohol per week** (approximately two standard drinks) compared to controls at 12-month follow-up
- The effect was consistent across settings and follow-up periods
- Brief advice (as few as 5 minutes) and extended brief interventions showed similar efficacy
- Effects were stronger in primary care settings than hospital emergency departments

The **WHO** classifies SBI for hazardous alcohol use as one of the most cost-effective public health interventions globally. A 2011 cost-effectiveness study from rural Australia (Collins and Wratten, *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*) found that increments in GP brief intervention rates generated cost-effective reductions in hazardous drinking, with costs per additional risky drinker achieving harm reduction that easily clear conventional healthcare cost-effectiveness thresholds.

A 2025 estimate by the National Preventive Health Agency found a **return of $14 for every $1 invested** in preventive health programs — a ratio that independent analyses of SBI for alcohol consistently meet or exceed in the international literature.

## The MBS Landscape: What Exists and What Doesn't

### Current MBS Coverage for Alcohol-Related Activities

Australian GPs have access to several MBS items that *can* include alcohol-related activities, but **no dedicated funded brief intervention pathway** exists:

**MBS Item 715 — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Assessment:**
This comprehensive annual health assessment explicitly includes an alcohol screening component (AUDIT-C administration) and can generate a referral to allied health via the Follow-Up items (10987, 10997). The 715 item generates a rebate of approximately $237.10 (full; 30+ minutes). This is a significant enabler of structured screening in the ACCHO sector (see Reports 013 and 058), but its use is restricted to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. The 715 is the closest model Australia has to a structured, funded screening-and-referral pathway for alcohol.

**MBS Item 16591 — Psychosocial Assessment in Antenatal Care (alcohol at 28 weeks):**
A rebated MBS item that *includes* alcohol screening as one component of a broader antenatal psychosocial screen, claimable once per pregnancy at approximately 28 weeks gestation. This is the only population-specific, funded alcohol screening item for the general public — and its narrow constraint (once, at 28 weeks, in pregnancy) means it captures only a small fraction of pregnant women and fails entirely to identify women who first become pregnant after developing hazardous drinking patterns.

**MBS General Attendance Items (23, 36, 44, 36, etc.):**
A GP can, in principle, discuss alcohol use as part of a general consultation under MBS Item 23 (Level B, under 20 minutes; rebate $43.90 from July 2025). However:
- There is no item that *specifically* funds or incentivises AUDIT administration and brief intervention as a distinct, claimable service
- The time required for a proper AUDIT, scoring, and brief intervention (15–20 minutes) typically pushes the consultation into MBS Item 36 territory (Level C, 20–40 minutes; rebate $85.45 from July 2025)
- No item provides a **dedicated incentive payment** equivalent to the Bulk Billing Practice Incentive Payment or the 75+ health assessment — a payment that specifically signals "do this for your patients' benefit"
- GP contractors working under a 70–75% billing model have a strong financial disincentive to extend consultations into alcohol screening when the rebate does not reflect the full cost of service

**MBS Chronic Disease Management Items (965, 967 from July 2025):**
The new GPCCMP items can include alcohol-related goals in a chronic disease plan, but only for patients with *existing* chronic conditions (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, COPD) — they are management items, not screening items, and require MyMedicare enrolment for full benefit access from 2027.

**Health Assessment Items (700-series, 75+ items):**
The 45–49-year health assessment (Item 717) includes a broad lifestyle assessment that *can* include alcohol, but does not mandate AUDIT administration or fund a structured brief intervention component. Similarly, the 75+ annual health assessment includes general lifestyle review. These items are claimable once per relevant age window (or annually for 75+), limiting their use as a population screening mechanism for hazardous drinking.

### The Critical Gap

No MBS item:
1. **Specifically funds AUDIT administration and scoring** as a distinct billable service in general practice for the general adult population
2. **Remunera a brief alcohol intervention** as a named, incentivised service
3. **Creates a clinical decision support signal** in the billing structure that prompts GPs to deliver brief interventions as a standard of care

This is in marked contrast to depression screening (PHQ-9 embedded within mental health treatment plans; MHTP creates a funded pathway under Item 2715), diabetes management (structured CDM items), and smoking cessation counselling (which can be billed under general attendance items with specific guidance and is supported by the Quitline referral infrastructure).

## Current Implementation Rates and the Detection Gap

The result of this structural gap is exactly what economic theory predicts: brief alcohol interventions are delivered at a small fraction of the clinically indicated rate.

**Key implementation data:**
- Only **56.2% of regular patients aged 15 and over** have an alcohol status *recorded* in their general practice electronic health record — meaning nearly half of all adults do not even have a documented screening history
- A cluster randomised trial published in *BMC Primary Care* (September 2024) examining Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services found that a structured support model for delivering brief interventions produced "a small but significant difference in provision of 'any treatment' for unhealthy alcohol use" — but rates of both verbal brief interventions and pharmacotherapy prescriptions remained low and were "not clinically significant" in their absolute magnitude
- The 2021 National Drug Strategy midpoint scorecard confirmed that brief intervention delivery in primary care remained a persistent implementation gap relative to international benchmarks

Qualitative research has consistently identified the same barriers:
1. **No time:** A brief intervention takes 15–20 minutes; a standard Item 23 consultation is funded for up to 20 minutes with a rebate that does not compensate for the time alcohol discussion requires
2. **No payment signal:** GPs report that alcohol is one of many competing preventive health priorities, with no payment system that specifically rewards addressing it
3. **No clinical confidence:** GP training in alcohol management remains inadequate; most training focuses on dependence recognition, not the motivational interviewing skills needed for zones II–III
4. **Stigma in the consultation room:** Both GPs and patients report discomfort raising alcohol; GPs fear damaging therapeutic relationships; patients fear judgement
5. **No referral pathway clarity:** After a brief intervention, GPs frequently do not know where to refer patients who need further support — and the referral infrastructure for Zone III–IV patients is itself underfunded (see Reports 058, 065)

The RACGP's June 2024 Funding Priorities Position Statement identifies primary care alcohol screening and brief intervention as an area requiring MBS investment, but does not specify a concrete item structure or funding quantum.

## The Policy Case for a Dedicated MBS Item

### What an Item Would Look Like

Policy advocates, academic researchers, and the RACGP have at various points described what a dedicated MBS brief alcohol intervention item would need to include. Based on the literature and comparable MBS items (particularly the mental health care plan and annual health assessment models), a functional brief intervention MBS item structure might include:

**Component 1 — Screening item (~$25–35):** A rebated item for GP administration of AUDIT or AUDIT-C, scoring, and documentation. Claimable once per 12 months per patient. Modelled on the approach of preventive health items in the 40–49 and 75+ cohort assessments, but available universally (or at minimum for adults 18+ with at-risk indicators such as attendance at an AOD-adjacent clinical setting).

**Component 2 — Brief intervention item (~$55–75):** A rebated item for delivery of a structured 10–20 minute brief alcohol intervention using FRAMES or 5As, for patients who screen in Zone II or III. Separate from the general attendance item, with a fee that reflects the complexity and skill involved.

**Component 3 — Extended brief intervention item (~$120–150):** For Zone III (harmful drinking), a multi-contact extended brief intervention item enabling up to 3–4 funded sessions, analogous to the Mental Health Treatment Plan extended items for psychology referral.

**Component 4 — Enhanced referral item:** A navigation item for Zone IV patients enabling GP coordination with specialist AOD services, analogous to the Chronic Disease Management referral item.

The total annual cost of a well-designed brief intervention MBS package, if claimed at clinically indicated rates, is difficult to estimate without detailed modelling. However, international comparisons are illustrative: the UK's National Health Service funds the **Identification and Brief Advice (IBA)** program for alcohol across all NHS settings, with an estimated per-encounter cost of £6–14 (approximately AU$12–28) for the screening and brief advice component alone. If Australia funded brief interventions for even 20% of the estimated 6.6 million hazardous drinkers who should be screened annually, at a cost of AU$50 per encounter, the total cost would be approximately AU$660 million per year — against a social and economic cost burden of $66.8 billion annually, a cost-benefit ratio of approximately 100:1 before any healthcare savings are calculated.

### The Inquiry Context: 2025–2026

The House Standing Committee on Health, Aged Care and Disability's **Inquiry into the health impacts of alcohol and other drugs in Australia** has been the defining policy process for this question in the current parliamentary term.

The inquiry was originally established in the 43rd Parliament but lapsed. It was re-referred by Minister Mark Butler in **August 2025** with expanded terms of reference: a health-focused review of alcohol and other drugs policy, treatment services, community programs, and workforce. It received over 200 submissions.

An **Issues Paper** was published in **March 2025**, and the Australian Government provided its response to that Issues Paper in **March 2026**. The inquiry's final public hearings were held in **May 2026**. A final committee report, which would include any recommendations on MBS primary care funding, was expected in the second half of 2026 but had not been released as of the date of this report.

Submissions from the **Foundation for Alcohol Research and Education (FARE)**, **Alcohol Change Australia**, the **National Drug and Alcohol Research Centre (NDARC)**, and multiple academic clinicians have consistently recommended:
- Implementing routine alcohol screening and brief intervention as standard practice in primary care
- Equipping GPs with targeted training and incentive structures
- Funding brief alcohol interventions as a distinct MBS item, separate from general attendance items

The NACCHO submission (December 2024) specifically highlighted the need for culturally safe alcohol screening tools and brief intervention resources within ACCHOs, noting the limitations of the 715 item as the sole funded pathway.

## International Comparisons

**United Kingdom — NHS IBA Program:**
The NHS has funded alcohol Identification and Brief Advice (IBA) across all NHS settings since 2008, with a national rollout to GP practices under the Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF) and Directed Enhanced Services. GP practices are paid an annual figure for maintaining an alcohol register and delivering brief advice. This has resulted in substantially higher screening rates than Australia and is one of the most studied brief intervention programs in the world.

**Canada — Screening and Brief Intervention for Alcohol:**
Canada's provincial health systems have variably funded brief interventions in primary care. British Columbia's health authorities fund specific addiction medicine consultations, and the Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (CCSA) publishes national guidance, though billing varies by province and is not nationally standardised.

**United States — SBIRT Program:**
The United States has funded Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment (SBIRT) for alcohol and other drugs across multiple Medicaid and Medicare programs since 2007. Specific Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes (99408, 99409) exist for brief intervention delivery, providing billing recognition in primary care settings.

Australia is a significant outlier among comparable OECD nations in not having a dedicated billing mechanism for GP-delivered alcohol brief interventions. This gap is particularly significant given Australia's high rates of hazardous drinking (31%) relative to OECD comparisons, and the comparatively high social and economic cost burden ($66.8 billion annually).

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Why hasn't this been done already?**

The persistence of this gap across successive Labor and Coalition governments is not an accident. Key structural factors:

- The **alcohol industry's political influence** in Australia is substantial. The Wine Australia lobby, Beer & Brewer Federation, and Spirits & Cocktails Australia have consistently opposed measures that would identify and reduce harmful drinking at the population level, including mandatory warning labels (as documented in Report 066) and GP-level screening infrastructure that would generate referral pathways away from consumption. This industry lobbying capacity has historically exceeded the counter-advocacy capacity of health groups on this specific issue.

- **Addiction medicine has low clinical status** relative to other specialties. There is no Medicare item for addiction medicine specialist consultations in the same category as cardiology or oncology; the field is under-resourced, under-trained, and historically under-funded in the MBS. A GP brief intervention item would require political will that has not materialised.

- **Prevention underfunding** is systemic across the health system, not specific to alcohol. Australia spends less than 2% of health expenditure on prevention (see Report 066), despite the National Preventive Health Strategy targeting 5% by 2030. A brief intervention item for alcohol would be a prevention expenditure, and preventive health items face structural MBS review scrutiny that treatment items do not.

**Does brief intervention work at population scale?**

The Cochrane evidence is strong at the individual level (20g/week reduction) but the population-level impact depends on implementation fidelity, which is historically poor outside trial conditions. The UK NHS IBA experience shows that even with payment incentives, many GPs do not deliver structured brief advice unless supported by digital prompts, training, and quality monitoring. An MBS item alone, without complementary training and clinical decision support infrastructure, may produce a lower-than-expected population-level effect.

**Who funds the infrastructure?**

An MBS item for alcohol brief intervention addresses GP billing but does not fund:
- Training clinicians in AUDIT administration and motivational interviewing
- Clinical decision support integration (prompting GP software to flag at-risk drinkers)
- Referral pathways for patients who screen in Zone IV and need specialist services
- The "stepped care" model that brief interventions conceptually sit within

Without investment in these complementary elements, even a funded brief intervention item may struggle to produce the health outcomes the economic models project.

**The National Alcohol Strategy 2019–2028:**
The current strategy commits to "implement routine alcohol screening and brief intervention as standard practice in primary care" — but without an MBS item, this commitment has no funding mechanism. The strategy's expiry coincides with the successor National Drug Strategy 2027 consultation underway in 2026 (Drug and Alcohol Program grants extended to 30 June 2027). The successor framework represents an opportunity to embed a dedicated MBS brief intervention item as a funded commitment, rather than an aspirational statement.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **National Drug Strategy 2027 — architecture of a successor framework:** The NDS 2017–2026 expires this year; DAP grants have been extended to July 2027. What should the successor strategy commit to across the demand reduction, supply reduction, and harm reduction pillars, and how does an MBS brief intervention item fit within it?

2. **First Nations AOD and ACCHO pharmacotherapy access** (persistently deferred from Reports 058, 059, 065): The gap between AUDIT-C use in MBS Item 715 health assessments and actual naltrexone/acamprosate prescribing rates (3.4% of likely-dependent patients) represents a critical structural failure in culturally safe AUD care.

3. **GLP-1 receptor agonists for AUD and the PBS pathway** (deferred from Report 065): The May 2026 *Lancet* RCT for semaglutide in AUD raises new questions about what a TGA–PBAC pathway for a GLP-1 AUD indication would look like, and whether this novel pharmacotherapy would be accessible to high-burden populations.

4. **The stepped care model in AOD: how the tiers connect** — Brief intervention in primary care sits at the base of a stepped care pyramid. Above it are: counselling/community AOD services (Report 047–051), pharmacotherapy (Report 058, 065), residential rehabilitation, and specialist acute care. A report synthesising how funding flows across these tiers, and where the handoff points break down, would map the full system.

5. **Alcohol and cancer: the PBS/MBS implications of a known carcinogen** — Alcohol is a Group 1 IARC carcinogen causally linked to cancers of the mouth, throat, oesophagus, liver, colon, rectum, and breast. The healthcare payment implications — from oncology PBS spending on alcohol-attributable cancers to the MBS cost of treating alcohol-related liver disease — deserve quantification.
