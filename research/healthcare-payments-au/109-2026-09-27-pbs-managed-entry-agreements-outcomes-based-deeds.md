# PBS Managed Entry Agreements and Outcomes-Based Deeds: Australia's Hidden Price Negotiation Architecture

**One-line summary:** Australia's Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme uses a growing system of managed entry agreements and confidential deeds to list medicines that fail standard cost-effectiveness tests — but the evidence-generation promise underpinning the system rarely delivers, outcomes-based arrangements cover a tiny fraction of agreements, and the entire architecture is now being tested to breaking point by gene therapies and advanced therapy medicinal products.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 108 showed that Australia's PBAC has an implicit ICER threshold of approximately AU$50,000 per QALY that is incompatible with gene therapies, CAR-T cells, and many rare disease medicines — yet some of these medicines are being listed on the PBS. The mechanism enabling those listings is the managed entry agreement and deed of agreement system: Australia's primary tool for funding medicines that cannot pass conventional cost-effectiveness assessment.

---

## Background

Every PBS listing begins with a positive PBAC recommendation, and almost all positive recommendations now come with a price condition. For most new medicines, the "price condition" is a simple negotiation: the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing and the sponsoring pharmaceutical company agree on a schedule price, which is the publicly visible list price. The PBS pays that price on every dispensed prescription.

But for an increasingly large fraction of the PBS — particularly for oncology, rare diseases, and high-cost biologics — the published schedule price is not the real price. The actual price paid by the Commonwealth is lower, sometimes significantly lower, and is hidden inside a **deed of agreement**: a legally binding commercial contract between the Commonwealth and the pharmaceutical company that is confidential under commercial-in-confidence protections.

The deed of agreement system was formalised in 2003 but has older antecedents in the PBS pricing process. It sits at the heart of how Australia maintains a universal drug subsidy program while listing medicines that would otherwise cost the system far more than any conventional threshold could justify.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Architecture: Two Core Agreement Types

Beneath the umbrella term "deed of agreement," the PBS system uses two main instruments:

**Special Pricing Arrangements (SPAs):** The company and Commonwealth agree that the medicine will be listed at a published schedule price but that the effective price — what the Commonwealth actually pays — will be lower. The difference is reconciled through a confidential quarterly rebate paid by the company to the Department. A medicine listed at $500 per course in the schedule might cost the Commonwealth $300 per course in practice. Neither the rebate quantum nor the effective price is publicly disclosed. The SPA allows Australia to fund medicines at competitive rates without triggering international reference pricing mechanisms — if Australia's official price is $500, that price can be referenced by other countries' payers, even if the real net price is $300.

**Risk Sharing Arrangements (RSAs):** A more complex structure in which the financial terms of the listing are explicitly linked to performance metrics — typically patient utilisation, budget impact, or (in a minority of cases) patient outcomes. The Commonwealth's cost exposure changes depending on how the medicine performs against agreed benchmarks.

These instruments are typically packaged together and implemented through a single deed. The majority of deeds last for five years from PBS listing, at which point parties typically renegotiate terms or the medicine transitions to standard price disclosure arrangements.

### 2. Scale and Composition

Research examining all Australian PBS managed entry agreements from January 2012 to May 2016 found that one-third of all new medication-indication pairs (MIPs) recommended for PBS listing had a managed entry agreement. The PBAC recommended MEAs for 90% of the 87 MIPs examined, and 92% of those recommendations were ultimately implemented as legal deeds.

The composition of these agreements reveals a striking imbalance:

- **81% were simple financial agreements** — structured as discounts (32%) or utilisation/budget caps (43%)
- **Only 5% were outcomes-based** — linking payment to actual patient health outcomes
- **75% of medicines subject to managed entry agreements were antineoplastic (cancer) medicines**

The dominance of financial over outcomes-based arrangements reflects the practical difficulty of real-world evidence collection at the time — patient registries were fragmented, data linkage was poor, and the administrative burden on companies and the Department was high. By the mid-2020s, with improved digital health infrastructure (including My Health Record, the Active Script List, and enhanced cancer registries), the case for outcomes-based arrangements is stronger — but the proportion of deeds with genuine outcomes conditions remains modest.

By approximately 2024-25, the total number of active PBS deeds of agreement is estimated at over 300 — a substantial administrative estate requiring ongoing monitoring, renegotiation, and compliance management. The Department manages these through its PBS Medicines Governance function, but the ANAO's December 2024 audit of PBS administration found that Health has not established adequate horizon scanning capabilities to anticipate the future cost implications of new and novel medicines moving through the pipeline.

### 3. The Managed Entry Scheme: A Conditional Access Pathway

Distinct from the broader deed system is the formal **Managed Entry Scheme (MES)**, introduced in January 2011 following a memorandum of understanding with Medicines Australia. The MES allows the PBAC to make a positive recommendation at a price justified by the existing evidence, conditional on the company providing more conclusive evidence within a pre-specified timeframe — typically 2-3 years.

The MES applies when all three conditions are met:
1. The PBAC agrees there is a genuine clinical need for the medicine
2. The PBAC would not recommend listing at the proposed price based on current evidence of clinical effect
3. A randomised clinical trial (or equivalent high-quality evidence) is due to report within a reasonable timeframe

In theory, a medicine enters the PBS at a provisional price, generates the promised evidence, and either the price is confirmed, adjusted downward if the evidence is weaker than hoped, or the listing is terminated if the evidence is absent. In practice, the "price adjustment downward" outcome has occurred rarely. The system has primarily functioned as an earlier access mechanism rather than a true risk-sharing or evidence-generation device — the promised evidence collection either doesn't materialise with the rigour required for a price renegotiation, or the political and practical difficulty of de-listing a medicine after patients have accessed it makes enforcement impractical.

### 4. The Confidentiality Problem and Its Consequences

Australia's confidential rebate structure creates a fundamental transparency gap. The publicly reported PBS expenditure figures — approximately $19.1 billion in 2024-25 — reflect amounts dispensed at schedule prices, not amounts actually paid by the Commonwealth after rebates are collected. The true net PBS expenditure is therefore substantially lower, but the magnitude is not publicly disclosed.

This has several downstream consequences:

- **Parliamentary accountability is compromised**: Estimates committees and other oversight bodies cannot accurately assess what the Commonwealth pays for individual medicines or classes of medicines.
- **Budget projections are opaque**: The PBS is counted as fully demand-driven expenditure in the federal budget, but a significant component is constrained by deed-based utilisation caps — meaning the real fiscal exposure differs from the projected fiscal exposure.
- **Price signalling fails**: Patients, clinicians, and policymakers cannot assess whether a medicine offers value relative to its effective price because that price is hidden.
- **Reference pricing is gamed internationally**: Australia's artificially high published prices, reflecting true net prices adjusted upward, can complicate international price negotiations for other countries while protecting Australian access.

A 2026 discussion in the Medical Journal of Australia, prompted by the PBS listing of delgocitinib — a topical JAK inhibitor developed by Leo Pharma and originally discovered by Japan Tobacco — raised the broader transparency question: should medicines manufactured by companies with problematic industries be subject to heightened scrutiny of their PBS arrangements? The answer is contested, but the question highlighted how little public visibility exists into the deed system.

### 5. Gene Therapies and the Outcomes-Based Frontier

The most significant pressure on the deed system is coming from gene therapies and advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs). Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec), Australia's first PBS-listed gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy type I, was listed in May 2022 at approximately AU$2.5 million per treatment. This listing was possible only through a novel deed structure that incorporates genuine outcomes-based elements:

- A **pay-over-time option** allowing the approximately $2.5 million cost to be structured across up to five years
- An **outcomes-based rebate component** linking a portion of the total payment to demonstrated continued patient performance over the five-year period — drawing on patient registry and real-world outcomes data collected via the SMA patient registry
- A **one-time treatment architecture** that required fundamentally different deed design from the repeat-dispensing model that governs most PBS deeds

The Zolgensma deed is a proof of concept that outcomes-based arrangements for ultra-high-cost, one-time treatments can work within the PBS framework. But it also highlights the limitations: the deed negotiation took years, the outcomes metrics and rebate triggers remain confidential, and the evidence generated by the registry is not publicly available in a form that allows independent assessment of whether the medicine's real-world performance matches the modelled ICER used to justify listing.

### 6. The Reform Agenda: IAG and the 50 Recommendations

The HTA Policy and Methods Review, released in September 2024, included 50 recommendations addressing Australia's HTA system including managed entry and deed structures. The government established the **HTA Review Implementation Advisory Group (IAG)** in November 2024, chaired by the PBAC chair and comprising consumers, clinicians, and industry representatives, to guide implementation.

The IAG's Interim Report (September 2025) identified five areas of focus. Those most directly relevant to managed entry include:

- **Building capability to assess emerging technologies**: Specifically developing a systematic framework for assessing ATMPs — including gene therapies and CAR-T — that is not purely QALY-based and can accommodate the time-horizon, durability uncertainty, and single-administration characteristics of these medicines.
- **Reducing time to access through more efficient pathways**: Including streamlining deed negotiation timelines, which currently add months to the period between PBAC recommendation and PBS listing.
- **Improving transparency and stakeholder engagement**: The most contested recommendation area — industry opposes disclosure of rebate terms that would expose international pricing, while consumer advocates argue the current opacity prevents effective oversight.
- **Outcomes-based framework development**: The IAG is working on technical standards for evidence collection, registry design, and rebate trigger mechanisms that could allow the proportion of genuine outcomes-based deeds to increase from ~5% toward a more substantial share of the portfolio.

The **PBAC discount rate reform** — reducing the base-case discount rate from 5% to 3.5-4%, recommended by the PBAC itself and consistent with international practice — has been under consideration since the 2022 Strategic Agreement with Medicines Australia. Its implementation would materially improve the modelled cost-effectiveness of gene therapies and other medicines with long-horizon benefits, potentially reducing the reliance on deed workarounds for medicines that would pass a more appropriate threshold under an updated discount rate. As of September 2026, the reform has not been implemented.

The IAG's final roadmap was due in January 2026. Government actions announced for the 2026-27 federal budget include updates to PBAC guidelines on the application of the lowest-cost comparator — a change that may improve access for some medicines but does not address the structural ATMP problem.

### 7. International Comparison: How Other Systems Handle This

Australia is not alone in using managed entry agreements and confidential rebates — they are now the global norm for high-cost innovative medicines. Key differences include:

| Country | System | Outcomes-based proportion | Transparency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Australia** | PBS MEAs/Deeds | ~5–10% | Confidential |
| **Italy** | AIFA Managed Entry | ~30–40% | Registry-mandated, partially public |
| **UK** | NHS Commercial Framework, CDF | ~20–30% | Partial public disclosure |
| **US** | Various (no national system) | Emerging | Highly variable |
| **NZ** | PHARMAC | Minimal managed entry; fixed budget | No separate pathway |

Italy's AIFA (Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco) runs the world's most extensive outcomes-based managed access system, with mandatory patient registries for approximately 200 medicines and publicly available aggregate performance data. The AIFA model was instrumental in funding haemophilia B gene therapy Hemgenix — which MSAC rejected in Australia in 2024 — via a "payment by results" arrangement. Italy's willingness to publish aggregate registry outcomes gives both payers and researchers visibility into real-world performance that the Australian system cannot currently match.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Evidence generation that never triggers repricing.** The managed entry premise — fund now, confirm value later — rests on the threat of price reduction or de-listing if evidence disappoints. In practice, Australia has rarely used this lever. Once patients are accessing a medicine and clinicians are prescribing it, the political economy of enforcement shifts decisively toward the company. This may mean the system provides earlier access without genuine risk sharing.

**Confidentiality vs accountability.** The case for confidential pricing is real: Australian net prices below published schedule rates help protect other countries' access. But the case for some form of aggregate transparency — reporting total rebates collected by therapeutic category, or publishing outcomes-trigger outcomes without the specific price terms — is compelling and has not been met.

**The administrative estate.** Managing several hundred active deeds — each with different monitoring obligations, evidence commitments, utilisation caps, and renegotiation timelines — places a significant burden on the Department of Health. The ANAO's 2024 audit found gaps in the Department's capacity to proactively manage the PBS cost landscape, which has implications for deed compliance monitoring.

**Outcomes-based in practice.** For outcomes-based deeds to function, high-quality patient-level data must flow from clinicians and hospitals to a central repository in a timely and reliable way. My Health Record and disease registries have improved but not yet enabled the real-time outcomes monitoring that more sophisticated outcomes-based deeds would require.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **NZ PHARMAC and rare disease access**: PHARMAC operates without managed entry or outcomes-based deed infrastructure and has explicitly stated its fixed-budget model cannot accommodate gene therapies — the contrast with Australia's growing ATMP pathway is sharp and consequential for New Zealanders with rare diseases.
2. **Rare disease registries and data infrastructure**: The patient registry system underpinning Zolgensma's outcomes-based deed — and similar registries for CAR-T and other ATMPs — is a key thread; the adequacy, coverage, and governance of Australia's disease registry infrastructure determines whether outcomes-based PBS reimbursement can scale.
3. **PBAC compassionate/special access pathways**: For medicines in the pipeline that have not yet achieved PBS listing, the Therapeutic Goods Administration's Special Access Scheme and the PBAC's own managed access provisions offer limited pre-listing pathways; the gap between TGA approval and PBS listing (391 days on average) is particularly costly for patients with progressive rare diseases.
4. **Discount rate reform: 5% → 3.5-4%**: This single technical change to PBAC's guidelines would materially alter the cost-effectiveness modelling for gene therapies and other long-horizon ATMPs, potentially reducing the need for deed workarounds; the political and fiscal reasons for the implementation delay deserve scrutiny.
5. **PHARMAC/PBS alignment and trans-Tasman pharmaceutical purchasing**: Whether Australia and New Zealand could share a purchasing mechanism for gene therapies and ultra-rare disease medicines — reducing per-unit cost and spreading the administrative burden of outcomes-based deed management — is a policy option that has been discussed but not pursued.
