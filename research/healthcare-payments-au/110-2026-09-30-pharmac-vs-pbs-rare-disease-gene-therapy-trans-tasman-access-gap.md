# PHARMAC vs PBS: New Zealand's Rare Disease and Gene Therapy Access Gap — and What a Fixed Budget Cannot Buy

**One-line summary:** New Zealand's PHARMAC, a globally admired single-buyer model, has been left structurally exposed by the era of gene therapies, CAR-T cells, and rare disease medicines — funding fewer modern medicines per capita than Australia by a factor of nearly three, with no viable pathway for one-time million-dollar treatments that Australia has already listed.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 109 examined Australia's managed entry agreement and deed-of-agreement architecture — the mechanism that allows the PBS to list gene therapies and high-cost rare disease medicines that cannot pass standard cost-effectiveness assessment. That architecture, however imperfect and opaque, does not exist in PHARMAC's framework. New Zealand's pharmaceutical agency, operating on a fixed Combined Pharmaceutical Budget and a contestable fund model, has been confronting the same gene therapy and rare disease pipeline while carrying far less fiscal headroom and no comparable payment innovation.

---

## Background

PHARMAC — the Pharmaceutical Management Agency — is New Zealand's government-owned pharmaceutical purchasing agency, established in 1993. It manages the Combined Pharmaceutical Budget (CPB), a fixed annual sum set by the government, and decides which medicines to fund within that budget. Unlike Australia's PBS, which is formally demand-driven (the Commonwealth pays for every dispensed script regardless of volume), PHARMAC operates a hard budget: if a medicine is funded, spending is controlled through a mix of price negotiation, utilisation restrictions, and supply agreements. If the CPB is exhausted, new medicines queue for future funding cycles.

This architecture produced genuine healthcare savings for New Zealand — PHARMAC is estimated to save NZ$1 billion or more per year compared with a scenario in which New Zealand paid average international medicine prices — and became a global reference model for pharmaceutical purchasing. In the era of small-molecule medicines with predictable costs, it worked well. The era of gene therapies, CAR-T cells, and ultra-rare disease biologics is testing it in ways the model was not designed for.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Budget: NZ$1.76 Billion for a Population of 5.1 Million

PHARMAC's Combined Pharmaceutical Budget for 2025–26 is NZ$1,760 million (approximately AU$1.63 billion), covering medicines administered in public hospitals, community pharmacy dispensing, vaccines, and some medical devices. For 2026–27 it rises to NZ$1,806 million — a 3% increase.

The per-capita comparison with Australia is stark:

- New Zealand spends approximately 4.9% of its total health budget on medicines
- Australia spends approximately 12.2%
- The OECD average is approximately 13.3%

Australia's PBS expenditure for 2024–25 was approximately AU$19.1 billion — for a population approximately 5.4 times New Zealand's. Even adjusting for population, Australia spends roughly twice as much per capita on publicly funded medicines as New Zealand.

### 2. The Access Gap: 143 Modern Medicines vs 51

The most definitive comparative analysis is the IQVIA *Access to Medicines 3* (AtoM 3) report, which found that between 2011 and 2020, Australia publicly funded approximately **143 modern medicines** while New Zealand funded approximately **51** — fewer than one-third of Australia's total. New Zealand ranked last among 20 OECD nations benchmarked.

On reimbursement timelines, the IQVIA data showed New Zealand taking an average of **512 days** from regulatory approval to public funding, compared with the 20-country OECD average of **233 days**. Australia's own timeline (~466–500 days) is also poor by OECD standards, but New Zealand's is more than twice as slow as the OECD average.

A trans-Tasman specific analysis by PHARMAC's own commissioned "Mind the Gap" research found that 89 cancer medicines are funded in both countries, 35 are funded in Australia but not New Zealand, and 13 are available in New Zealand but not Australia. PHARMAC's conclusion — that only 3 of the 35 Australian-only medicines offer "meaningful benefit" — has been disputed by oncologists and patient groups, who argue the methodology understates the real clinical value. A 2024 report by Te Aho o Te Kahu (New Zealand's Cancer Control Agency) found 24 cancer medications available in Australia but not New Zealand, including 12 with notable clinical benefits.

### 3. The June 2024 Budget Uplift: A Turning Point — Partially

In June 2024, the New Zealand government announced an unprecedented NZ$604 million uplift to PHARMAC's Combined Pharmaceutical Budget over four years — the largest single funding increase in PHARMAC's history. The announcement was politically significant: it reflected both genuine patient advocacy pressure and an acknowledgement that the access gap had become unsustainable.

PHARMAC moved quickly. Within the first year of the uplift, it funded or expanded access to **66 medicines** — 33 for cancer and 33 for other conditions — against an original internal estimate of 54. Approximately 250,000 New Zealanders were expected to benefit in the first year.

Notable medicines funded through the uplift include:
- Expanded access to **Trikafta** (elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor) and funding of **Alyftrek** for cystic fibrosis, effective 1 April 2026 — both were funded in Australia substantially earlier
- **Brentuximab vedotin** (Adcetris) as first-line treatment for systemic anaplastic large cell lymphoma, from 1 April 2026
- Several immunotherapy and targeted therapy agents in oncology

However, the uplift does not fundamentally change PHARMAC's structural constraints: it is a one-time catch-up, not a change to the funding model. Gene therapies, CAR-T products, and ultra-rare disease medicines remain outside the CPB architecture's practical reach.

### 4. Gene Therapies: A Model That Does Not Fit

Australia listed **Zolgensma** (onasemnogene abeparvovec) for spinal muscular atrophy type I on the PBS in May 2022 at approximately AU$2.5 million, enabled by a deed of agreement with outcomes-based elements including a pay-over-time structure and a registry-linked rebate (see Report 109).

New Zealand does not have Zolgensma on the Schedule. PHARMAC instead funded **nusinersen (Spinraza)** from 1 January 2023 — the first publicly funded SMA treatment in New Zealand, and it took until 2023 because an earlier 2019 expert recommendation was deferred pending longer-term clinical trial data. Nusinersen requires repeat dosing (four doses in year one, then three doses per year indefinitely) at an approximate cost of NZ$150,000–$200,000 per patient per year, compared with Zolgensma's single administration.

The structural reason NZ cannot easily replicate Australia's Zolgensma deed is that PHARMAC's fixed-budget model has no mechanism for pay-over-time structures that amortise a multi-million dollar single payment across future budget years. The CPB is appropriated annually; multi-year outcomes-based commitments require a different fiscal architecture. PHARMAC has no equivalent of the PBS deed-of-agreement framework's annuity or instalment payment provisions.

A broader principle follows: the gene therapy pipeline — with products targeting Gaucher disease, MPS, haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and beyond — is structurally incompatible with PHARMAC's current model. Australia's PBS, through its deed system, has at least a contested and imperfect mechanism for these products. New Zealand currently has none.

### 5. CAR-T: Not Yet

As of mid-2026, no CAR-T therapy product is funded through PHARMAC or delivered routinely in New Zealand's public health system. In Australia, four CAR-T products (axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel, and idecabtagene vicleucel) are funded under the Highly Specialised Therapies scheme and delivered at approximately 11 sites concentrated in Victoria, New South Wales, and Queensland (see Report 107).

The Malaghan Institute of Medical Research — New Zealand's lead cellular immunotherapy research body — has been running the ENABLE-2 CAR-T clinical trial and is preparing regulatory submissions to Medsafe, engaging PHARMAC on funding pathways and Health New Zealand on delivery infrastructure. The Malaghan's stated ambition, as of May 2026, is to have a domestically developed CAR-T therapy available through the public health system by **2027**, subject to results, regulatory approvals, and PHARMAC funding.

The New Zealand pathway differs from Australia in one potentially important respect: Malaghan is pursuing a **locally manufactured** CAR-T product rather than relying on the global supply chains of the major commercial manufacturers (Kite/Gilead, Novartis, BMS, J&J). A local manufacturing capability would reduce vector manufacturing lead times (currently 3–6 weeks for commercial products, which creates clinical urgency problems in rapidly progressing haematological malignancies), potentially reduce costs, and build domestic research and manufacturing infrastructure. Whether PHARMAC can fund such a product at a viable price remains the central unanswered question.

### 6. Rare Disease Policy: The $5 Million Fund and the Definition Change

Until November 2025, PHARMAC defined a rare disorder as a condition affecting fewer than 1 in 50,000 people — a narrow definition substantially more restrictive than the European standard (1 in 2,000) and the definition adopted by New Zealand's own Ministry of Health in the 2024 *Aotearoa New Zealand Rare Disorders Strategy*.

From **1 November 2025**, PHARMAC aligned its operational definition with the Strategy, broadening the threshold to **1 in 2,000**. This change is expected to significantly expand the pool of conditions and medicines that can be assessed through the rare disorder funding pathway.

However, the amount available through the formal rare disorder contestable fund remains approximately **NZ$5 million per year** — a figure widely regarded by advocates as insufficient. For context, a single patient receiving enzyme replacement therapy for mucopolysaccharidosis type IVA may cost NZ$1–2 million per year. The $5 million annual envelope can support, at most, a handful of ultra-rare disease patients on high-cost ERT. New Zealand has 42 medicines on the Pharmaceutical Schedule that were previously assessed via the Named Patient Pharmaceutical Assessment (NPPA) pathway for individual rare disease patients; many others remain individually funded (or unfunded) case-by-case, with clinicians applying to PHARMAC's legacy NPPA mechanism.

### 7. The 2022 PHARMAC Review and Its Aftermath

In 2022, the New Zealand government commissioned an independent review of PHARMAC — its first since the agency was established. The review panel found systemic gaps in PHARMAC's approach to equity, engagement with patients and clinicians, and decision-making transparency, and made a series of recommendations. PHARMAC responded with 30 commitments across five themes including decision-making reform, te Tiriti o Waitangi obligations, equity, and engagement.

One key review recommendation was that the *rare disorders* definition be updated — the 1 in 2,000 threshold change implemented in November 2025 directly follows from this. Another recommendation was increased transparency in PHARMAC's decision-making, including more detailed publication of funding criteria and declined application reasoning.

The review did not recommend abolishing or replacing PHARMAC's single-buyer model, but it did acknowledge that the CPB's fixed nature creates a structural tension with the accelerating pace and cost of novel medicine development. This tension has deepened materially since 2022.

### 8. The Trans-Tasman Non-Alignment Problem

Despite the geographic, linguistic, and institutional proximity of Australia and New Zealand, there is no systematic trans-Tasman pharmaceutical purchasing alignment. The two countries maintain:

- **Separate regulatory systems**: Australia's TGA and New Zealand's Medsafe operate independently, with mutual recognition of some good manufacturing practice audits but separate registration pathways. A 2003–2011 effort to establish a joint Australia New Zealand Therapeutic Products Agency (ANZTPA) was abandoned. Products approved by TGA require separate Medsafe registration before PHARMAC can fund them, adding delay.

- **Separate HTA bodies**: Australia's PBAC conducts cost-effectiveness assessment independently of PHARMAC's assessment processes. There is no systematic sharing of clinical or economic assessment data, though both agencies participate in some international HTA collaboration networks.

- **No joint pricing negotiations**: Australia's PBS negotiates prices with pharmaceutical companies through the PBAC/NEPM process; PHARMAC negotiates entirely separately. New Zealand's smaller population (~5.1 million vs Australia's ~26.7 million) fundamentally weakens its negotiating leverage.

- **Mismatched registration dates**: Because TGA and Medsafe assess independently and often at different times, products approved in Australia may not be registered in New Zealand for months or years, creating an administrative barrier even before PHARMAC's funding assessment begins.

Australia's Medicines Australia industry peak body has explicitly advocated against any move to import the PHARMAC model into Australia's PBS, arguing that PHARMAC's access outcomes demonstrate its structural limitations. New Zealand patient groups have, for different reasons, advocated strongly for greater trans-Tasman alignment — primarily to give PHARMAC access to Australia's PBS price negotiations and to allow faster adoption of PBS-listed medicines.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Can PHARMAC adapt its model to fund gene therapies?** The fixed CPB architecture is structurally incompatible with multi-million-dollar one-time payments unless the government agrees to fund these products outside the CPB or creates a separate ATMP fund — as France (ATU/AAP) and Italy (AIFA managed entry) have done. There is no such fund in New Zealand, and the 2024 budget uplift, while welcome, did not create one.

**Is the $604 million uplift a bridge or a fix?** PHARMAC's 66-medicine response to the uplift demonstrates that the access gap was primarily driven by underfunding, not structural failure. But the medicines now being listed are primarily standard-of-care oncology and chronic disease treatments from the previous decade's pipeline — the products expected to reach PHARMAC's queue in the late 2020s include gene therapies, ADCs, bispecific antibodies, and next-generation CAR-T products at costs that may individually exceed the entire rare disorder fund.

**Would a trans-Tasman PBAC/PHARMAC joint HTA process help?** In principle, sharing cost-effectiveness assessment work between the two agencies (while maintaining separate funding decisions) would reduce duplication and allow faster assessment of smaller-market rare disease products. The IAG reform process in Australia has not included trans-Tasman collaboration as a priority, and New Zealand's Ministry of Health has not publicly committed to any formal bilateral framework.

**What does PHARMAC's experience imply for Australia's PBS?** PHARMAC is, in some respects, a preview of what Australia's PBS would face if its budget were fixed rather than demand-driven. The structural problems PHARMAC faces with gene therapies — no mechanism for multi-year amortisation, no outcomes-based deal architecture, no separate ATMP fund — are problems Australia has partially (imperfectly) solved through its deed system. They are problems that would immediately confront the PBS if a government ever attempted to introduce a hard budget cap.
