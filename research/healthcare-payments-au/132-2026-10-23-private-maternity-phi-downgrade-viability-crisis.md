# Private Maternity's Slow Exit: PHI Policy Downgrades, Closure Cascade, and the Public Hospital Overhang

**One-line summary:** Australia's private maternity sector is shrinking under the compound pressure of Gold-tier premium inflation that has driven hundreds of thousands of members to downgrade to policies that exclude pregnancy cover, with at least 18 private maternity units closing since 2018 and credible projections that the sector could disappear entirely by the end of the decade — creating an uncosted contingent liability for already-strained public hospitals.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 131 identified the postnatal care payment black hole as a product of fragmented MBS architecture and inequitable access between public and private settings — but it largely assumed a functioning private maternity sector continued to exist. This report examines the structural forces dismantling that sector.

---

## Background

Australia's private health insurance framework divides hospital cover into four tiers — Basic, Bronze, Silver, and Gold — under a legislative product standardisation that took effect in April 2019. The tier structure was designed to make PHI more legible for consumers: each tier carries a mandatory minimum set of clinical categories.

Pregnancy and birth — meaning private obstetric care as a private patient in a private or public hospital — sits exclusively in Gold. It cannot be included in Basic, Bronze, or standard Silver policies. Funds may include it in "Silver Plus" products, but this is at insurer discretion and inconsistently offered. A consumer who holds a Basic, Bronze, or standard Silver policy and becomes pregnant has no PHI entitlement to private obstetric care, regardless of how long they have held that policy.

The 12-month waiting period for pregnancy and birth cover applies on first take-up or on upgrading to a policy that includes it. A woman who discovers she is pregnant and attempts to upgrade to Gold at that point cannot use the cover for that pregnancy.

This architecture, combined with a sharp divergence in premium inflation between Gold and lower tiers, has produced a structural misalignment: the policy tier needed to access private maternity care is also the tier that has become the fastest-growing and most unaffordable segment of the market.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Gold Premium Inflation: The Structural Driver

CHOICE analysis published in March 2026 found that Gold hospital cover has increased in price by approximately **71% over the past six years** — more than three times the approved average premium increase over the same period (~21%). The mechanism is partly mathematical: as cost-conscious consumers (typically younger and healthier) downgrade to lower tiers, Gold's risk pool concentrates higher-claiming members, which drives further premium increases and accelerates the adverse selection spiral.

The 2026 premium round — an industry-weighted average increase of **4.41%** approved by the Minister — masked highly differentiated increases at the tier level. CHOICE found that across the five largest health funds, Gold-level hospital policies increased by an average of **13.3%** in 2026, while Basic, Bronze, and Silver policies increased by an average of only **2.6% to 3.3%**. The single largest increase identified was HCF's Hospital Optimal Gold cover, which rose by **25%** in 2026.

Private Healthcare Australia (the PHI industry body) acknowledged in its 2026 commentary that Gold cover is "now mostly bought by those most likely to make a claim" — a candid admission that the adverse selection spiral is entrenched.

In parallel, hospital and medical inflation ran at **6.7% in 2024** (APRA data), approximately double the national consumer price inflation rate over the same period. The gap between PHI premium growth and hospital cost growth has compressed insurer margins and — critically for this analysis — reduced the rebate rates that private hospitals receive from funds for maternity admissions.

### 2. The Downgrade Cascade: 360,000 Fewer Gold Members

An ABC investigation reported in February 2026 that approximately **360,000 people have left Gold-tier policies since 2020**. The cost differential between Gold and lower tiers is acute: industry comparison data suggests an average single-person Gold policy runs at roughly $200–$300 per month depending on the fund and excess level, compared with approximately **$88–$106 per month** for Basic or Bronze hospital cover.

For a two-income household in their late 20s or early 30s — the demographic most likely to be planning a family — this premium gap of $100–$200 per month creates a significant incentive to downgrade, particularly given cost-of-living pressure. The problem is that many consumers downgrade before pregnancy is planned, triggering the waiting period trap: they cannot re-upgrade and have the cover apply in time.

One structural gap the AMA has identified: there is no mechanism in the current PHI framework for a woman who discovers an unplanned pregnancy to access maternity cover in a compressed timeframe. The **12-month waiting period is fixed by legislation** and cannot be waived at insurer discretion for new or unexpected pregnancies.

### 3. The Closure Cascade: At Least 18 Units Closed Since 2018

The financial consequences of declining private birth volumes are visible in the pattern of maternity unit closures:

- **At least 18 private maternity units** have closed across Australia since 2018, according to industry commentary. In **2024 alone, five more units closed**.
- The most high-profile 2025 closures involved Healthscope — Australia's second-largest private hospital operator — shutting maternity services at two capital-city facilities:
  - **Darwin Private Hospital**: Closed June 2025. Healthscope cited a near-halving of private births over a decade — from approximately **700 births in 2013 to fewer than 300 in 2024** — combined with inability to recruit qualified midwives. NT Health confirmed that women who were private patients have been redirected to Royal Darwin Hospital, which does not have private room accommodation.
  - **Hobart Private Hospital**: Closed August 2025. The hospital had conducted a national recruitment campaign for 12 months without successfully attracting a single new qualified midwife. It oversaw approximately **510 births in 2024** across seven birthing suites before closure.

The Healthscope closures drew sharp reactions from peak bodies. The AMA described them as demonstrating that "maternity services are like the canary in the coalmine" for private hospital viability. Catholic Health Australia criticised the closures as evidence of "for-profit healthcare providers cherry-picking the work that suits the bottom line," arguing that maternity care — high-volume but low-margin — is the first service class to be rationalised when private hospital economics deteriorate.

### 4. The Structural Decline of Private Births

The population-level trend underpinning unit closures is well-documented in AIHW data:

- In 2023, **75% of hospital births were in public facilities**; approximately 25% were in private hospitals or private birth suites. This already reflects a sector that, in a population of 310,000 annual births, accounts for roughly 77,000–80,000 private births per year.
- Over the decade from **2012 to 2023**, the number of babies born in private hospitals fell by approximately **22%**, according to an MJA opinion piece published in July 2024.
- Modelling published in that MJA piece — co-authored by researchers who described it as a projection rather than a finding — concluded that on current trends, **private maternity hospitals could cease to exist by the end of this decade**.

Australia's birth rate context compounds this: the total fertility rate reached a historic low in 2023 (below 1.6), meaning fewer total births are available to the sector even before accounting for the PHI-driven shift from private to public settings.

### 5. The Insurer–Hospital Funding Squeeze

The financial viability equation in private maternity involves three parties: the PHI fund, the hospital (covering accommodation, theatre, midwifery, and allied health), and the medical specialists (the obstetrician, anaesthetist, and any paediatrician). The current framework creates structural underpayment at each level.

**For hospitals:** The rates that health funds pay private hospitals for obstetric admissions are negotiated bilaterally under contracts — a system the Australian Private Hospitals Association (APHA) has repeatedly criticised as allowing funds to "underpay hospitals for treatment and services." There is no floor rate set by regulation. Maternity is described as one of the highest-cost admission categories for private hospitals (requiring continuous midwifery staffing, theatre availability for emergency caesarean at any hour, and neonatal resuscitation capability) and one of the lowest-margin, because insurer rebate rates have not kept pace with these costs.

**For specialists:** The AMA's January 2026 Position Statement on key principles for private maternity funding identified that:
- MBS rebates for **antenatal consultations** are inadequate relative to the time, skill, and risk involved
- MBS rebates for **maternity imaging** (obstetric ultrasound) are below the cost of providing the service in the private sector
- **Paediatric and neonatal hospital attendances** for babies born in private facilities — where a paediatrician must be on call or in attendance — have either no MBS rebate or inadequate rebates for the private hospital context
- Rebates have not been **annually indexed** to the cost of maternity care, creating a cumulative shortfall over time

RANZCOG corroborated the AMA's position in its budget submissions, noting that health funds "often underpay private hospitals for obstetric and gynaecological services."

### 6. The 2026 Private Health Reform Consultation

The Department of Health, Disability and Ageing released **Private Health Sector Reform Consultation Paper 1** on 2 July 2026, explicitly naming maternity and mental health as the two service areas facing the most acute viability pressure. The paper — the first tranche of a broader private health sector reform process — sought stakeholder views on proposed reforms to address these gaps.

Submissions closed in August 2026 (with some extensions). Key stakeholder positions:

- **Catholic Health Australia (CHA):** Welcomed the paper as spanning maternity, mental health, and regional access; called for "swift implementation" of reform. Previously described the closure trend as evidence of profit-driven healthcare decision-making and called for a regulated access requirement for maternity services at private hospitals.
- **Australian Private Hospitals Association (APHA):** Described the reforms as "positive if right" but warned that the proposals do not address the fundamental contracting dynamic that allows health funds to underpay hospitals.
- **Australian Medical Association (AMA):** Supported the direction but stated in its September 2026 response that the paper "must address funding pressures" as a precondition of meaningful reform. The AMA was "disappointed" that a specific proposal on psychiatric inpatient rebates — a parallel crisis to maternity viability — was omitted.
- **Private Healthcare Australia (PHI industry body):** Said the paper "recognised many challenges the sector has been highlighting, including growing pressure on Gold hospital cover," and called for reforms to "keep health insurance affordable."

As of October 2026, the consultation outcome has not been published and the proposals do not represent government policy.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Who bears the transition cost if private maternity disappears?** The 77,000–80,000 births annually in private facilities do not disappear if the private sector contracts — they shift to public hospitals. Public maternity services in most states are already at or near capacity. The costing of this contingent transfer has not been done publicly; it represents a substantial uncosted risk in the federal–state health funding architecture.

2. **Can the tier structure be fixed without destabilising the whole PHI market?** Lowering the tier threshold for maternity cover — or reducing the waiting period for pregnancy — would improve access but potentially disrupt the risk-pooling logic of the current tier structure. Funds and their actuaries resist changes that could make Gold (already in adverse selection) uninsurable. The reform design question is whether maternity cover can be made accessible at lower tiers without triggering further Gold exodus.

3. **Does the "canary" metaphor understate the problem?** The AMA has used maternity closures as a proxy signal for the financial state of private hospitals generally. But maternity is arguably more fragile than other specialties because: (a) it requires 24-hour obstetric and anaesthetic cover even for low-birth-volume units; (b) the MBS rebate structure is peculiarly inadequate for the private context; (c) the workforce (midwifery, obstetric anaesthesia) is in structural shortage. The closure dynamic in maternity may not be a leading indicator for the rest of the private hospital sector; it may be specific to maternity.

4. **What happens to private obstetricians?** An obstetric practice is not viable without hospital access. If private hospitals lose critical mass in maternity, obstetricians in those facilities cannot maintain their practice — creating a secondary workforce dislocation. RANZCOG has not yet quantified this effect, but the Darwin closure is already working through this logic: obstetricians previously practising at Darwin Private Hospital are now negotiating access arrangements to deliver their patients at public facilities, disrupting their practice economics.

5. **Is the 2026 reform process moving fast enough?** The first consultation paper was released in July 2026; submissions closed in August; a government response has not been published. Industry commentary suggests a reform timetable of 2027–28 for legislative or regulatory change. Units are closing now.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHI contracting reform: what a regulated minimum for hospital-insurer agreements would look like** — the "contracting abuses" identified by APHA sit at the intersection of competition law, contract law, and PHI regulation; what the reform options are.

2. **Obstetric anaesthesia workforce: the invisible bottleneck in private and rural maternity** — anaesthetic cover is a prerequisite for safe obstetrics, yet anaesthetic workforce shortages are now cited alongside midwifery shortages in private unit closures.

3. **Private Health Insurance Ombudsman: how the complaints system works and what PHI downgrade disputes reveal about policy design** — the PHIO receives tens of thousands of complaints annually; what the maternity-related complaint data reveals.

4. **State government responses to private maternity closure: whether public hospitals are resourced to absorb the transition** — how state health departments have responded to Darwin, Hobart, and other closures, and whether public hospital funding has followed the patient shift.

5. **Global comparisons: how other countries with mixed public-private maternity systems manage the PHI viability problem** — the UK (NHS + private insurance), Germany (statutory + private insurance), and France (public tariff-setting) each handle this differently; what Australia could learn.
