# Medical Indemnity, Birth Injury Liability, and Australia's No-Fault Gap: How the Long Tail Drives Obstetric Costs, Unit Closures, and the Case for Scheme Reform

**One-line summary:** Australia's fault-based medical negligence system produces multi-million-dollar birth injury settlements with liability tails lasting two or more decades — a structural driver of obstetrician premiums reaching $65,000–$100,000+/year that flows directly into gap fees, private unit closures, and an inequitable access system the National Injury Insurance Scheme was designed to fix but has not yet delivered.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Yesterday's report on maternity payment architecture identified high medical indemnity premiums as one of the three structural forces (alongside outdated MBS rebates and PHI risk equalisation exclusion) that are making private obstetrics financially unsustainable; today's report examines that liability system in detail — how birth injury claims work, what the government pays to manage them, and why a no-fault alternative remains unrealised.

---

## Background

Of all the liability pressures bearing on the Australian healthcare system, few are as structurally intractable as birth injury. A clinically avoidable injury during labour and delivery — oxygen deprivation producing hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), a shoulder dystocia resulting in brachial plexus injury, a delayed emergency caesarean leading to cerebral palsy — can generate a claim worth millions of dollars, lodged not at birth but anywhere from the child turning seven to the child turning eighteen, or even later, depending on the state's limitation period. The practitioner who attended that birth may be retired. Their practice may be dissolved. Their insurer may have changed. Yet the liability follows them through a mandatory run-off cover framework.

This long-tail structure makes birth injury claims unlike almost any other liability category in Australian medicine. And it makes obstetrics unlike almost any other specialty in terms of how medical indemnity insurance is priced, what government support is required to keep the private sector viable, and why the case for a no-fault alternative — long argued by RANZCOG, MJA commentators, and now effectively stranded in Treasury's 2019 discussion paper — matters in concrete dollar terms.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Long-Tail Mechanism: HIE, CP, and Eighteen-Year Limitation Pauses

Birth injury claims arise most commonly from:

- **Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE):** Oxygen deprivation during labour or delivery, typically from placental abruption, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, or failure to respond to foetal heart rate decelerations in time. HIE is the most common causative pathway for birth-related brain injury, and the most litigated.
- **Cerebral palsy:** The endpoint of severe perinatal brain injury — a permanent disorder of movement and posture. Australia has approximately 34,000 people living with cerebral palsy at any time (Cerebral Palsy Alliance), with around 700 new cases per year. A minority — estimates typically range from 10–15% — are potentially attributable to care events during the intrapartum period, though causation is scientifically disputed.
- **Brachial plexus and Erb's palsy:** Arising from shoulder dystocia management, especially excessive traction.
- **Hyperbilirubinaemia and kernicterus:** Failure to recognise and treat severe neonatal jaundice.

The critical feature of these injuries from a liability perspective is the **limitation period structure.** In most Australian states and territories, a minor's right of action is protected until they reach the age of 18 — at which point a new limitation period begins to run. In New South Wales (Limitation Act 1969, s.11A), for example, a person injured as a child has three years from turning 18 (or from discovering the cause of action) to commence proceedings; there is a 12-year long-stop from the date of the act or omission in some jurisdictions but not all. The practical effect: a birth injury can generate litigation up to 21 years after the delivery.

This is the "liability tail" that dominates obstetrician indemnity pricing. A claim arising in 2026 from a birth in 2005 is not unusual. An injury in 2026 that results in litigation in 2044 is foreseeable. No other common surgical specialty — not cardiac surgery, not neurosurgery — generates an equivalent forward liability from a single procedure on a healthy patient.

### 2. Claim Values: The Multi-Million-Dollar Settlement Range

Australia does not maintain a public registry of medical negligence settlement amounts. Available data comes from court awards, law firm case summaries, and actuarial industry reports.

Individual cases in the public record illustrate the upper range:

- **Adelaide, 2021:** A 15-year-old boy awarded $9.5 million following oxygen deprivation for 27 minutes immediately after birth. The amount was agreed rather than court-ordered, and was intended to cover lifetime 24-hour care costs. (InDaily, 2021)
- **Sydney, 2019:** A $9 million settlement at mediation for a claim arising from failure to perform timely caesarean section after two bradycardia events during labour; penicillin was administered during the birth, and the claim was that this masked signs warranting earlier intervention. (Gerard Malouf & Partners, 2019)
- **Queensland (undated by court, from reporting ~2006):** A 10-year-old received $5.39 million including $2.9 million for future care needs — an older award that provides a lower bound.

Secondary sources from legal marketing platforms (not authoritative data) report a "typical" band for severe cerebral palsy settlements in Australia of roughly **$5–$10 million** for the most severe cases, primarily driven by:
- Future care costs: attendant care 24 hours/day for a lifetime
- Modified accommodation requirements
- Future medical costs (spasticity management, orthopaedic surgery, assistive technology)
- Economic loss to the child (earnings foregone)
- Pain and suffering/general damages, which in New South Wales are capped at approximately $729,500 (2025-26 indexation under the Civil Liability Act 2002) but remain uncapped in some jurisdictions

**Key point:** There is no cap on future care or economic loss components. A 2026 birth resulting in severe HIE can generate a settlement lifetime cost of $10–$15 million including future care, spread over 70+ years of the claimant's expected life.

### 3. The Indemnity Cost Structure and Government Subsidies

Australia's medical indemnity sector is dominated by four providers: **Avant** (approximately 55% market share), **MDA National**, **MIGA**, and **MIPS**. These are either mutual defence organisations (MDOs) or insurance companies regulated under APRA.

**Obstetric premiums** are the highest in Australian medicine. Secondary sources from medical profession calculators, individual obstetrician fee schedules, and profession advocacy materials consistently quote:

- Full-scope private obstetrician premiums: **$65,000–$100,000+ per year** (with some sources citing higher for high-volume practices or those with prior claim history)
- GP with obstetrics (delivering babies): significantly lower but still substantially elevated vs. standard GP indemnity (~$15,000–$40,000/year in some estimates, depending on volume)

These premiums reflect the combination of long-tail exposure, high claim values, and the difficulty of pricing a liability that may not crystallise for two decades.

**Government schemes** exist specifically because the liability tail would otherwise make private obstetric practice uninsurable or excessively expensive:

**a. Premium Support Scheme (PSS)**
Introduced post-2002 crisis. Subsidises premiums for practitioners whose Gross Medical Indemnity Cost (GMIC) exceeds 7.5% of gross private billings. Administered by MDA National on behalf of the Commonwealth under the *Medical Indemnity Regulations 2020*. Eligible obstetricians — whose indemnity costs are very likely to exceed the 7.5% threshold — receive a Commonwealth subsidy on their annual premium. Without this subsidy, full-scope obstetric practice in the private sector would be economically marginal for all but the highest-billing practitioners.

**b. High Cost Claims Scheme (HCCS)**
Reimburses insurers/MDOs for **50% of the amount by which an indemnity payment exceeds the HCCS threshold** — currently $500,000 for incidents notified from 1 July 2018. This means when a birth injury settlement reaches $9.5 million, the insurer pays the first $500,000 fully, then pays 50% of the remaining $9 million (i.e., $4.5 million), while the Commonwealth covers the other $4.5 million. This makes the largest birth injury claims partially Commonwealth-funded by design. The HCCS was replaced by the **Allied Health High Cost Claims Scheme (AHHCCS)** for allied health (including midwives) from 1 July 2020, with the same $500,000 threshold.

**c. Run-Off Cover Scheme (ROCS)**
Perhaps the most structurally significant government intervention. When a medical practitioner **ceases practice** — retires, becomes permanently incapacitated, dies — their claims-made insurance expires. Without ROCS, a retired obstetrician would have no cover when a claim arrived 15 years after the birth. ROCS provides Commonwealth-backed run-off cover for eligible practitioners who have ceased practice. The Australian Government Actuary publishes annual reports; the **21st Report on the ROCS for medical indemnity (2024-25)** was published July 2026, but its content was not accessible to confirm current claim volumes or payments.

**d. Midwife Professional Indemnity Scheme (MPIS)**
From **1 July 2025**, an expanded MPIS now covers **100% of eligible claims** against endorsed midwives providing intrapartum care outside hospitals (home births, birth centres). MIGA is currently the sole approved provider. This represents a shift from the previous arrangement where MPIS covered 80% of claims above threshold. The reform was enabled by $56.5 million in 2024-25 Budget funding (the same package that removed the collaborative arrangement requirement for participating midwives).

### 4. The NIIS Gap: Medical Injury Coverage Promised but Not Delivered

The **National Injury Insurance Scheme (NIIS)** was proposed by the Productivity Commission in its landmark 2011 *Disability Care and Support* report. The Commission recommended a federated system of state-based no-fault catastrophic injury schemes covering four categories: motor vehicle accidents, workplace accidents, medical treatment injuries, and general accidents.

In 2026, the NIIS's actual implementation looks like this:

**What exists:**
- **Queensland** is the only state with an operational NIIS scheme (NIISQ). It covers catastrophic injuries from motor vehicle accidents — permanent spinal cord injuries, traumatic brain injuries, severe burns, multiple/high-level limb amputations, brachial plexus injuries, and permanent blindness. NIISQ published its **2025-26 Annual Report** in September 2026, marking the scheme's 10th anniversary. Motor vehicle is the only operational coverage area.
- Most other states have motor-vehicle-only catastrophic injury schemes (WorkCover equivalents cover workplace injuries separately) but have not implemented NIIS-aligned coverage.

**What does not exist:**
- **Medical treatment injury coverage** — the category that would capture birth injuries — has not been implemented anywhere in Australia. Treasury's most recent policy document is the 2019 *Medical Treatment Injury Discussion Paper*, which identified design challenges including: defining "medical injury" vs. "inherent risk"; setting eligibility thresholds for catastrophic vs. severe injury; navigating Commonwealth/state funding boundaries; and determining whether patients retain a common-law right to sue.
- **Birth injury specifically** falls into a particular classification limbo. A 2011 paper by MacLennan in the *Australian and New Zealand Journal of Obstetrics and Gynaecology* argued for a dedicated no-fault cerebral palsy pension scheme, noting that even under a no-fault framework, practitioners remain exposed to common-law damages claims for large heads of damage like lifetime economic loss. A 2012 MJA commentary noted that the NIIS design for medical injuries was "mostly ill-defined" and that cerebral palsy was being provisionally classed as "non-avoidable" — meaning it might not qualify for no-fault support.
- A Queensland submission to Senate estimates proposed that congenital cerebral palsy linked to pregnancy or birth be **excluded from NIIS** and handled through the NDIS instead — recognising that the NDIS already funds disability support for people with CP but not through a compensation model.

**The result:** Birth-injured children in Australia have two pathways. If they can prove negligence — that a clinician's breach of duty of care caused the injury — they can receive very large tort damages. If they cannot prove negligence (because the injury was unforeseeable, or evidence is unclear, or limitations have expired), they receive NDIS support if they meet the disability threshold, which provides lifelong care but no economic loss compensation, no lifetime damages, and no recognition of fault or systemic accountability.

### 5. The New Zealand Contrast: ACC's Medical Misadventure Coverage

New Zealand's **Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC)** provides a striking contrast. ACC is a universal no-fault scheme covering personal injuries from accidents, including medical injuries ("treatment injuries"). Under ACC's treatment injury coverage:

- Birth injuries meeting the treatment injury criteria (an injury caused by treatment that would not be expected as part of the ordinary course of treatment) are covered
- Families receive lump-sum serious injury compensation, weekly earnings-related compensation, and medical and rehabilitation costs
- The right to sue in tort for compensation is removed (though exemplary/punitive damages claims can still proceed in some circumstances)

New Zealand obstetricians pay substantially lower medical indemnity premiums than their Australian counterparts — a frequently cited consequence of ACC eliminating the long-tail litigation risk. New Zealand's obstetric workforce and private maternity sector face different pressures, but the liability removal is real.

The *lack* of equivalent coverage in Australia means Australian obstetricians bear the full insurance cost of the long-tail litigation risk, which is then passed on via:
- Higher gap fees (to offset higher premiums)
- Lower provision of rural and regional obstetric services (where claim volumes are lower but premiums remain high)
- Exit from private practice by older obstetricians facing the premium-revenue squeeze

### 6. The Private Obstetrics Crisis: The Premium-Gap-Closure Cascade

The pathway from the liability system to private maternity unit closures runs as follows:

1. **Premiums are high** (~$65,000–$100,000+/year) because the liability tail is long and claim values are large
2. **Gap fees are charged** because MBS rebates (~$42/visit for routine antenatal; ~$700–$1,400 for delivery items) plus PHI benefits do not cover the premium cost, let alone obstetrician income
3. **PHI gold-tier is required** for private obstetric admission but O&G services are **excluded from the risk equalisation pool**, meaning insurers receive no cross-subsidy for high-cost obstetric claims
4. **Consumer cost-of-living pressure** is reducing PHI gold-tier uptake — fewer women holding qualifying cover
5. **Private hospital maternity units operate on thin margins** — fixed costs are high, birth volumes are declining (total fertility rate 1.50 in 2023, a record low), and Healthscope closures in 2024-25 have been particularly visible
6. **Unit closures accelerate the cycle** — fewer private hospitals offering maternity care means remaining obstetricians take higher volumes or also exit private practice

RANZCOG has documented at least 14 private hospital maternity unit closures since 2018; Avant's submission to federal government consultations put the figure at **18 closures since 2018**. The Department of Health's financial health check flagged obstetrics as "of particular concern." The November 2025 RANZCOG roundtable identified six reform pathways but produced no government commitment. The April 2025 RANZCOG/NASOG parliamentary push for O&G risk equalisation has not resulted in legislative action.

### 7. The Midwife Indemnity Subsidy: A Partial Model for What Could Be Done

The 100% Commonwealth coverage of intrapartum claims for endorsed midwives from 1 July 2025 is notable. It suggests the government accepts that market-priced indemnity for certain maternity practitioners is not viable — and has effectively nationalised the birth injury liability for this cohort.

The same logic does not apply to obstetricians operating in private practice, despite similar long-tail exposure. The asymmetry reflects the different political economy: obstetricians are typically high-earning specialists; the government's calculus for premium support looks different. But the structural problem — birth injury liability as an uninsurable or unaffordable risk — is the same.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **Is the NIIS medical treatment injury component ever coming?** The 2019 Treasury discussion paper has not been followed by a policy decision. State governments face fiscal pressure and are unlikely to advocate for an expensive new scheme. The Commonwealth has not provided a policy position update in five years. Meanwhile the 2030s are when NIIS birth injury claims from current births would begin to mature.

2. **Should birth injury be no-fault?** The scientific evidence is genuinely mixed: 85–90% of cerebral palsy is likely not attributable to intrapartum events, yet the tort system funds claims based on identifiable care acts. A no-fault scheme for all birth-related brain injury regardless of causation would reduce litigation, lower premiums, and provide more consistent support — but would require substantial public funding and would remove the accountability signal that litigation (for all its flaws) provides.

3. **Is the private obstetrics market worth saving?** Australia could theoretically accept the trend toward public-only birth — a system in which birth is a public service, like emergency surgery. But this would further strain already-stressed public obstetric and midwifery workforces, eliminate choice for women who value continuity with a named practitioner, and make the public system the sole setting for complex presentations.

4. **Can the HCCS threshold keep pace with claim inflation?** The threshold was $300,000 (pre-2018) and is $500,000 today. If settlement values continue to increase — driven by lifetime care cost inflation, improved life expectancy for people with severe disabilities, and expanding heads of damage — the threshold proportion that the Commonwealth absorbs falls. A periodic review mechanism is not apparent in current policy.

5. **Accountability without litigation?** If Australia moves toward no-fault for birth injury, what replaces the accountability function of negligence claims? Queensland's landmark birth trauma framework (2024) and the national maternity safety systems are institutional responses, but they are not directly linked to compensation outcomes.

1. **New Zealand's ACC in depth:** How the ACC medical treatment injury category works, its actual claim volumes for birth injury, and why New Zealand obstetricians pay materially lower premiums — a concrete policy benchmark for the Australian debate.

2. **Cerebral Palsy Alliance and the NDIS pathway:** How CP is funded post-NDIS, whether the early childhood pathway under NDIS 2.0 (the Foundational Supports reform) affects CP families, and the interface between tort compensation and NDIS support packages.

3. **Birth trauma and the maternity safety system:** The 2024 NSW inquiry into birth trauma made 43 recommendations — what are the payment/funding implications, and how does birth injury intersect with Australia's maternity adverse event reporting systems (SOMANZ, PSANZ).

4. **APRA and the medical indemnity market concentration:** Avant's 55% market share raises concentration risk; the 2002 UMP crisis (when Australia's then-largest MDO collapsed) illustrated what happens when the indemnity market fails — what safeguards exist today?

5. **Tort reform and the Civil Liability Act caps:** Whether the general damages cap under state Civil Liability Acts adequately limits obstetrician liability exposure, and whether removing or raising it would accelerate the private obstetrics crisis further.
