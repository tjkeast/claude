# BRCA1/2 and Hereditary Breast-Ovarian Cancer in Australia: Testing, PARP Inhibitors, and the Limits of a More Developed System

**Report 098 — 2026-09-17**

**Summary:** Australia's BRCA1/2 and hereditary breast-ovarian cancer (HBOC) system is meaningfully better funded than its Lynch syndrome counterpart — mainstream testing models, a suite of MBS items linked to treatment eligibility, and a rapidly expanding PBS roster of PARP inhibitors have given Australia a functional (if still incomplete) genomic oncology pathway. But the same structural gaps persist: low cascade testing uptake, a genetic counselling workforce that cannot absorb population demand, and no funded population genomic screening program despite compelling cost-effectiveness evidence. This report maps the MBS and PBS architecture, documents the key 2024–2026 expansions, and asks where the system is still failing the estimated 33,000–67,000 Australians who carry a BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathogenic variant.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 097 documented that Lynch syndrome affects ~90,000 Australians but remains chronically under-diagnosed, with an MBS testing pathway that breaks down at step 2 (no funded MLH1 methylation or MSI testing) and a 49% referral gap at genetics services. The HBOC/BRCA system is frequently cited as the more developed parallel — and understanding it illuminates what Lynch syndrome policy could look like, and why even the more-resourced system still has critical gaps.

---

## Background: The Scale of BRCA1/2 in Australia

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are tumour suppressor genes encoding proteins central to double-strand DNA repair via homologous recombination. Pathogenic variants cause hereditary breast-ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC), conferring substantially elevated lifetime risks across multiple cancer types.

**Prevalence:** Combined BRCA1 and BRCA2 pathogenic variant carrier rates are estimated at approximately 1 in 400 to 1 in 800 in the general (non-Ashkenazi-Jewish) population. Applied to Australia's population of ~27 million, this implies roughly **33,750–67,500 Australians** carry a pathogenic BRCA1 or BRCA2 variant. Ashkenazi Jewish Australians carry these mutations at approximately 2.5% — around ten times the background rate.

**Cancer burden:** BRCA1/2 variants contribute to:
- ~5–10% of all breast cancers in Australia: against an estimated **20,640 breast cancer cases** expected in 2025 (AIHW), this implies approximately **1,000–2,000 BRCA-attributable breast cancers per year**
- ~15–20% of high-grade epithelial ovarian cancers: against an estimated **1,929 ovarian cancer cases** in 2025, this implies approximately **290–385 BRCA-attributable ovarian cancers per year**
- Smaller proportions of pancreatic, prostate (particularly high-grade and metastatic), biliary tract, and male breast cancer

Lifetime cancer risks for confirmed carriers are substantial:

| | BRCA1 carriers | BRCA2 carriers |
|---|---|---|
| Breast cancer (lifetime) | ~72% | ~69% |
| Ovarian cancer (lifetime) | ~44% | ~17% |
| Contralateral breast cancer | ~40% by 10 years post-diagnosis | ~26% by 10 years |
| Prostate cancer (lifetime, men) | ~3.8× general population | ~8.6× general population |

## Key Findings

### 1. MBS Testing Architecture: Substantially More Developed Than Lynch Syndrome

The MBS BRCA testing suite has evolved substantially since 2017 and now covers multiple clinical contexts with dedicated items. The architecture as of 2026:

**Germline BRCA1/2 testing — hereditary cancer risk:**
- **MBS items 73296/73297** (amended 1 March 2025): Fund characterisation of germline gene variants in patients with breast, ovarian, fallopian tube or primary peritoneal cancer with ≥10% risk of a pathogenic variant (estimated by a validated algorithm), or in relatives of confirmed variant carriers. The March 2025 amendment expanded the eligible gene panel beyond BRCA1/2 to include STK11, PTEN, CDH1, PALB2, and TP53 — enabling oncologists to order a broader multigene panel without multiple separate items. Item fee: ~$400; Medicare benefit 75% ($300) or 85% ($340).

**Somatic/tumour BRCA and HRD testing — treatment eligibility:**
- **MBS item 73307** (introduced 1 January 2024): Funds HRD (homologous recombination deficiency) testing of tumour tissue in patients with newly diagnosed advanced or metastatic high-grade epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube or primary peritoneal cancer, to determine eligibility for PBS-subsidised PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy. This is a crucial structural improvement: it means the tumour test gateway to treatment funding is now rebated, removing a previous out-of-pocket barrier.

**BRCA testing in prostate cancer:**
- **MBS items 73303/73304** (expanded): Fund BRCA1/2 testing (germline and somatic) in men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) to determine eligibility for olaparib or talazoparib+enzalutamide. A 2025 guidance paper (*Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology*) highlighted that despite MBS funding, adoption remained variable and called for systematic testing protocols at mCRPC diagnosis.

**Contrast with Lynch syndrome:** Lynch syndrome has no equivalent of item 73307. The critical Step 2 of its diagnostic cascade — MLH1 methylation and MSI testing — has no MBS item. In HBOC, the tumour testing pathway is funded; in Lynch syndrome, it is not.

### 2. The Mainstream Testing Model: From Genetics Clinic to Oncology Clinic

Since 2017, Australia has operated a "mainstream" BRCA testing model in which treating oncologists and surgeons — not clinical geneticists — can order MBS-rebated BRCA germline testing for eligible patients. This model has progressively expanded:

- **South Australia pilot (2022–2023):** A statewide evaluation (Jennings et al., *Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology*, 2025) found that mainstream BRCA1/2 testing significantly increased after pathway reform. However, the same study found **no equivalent improvement in MMR gene (Lynch syndrome) mainstream testing** — confirming that HBOC and Lynch syndrome have been treated as structurally different problems even where the same statewide infrastructure exists.
- Patient satisfaction with mainstream testing is high (~90–95%), with patients generally preferring their treating clinician over a separate genetics appointment.
- The mainstream model remains limited: it requires the ordering clinician to provide genetic counselling or arrange referral. In busy oncology settings, this is often not achieved — a gap documented in Australian surveys of clinician attitudes.

### 3. PBS PARP Inhibitors: A Rapidly Expanding Treatment Formulary

The PBS now contains a growing suite of PARP inhibitors for BRCA-attributable cancers:

**Olaparib (Lynparza, AstraZeneca):**
- Ovarian cancer, first-line maintenance (gBRCA1/2): listed
- Ovarian cancer, first-line maintenance (HRD+, broader): expanded January 2024
- **Metastatic HER2-negative breast cancer (gBRCA1/2): listed from 1 January 2025** — ~300 eligible patients annually; patient cost $31.60/script ($7.70 concession)
- Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (BRCA1/2): listed

**Niraparib (Zejula, GSK):**
- Ovarian cancer, first-line maintenance (BRCA-mutated): listed September 2022
- Ovarian cancer, first-line maintenance (HRD+): expanded April 2024

**Talazoparib + enzalutamide (Talzenna, Pfizer):**
- First-line mCRPC (BRCA1/2): ~180 eligible patients annually

The January 2025 olaparib listing for metastatic breast cancer was the most significant recent expansion. Prior to listing, eligible patients paid $100,000+ per year out-of-pocket or accessed through compassionate supply. The listing required an amended MBS item for germline BRCA testing in metastatic breast cancer patients — meaning that treatment access now directly drives testing uptake for this indication.

### 4. Risk-Reducing Surgery: Funded but Inaccessible

BRCA1/2 carriers can dramatically reduce their cancer risks through risk-reducing surgery:
- **Risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (RRBSO):** Reduces ovarian cancer risk by ~95%; also reduces breast cancer risk in premenopausal women (up to 50% at age 40). MBS covers this under standard surgical items.
- **Risk-reducing mastectomy (RRM):** Reduces breast cancer risk by ≥95%. MBS covers this under relevant breast surgical items.

Despite MBS coverage, access is the acute problem. A November 2024 joint statement by Breast Surgeons of Australia and New Zealand (BreastSurgANZ) and Breast Cancer Network Australia (BCNA) documented:
- Wait times **exceeding 12 months** for risk-reducing surgery in many public hospital settings
- Inconsistent classification of these procedures as semi-urgent elective operations
- Significant geographic variation in access: metropolitan tertiary centres have specialist HBOC teams; regional patients may not

This is a structural access failure: MBS funding exists in principle, but the operational prioritisation system does not treat these risk-reducing procedures as urgent despite their time-sensitive nature for confirmed BRCA carriers.

### 5. The DNA Screen Signal: Population Screening Is Needed and Not Funded

The DNA Screen pilot (Lacaze et al., *Nature Health*, January 2026) — a Monash University-led program offering genomic screening to 10,263 Australians aged 18–40 — detected pathogenic variants in 2.0% of participants across BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome genes, and familial hypercholesterolaemia.

The key finding: approximately 1 in 50 young Australians carries a high-risk hereditary variant across these three conditions, and 74.5% of those with pathogenic variants were **ineligible for current government-funded testing** under existing MBS criteria. A companion modelling study (*eClinicalMedicine*, 2023) found that combined population genomic screening at AU$200–500/sample would be cost-effective and health-saving across an 8.3 million-person simulated cohort.

No population genomic screening program has been funded as of September 2026. The National Framework for Genomics in Cancer Control (Cancer Australia, January 2025) articulates a vision for equitable genomic access in cancer care but has not yet been matched by new MBS investment.

### 6. Genetic Counselling Capacity: The Binding Constraint

The same workforce crisis that limits Lynch syndrome identification limits HBOC. The 2024 Australian/New Zealand genetic counsellor workforce survey (*Genetics in Health*) found:
- ~432 clinical genetic counsellors in Australasia (Australia + New Zealand combined)
- Two-thirds perceive a **critical shortage**
- Wait times >12 months at many public genetics services

HBOC creates a specific cascade demand: when a BRCA1/2 proband is identified, each first-degree relative has a 50% chance of carrying the family variant. Internationally, cascade testing uptake among eligible relatives averages only **30–50% within 3 years** of index diagnosis. In Australia, First Nations, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD), and rural families face additional structural barriers to cascade testing uptake.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Diagnostic-therapeutic linkage is driving testing — but who falls outside it?** The expansion of PARP inhibitor PBS listings is materially increasing BRCA testing volumes through treatment-eligibility pathways. But this means testing is most accessible for people who already have cancer and whose clinician knows to test. Unaffected carriers — who would benefit most from preventive intervention — remain largely outside the funded pathway.

**2. The mainstream model's informed consent problem.** MBS-rebated mainstream BRCA testing requires genetic counselling before ordering. In practice, busy oncologists provide brief pre-test information, not full counselling. The South Australian evaluation found this gap persisted even in the statewide mainstream model. Psychosocial consequences of BRCA diagnosis (preventive surgery decisions, implications for children and relatives, reproductive choices) are substantial and require proper support.

**3. Equity in cascade testing.** Cascade testing for confirmed relatives is theoretically free under MBS item 73297 — but effective access requires the proband's family to communicate the result, relatives to seek testing, and testing to be geographically accessible. These conditions do not hold uniformly. Cascade testing is a public health intervention requiring active infrastructure, not a passive eligibility right.

**4. Surveillance vs. prevention.** MBS funds high-risk surveillance for unaffected BRCA1/2 carriers (annual MRI and mammogram under items for high-risk women). But this is detecting cancer, not preventing it. Risk-reducing surgery prevents cancer — and access barriers mean prevention is less available than surveillance.

**5. The prostate cancer blind spot.** BRCA2 substantially elevates prostate cancer risk (~8.6× general population) and is now a treatment-eligibility determinant for PARP inhibitors in mCRPC. However, urologists and oncologists treating prostate cancer were slower to adopt systematic BRCA testing than breast and ovarian cancer specialists — the 2025 Australian guidance paper was needed precisely because routine testing had not yet been embedded in practice.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH): Australia's parallel under-diagnosis hereditary condition** — FH affects ~1 in 250 Australians (~108,000 people), carries 20-fold elevated cardiovascular risk if untreated, and has a cascade testing framework with MBS support since 2020. It offers a distinct comparison: a non-cancer hereditary condition that has been treated differently within the same health system.

2. **The Australian National Genomics Strategy and genomic testing MBS reform** — The January 2025 National Framework for Genomics in Cancer Control, the MSAC pipeline for new genomic testing items, and the gap between vision and funded reality.

3. **Multi-gene panel testing and variants of uncertain significance (VUS)** — The March 2025 expansion of MBS items 73296/73297 to cover broader gene panels raises the unresolved challenge of VUS: gene variants whose clinical significance is unknown, creating diagnostic uncertainty for patients and clinicians.

4. **Gastrointestinal cancer genetics beyond Lynch and BRCA: FAP, MAP, Peutz-Jeghers syndrome** — The hereditary CRC syndromes outside Lynch syndrome and their funding architecture.

5. **Genetic counsellor workforce reform and telegenetics** — Pathways to scale genetic counselling capacity, including telegenetics delivery (already partially implemented) and allied health workforce models.
