# Community-Managed Mental Health: Australia's Block Grant System, the 500,000-Person Psychosocial Gap, and the Race to Build Foundational Supports

**Report 049 — 2026-07-25**

**Summary:** Australia funds its community mental health NGO sector at roughly 7% of total state mental health spending — compared to 30% in New Zealand — leaving an estimated 500,000 Australians with significant mental illness without access to the psychosocial support they need. The sector is delivered through a patchwork of PHN-commissioned block grants, state contracts, and the Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program, with short-term funding cycles causing chronic workforce instability. A 2024 ANAO audit found only 16% of PHNs met basic outcome measurement targets. The government's 2026 response — a National Agreement on Foundational Supports committing $4 billion over five years — begins with children's developmental supports; adult psychosocial funding remains in design limbo as NDIS tightening proceeds from 2028.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 048 showed that Victoria's $1 billion-per-year Mental Health Levy is the Commonwealth's most credible test of whether dedicated health hypothecation can fund reform — but the question it leaves open is whether the money is reaching the most critical and least-funded layer of the system: the community-managed NGO sector that delivers the non-clinical psychosocial support that hospital systems cannot.

---

## Background

Australia's mental health system has three broad layers. The first — acute hospital care and specialist inpatient services — absorbs the largest share of public funding and receives the most political attention. The second — Medicare-funded clinical services, primarily GP mental health plans and psychology under Better Access (covered in Report 011) — absorbs most of the Commonwealth's ambulatory health budget. The third layer is the one that most Australians with serious mental illness actually depend on for daily functioning: community-managed mental health services delivered by non-government organisations (NGOs) and funded through block grants from state governments and Primary Health Networks (PHNs).

This third layer — encompassing housing support, peer support workers, psychosocial recovery coaching, drop-in services, social inclusion programs, and assertive outreach — is structurally the most important and chronically the most underfunded. Unlike clinical services that are counted in Medicare data and attributed to specific MBS items, the NGO sector's work is largely invisible in the formal data systems governments use to allocate funding.

The result, documented repeatedly by peak bodies, the Productivity Commission, and successive National Mental Health Commission reviews, is a gap that has widened for decades: between what Australians with mental illness need, what the acute system provides, and what the community sector can deliver.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of the Psychosocial Gap

The most comprehensive recent estimate of unmet need comes from an analysis commissioned in 2023–24 and cited in Mental Health Australia's 2026–27 Pre-Budget Submission:

- **~500,000 Australians** with significant mental illness are missing out on psychosocial support outside the NDIS
- Of these: **263,100 experience moderate mental illness** and **230,500 experience severe mental illness**
- The Productivity Commission estimated that serving the **154,000 people with the most severe unmet need** would require an additional **$610 million per year** — far beyond current Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP) funding of approximately $100 million per year
- The gap in access to mental health supports between **high-income and low-income Australians** has almost doubled over the past decade (Mental Health Australia, 2026)

A Grattan Institute report (December 2025) found about 130,000 adults are falling through the cracks in the "missing middle" — too unwell for GPs but not severe enough for overwhelmed public acute services. The report argued Australia could build a national psychosocial support system within five years without additional net spending, through structural redesign rather than new money.

### 2. The NGO Sector's Funding Margin

The chronic underfunding of the community-managed sector is visible in the numbers:

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Total government mental health expenditure (2022–23) | $12.6 billion ($478 per person) | AIHW / PC Report on Government Services 2026 |
| State/territory share | ~$8 billion (two-thirds) | AIHW |
| Commonwealth share | ~$4.6 billion (one-third) | AIHW |
| NGO share of state mental health budgets | **~7%** | PC / MHCC analysis |
| New Zealand's equivalent NGO share | **~30%** | International comparison |
| Queensland NGO share (lowest state) | **4.7%** | QAMH |
| NSW NGO share (2022–23) | **9.3%** (down from 10.2%) | Productivity Commission 2026 |
| Historical NGO share (1992) | <2% | Mental Health Australia |
| NGO share progression over 30 years | 2% → 7% | Long-term trend data |

The trajectory is clear: the community sector has grown as a share of mental health spending, but only marginally over three decades, and far more slowly than the clinical and acute sectors. Australia's chronic preference for hospital-based and Medicare-funded services has left the community layer at roughly one-quarter of New Zealand's investment rate as a proportion of the total system.

### 3. The Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP)

The primary Commonwealth funding vehicle for community-based psychosocial support is the **Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP)**, commissioned through PHNs. Key details:

- Supports approximately **25,000 people** with severe mental health challenges who are not in the NDIS
- **2024–25 MYEFO** extended the CPSP for two years to 30 June 2027, committing $272.1 million ($267.9 million to PHN arrangements specifically)
- Approximate annual funding: **~$100 million through PHNs**
- Services include care coordination, navigation, case management, brief interventions, psychosocial support, and social prescribing
- Services are commissioned by 31 PHNs and delivered by local NGOs

The program's design reflects a deliberate choice made in the post-NDIS landscape: rather than fund psychosocial supports directly from Commonwealth appropriations, the CPSP uses PHNs as commissioning intermediaries. PHNs assess local needs, tender for services, and contract NGOs. This gives PHNs flexibility but introduces a layer of administrative complexity and highly variable local approaches across the country's 31 PHN regions.

### 4. PHN Accountability: The ANAO Audit Finding

A February 2024 **ANAO performance audit** on the Department of Health and Aged Care's management of Primary Health Networks found serious accountability gaps:

- The Department had **failed to demonstrate** that the PHN delivery model was achieving its policy objectives
- PHNs are assessed against six mental health performance indicators, including whether people are accessing different levels of care
- Only **16.1% of PHNs** met the **outcome measures performance indicator** — which requires that at least 70% of commissioned service providers collect outcome data at the beginning and end of each mental health care episode
- This means in over 83% of PHN regions, basic before-and-after outcome measurement is not consistently happening

The accountability gap is structural: PHNs receive block grants, subcontract to NGOs, and are supposed to report outcomes to the Department — but the data infrastructure, standardisation, and performance management to make this work at a national level has not been built. Funding goes out; evidence of what it achieves does not come back reliably.

### 5. The BCG/PHN Review (December 2025)

In late 2025, the Department released a final report from an independent review of the PHN program and mental health flexible funding stream, conducted by Boston Consulting Group and ConceptSix. Key recommendations include:

- **Consolidate** the multiple separate mental health funding streams into a **single flexible stream**, giving PHNs clearer mandate and fewer administrative silos
- Move to **longer-term rolling grants** (rather than annual or short-term contracting) to improve service stability and provider confidence
- Establish a new **Quality Improvement team** within the Department to drive consistent performance assessment
- **Standardise reporting** across PHNs to enable national data aggregation and benchmarking
- Clarify PHNs' accountability role as **trusted partners of the Commonwealth** with genuine responsibility for local primary mental health care

The recommendations were welcomed by the PHN Cooperative. Implementation is expected to require a one-off investment in Department resourcing and the development of new funding deed templates — a reform with meaningful but not immediate impact on service delivery.

### 6. State-Level Workforce Instability

Below the PHN commissioning layer, the NGO organisations that actually deliver services face a distinct structural problem: chronic short-term contracts. The dominant funding model — annual or biennial grants from PHNs or state health departments — means:

- NGO staff face **rolling job insecurity**, with many frontline workers on fixed-term contracts renewed annually
- Organisations cannot offer competitive superannuation, career progression, or training investment
- **Workforce turnover is high**, disrupting continuity of care for people with the most complex needs
- Organisations spend significant management resources on re-tendering and grant renewal rather than service improvement

NSW has been the most active state in responding to this. The **NSW Secure Jobs and Funding Certainty Roadmap** (2025), a 2023 state election commitment, introduced five-year funding contracts for community service organisations. The Mental Health Coordinating Council (MHCC) has specifically advocated for this model as the minimum necessary to stabilise the NGO mental health workforce.

But NSW's 2026–27 Budget, while welcomed for some new crisis-sector investment, was criticised by Mind Australia and One Door Mental Health for providing **no certainty around NSW's investment in psychosocial Foundational Supports** — the critical next phase of reform. The Black Dog Institute published a joint statement noting community mental health gaps in NSW are continuing to widen.

### 7. The National Agreement on Foundational Supports 2026–31

The most significant structural development of 2026 is the **National Agreement on Foundational Supports**, signed on 2 February 2026 between the Commonwealth and all states and territories:

- Five-year agreement (2026–31)
- **50/50 cost-sharing** between Commonwealth and states/territories
- **$4 billion committed over five years** for the first implementation phase
- First phase (**Thriving Kids**): from 1 October 2026, covers children aged 8 and under with developmental delay and/or autism with low to moderate support needs — redirected away from NDIS into a separate funded stream
- **Adult psychosocial supports**: identified as the next cohort for negotiation with states and territories, but not included in the initial $4 billion commitment — design is still in progress

The agreement represents the Commonwealth's most concrete response to the psychosocial gap, but its timetable raises concern for advocates. NDIS access tightens from **1 January 2028** — meaning people with psychosocial disability who do not meet a tighter eligibility threshold will exit (or fail to enter) the NDIS from that date. The community sector has been explicit: without a fully funded, co-designed alternative firmly in place before 2028, people will fall through the gap.

The Queensland Alliance for Mental Health (QAMH) noted after the 2026–27 Federal Budget that $2.7 billion in existing health, disability, and ageing programs is being redirected over five years, raising questions about whether mental health NGOs will face funding cuts as the Foundational Supports architecture is built.

### 8. The Indexation Problem

A recurring structural issue for community mental health NGOs receiving government funding is **indexation adequacy**. QAMH highlighted that for 2026–27:

- Queensland's approved NGO indexation rate is **3.31%**
- But the 2026–27 Budget revised the CPI forecast upward to **3.75%**
- The indexation rate was calculated on the earlier (lower) MYFER forecast, leaving NGOs with a **real effective funding cut** of ~0.44 percentage points
- QAMH formally wrote to the Queensland Treasurer requesting a CPI-aligned adjustment

This is not a Queensland-specific problem: across Australian states, community sector indexation has rarely kept pace with actual cost increases in wages, rent, and insurance — systematically eroding the real purchasing power of block grants over time.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The design-timing tension.** The NDIS will tighten from 2028, but adult psychosocial Foundational Supports are not yet designed, funded, or legislated. The sector's fear is that eligibility gates close before the alternative infrastructure exists.

**2. Accountability vs flexibility.** The PHN block grant model was designed to give regional flexibility and reduce red tape. The ANAO audit found that flexibility without accountability systems has produced a system where outcomes are not measured. The BCG review's recommendations will standardise reporting, but whether this constrains local responsiveness is contested.

**3. The commissioning layer problem.** Having 31 PHNs independently commission community mental health services means 31 different funding rounds, contracts, and reporting requirements for NGOs operating nationally. Mind Australia and other large national providers must navigate PHN-by-PHN variation that adds administrative cost without clinical benefit.

**4. State variation in commitment.** Queensland at 4.7% and NSW at 9.3% (and falling) illustrate that the community sector receives starkly different shares of mental health budgets across states. There is no national minimum standard for what proportion of mental health spending should reach the NGO sector.

**5. The wage gap.** Community sector mental health workers are paid significantly less than equivalent public health workers, despite the NDIS wage-setting process having partially narrowed this gap. The Fair Work Commission's Equal Remuneration Order for the social and community services sector has improved base pay, but premium relativities with clinical counterparts remain. This drives talent into clinical settings and away from the community sector precisely where need is highest.

**6. Victoria's model as a test case.** As documented in Report 048, Victoria's Mental Health Levy is now generating over $1 billion per year for mental health reform. How much of that is reaching community-managed services — rather than hospital infrastructure and specialist workforce — will be the system design question of the next five years.
