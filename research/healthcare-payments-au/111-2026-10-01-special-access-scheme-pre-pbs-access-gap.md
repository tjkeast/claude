# Australia's Special Access Scheme and the Pre-PBS Access Gap: How Patients Navigate the 726-Day Wait

**One-line summary:** The TGA's Special Access Scheme — designed as a safety valve for individual patients needing medicines not yet listed in Australia — has expanded dramatically under the weight of a growing PBS access gap, a medicinal cannabis market now worth over $1 billion per year, and a private obesity-medicine boom, creating a two-tier system in which out-of-pocket costs run to hundreds of dollars per month for those who cannot access free compassionate programs.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 110 examined the NZ–Australia pharmaceutical access gap, noting that while Australia's PBS lists far more medicines than PHARMAC, Australian patients still face a significant delay between TGA approval and PBS listing — and New Zealand's even longer delays leave patients dependent on individual Named Patient Pharmaceutical Applications (NPPA). Australia's equivalent, the Special Access Scheme, is the mechanism that bridges that gap — but it is under growing strain.

---

## Background

There are two distinct moments in the life of a medicine in Australia: the day the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) approves it as safe and effective for marketing, and the day it appears on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Schedule (PBS) with a government subsidy that makes it accessible. Between those two moments — sometimes months, often years — patients who need the medicine face a difficult choice: pay full price out of pocket, seek access through a pharmaceutical company's compassionate program, enrol in a clinical trial, or go without.

The Special Access Scheme (SAS) is the primary legal mechanism by which clinicians access unapproved therapeutic goods for individual patients. It sits under the *Therapeutic Goods Act 1989* and predates most of Australia's modern pharmaceutical access architecture. Alongside it operates the Authorised Prescriber (AP) scheme, which allows approved practitioners to prescribe specific unapproved goods to defined classes of patients without seeking individual approvals each time. Together, these pathways were conceived as controlled safety valves. They have become something considerably larger.

## Key Findings

### 1. The SAS Architecture: Three Pathways for Different Clinical Scenarios

The SAS operates through three pathways, each calibrated to a different level of urgency and risk:

**Category A** is for patients who are seriously ill, with death reasonably likely to occur within months or in the absence of early treatment. Practitioners can supply the unapproved good immediately and notify the TGA within 28 days. No prior approval is required, and the prescriber takes on responsibility for the clinical decision. Category A is the classic emergency pathway.

**Category B** requires formal TGA approval before supply. Practitioners submit an application, typically receiving a decision within 2–5 business days. This is used when the patient needs access to an unapproved good but the urgency does not meet the Category A threshold — for instance, where a medicine is TGA-registered but not PBS-listed, and the waiting time for PBS subsidy is indefinite.

**Category C** is a streamlined notification-only pathway for products with an established history of safe use, appearing on a TGA-approved list. Practitioners notify the TGA but receive an immediate right to supply without individual assessment. Since the 2022 reforms, this list has been expanded substantially, and it has become the dominant channel for medicinal cannabis prescribing.

The **Authorised Prescriber (AP) scheme** operates differently: a practitioner obtains approval from the TGA (or a Human Research Ethics Committee) to prescribe a specific unapproved good to a class of patients, then can do so without further individual applications. This is particularly suited to specialists in fields where multiple patients need the same unapproved treatment — for instance, oncologists accessing medicines undergoing PBAC assessment.

In July 2022, the TGA reformed the entire SAS and AP framework: the online portal replaced paper-based submissions, the SAS-C list was expanded, and the approval process for AP applications was streamlined. In October 2024, further updates were made, primarily relating to therapeutic vapes for smoking cessation.

### 2. The Scale of the SAS: From Safety Valve to Workhorse

The SAS was once genuinely exceptional — a mechanism for unusual, individual cases. It is no longer.

In 2023, the TGA received more than **264,000 SAS submissions** — compared with fewer than 60,000 a decade earlier. In 2023–24, more than **242,000 applications and notifications** were processed. This represents a more than fourfold increase in a decade, driven overwhelmingly by medicinal cannabis.

Medicinal cannabis now accounts for roughly **80% or more of SAS-B applications**. The Australian medicinal cannabis market — accessed almost entirely through SAS or AP pathways — was estimated to be worth over **$1 billion** in 2024. More than **500,000 individual SAS approvals** for medicinal cannabis have been issued since the pathway opened. The leading conditions approved for are pain, anxiety, and sleep disorders, and Australia has become one of the largest medicinal cannabis markets in the world.

In August 2025, the TGA opened a formal consultation on whether the existing medicinal cannabis oversight framework remained appropriate, receiving **790 submissions** from clinicians, pharmacist bodies, colleges, patient groups, industry, and government. The TGA's response was published in February 2026. The consultation reflected ongoing concerns — including from the AMA — about direct-to-consumer telehealth entities exploiting the SAS to prescribe medicinal cannabis outside standard clinical governance frameworks. The AMA's May 2025 submission specifically flagged the behaviour of some vertically integrated telehealth operators, escalating concerns to the TGA, AHPRA, and the Medical Board of Australia.

### 3. The Patient Access Gap: 591 Days Became 726 Days

Separate from medicinal cannabis, the SAS plays an increasingly significant role as a bridge for patients waiting on PBS listing decisions. The scale of the access gap has grown substantially.

In 2024, the Amgen-commissioned *Patient Access Gap Report* found that between January 2021 and April 2024, the average time from TGA approval to PBS listing for novel medicines was **591 days**. By 2025, Amgen's updated analysis estimated the figure had risen to **726 days** — an increase of 135 days. For medicines assessed on a cost-effectiveness basis, more than **80% required multiple PBAC submission attempts** before listing, with an average timeline of 638 days; cost-minimisation applications were faster (488 days) and more likely to succeed on first attempt.

In March 2025, the problem became acute: the Department of Health informed pharmaceutical companies that **44 of 77 medicines** scheduled for PBAC review that month would face indefinite delays due to "workload and resourcing issues." The Minister for Health subsequently announced an additional PBAC meeting for 2025, but the structural backlog reflects a regulatory and bureaucratic bottleneck that individual scheduling fixes cannot resolve. The HTA Implementation Advisory Group (IAG), whose 50 recommendations to modernise Australia's health technology assessment were finalised in January 2026, has not yet produced the systemic reforms needed to reduce these delays.

For patients waiting on a medicine that has been TGA-approved but not yet PBS-listed — sometimes for multiple years — the SAS-B or compassionate access pathway is the only legal route to treatment.

### 4. The Cost Problem: Full Price, No Regulation

The fundamental financial reality of SAS access is blunt: **unapproved products are not covered by the PBS**. Patients pay full price. The TGA has no jurisdiction over pricing of unapproved products supplied through the SAS — manufacturers and distributors set prices in the private market, with no schedule fee, no government-negotiated price, and no safety net.

For some drug categories, the costs are prohibitive:

- **Wegovy (semaglutide, weight management):** Recommended by the PBAC for subsidised listing in late 2025 for people with severe obesity and existing cardiovascular disease, but not yet PBS-listed as of October 2026. Available on private prescription at approximately **$460 per month** at the 2.4 mg maintenance dose.
- **Ozempic (semaglutide) for weight loss:** PBS-listed for type 2 diabetes but not for weight loss as a primary indication. Private prescription for weight management costs approximately **$150–$170 per month**.
- **Mounjaro (tirzepatide) for weight loss:** Approximately **$395 per month** via private prescription.

These costs place effective treatment beyond routine reach for most Australians on median incomes ($67,600/year, $5,633/month), let alone those on low incomes, pensions, or fixed budgets. A year's treatment with Wegovy at full private price costs roughly **$5,520** — comparable to two weeks' gross median income.

For oncology and other high-cost medicines not yet PBS-listed, the costs are more severe. Many medicines awaiting PBAC approval cost tens of thousands of dollars per year at international private prices, and some cost hundreds of thousands. This is where the compassionate access architecture becomes critical.

### 5. Compassionate Access Programs: Who Gets Free Medicine and Who Pays

Pharmaceutical companies operating in Australia routinely run **Compassionate Access Programs (CAPs)** — also called expanded access programs, managed access programs, or named patient programs — through which they supply medicines to patients outside of clinical trials, either free of charge or at cost-share, while PBAC assessment is pending or after a negative PBAC determination.

A 2024 audit from a single Australian oncology institution, presented at the Clinical Oncology Society of Australia annual scientific meeting, found that of **237 cases** accessing drugs through compassionate programs:

- **160 (67.5%)** received the drug **completely cost-free** — provided by the company as pre-commercial or post-negative access
- **77 (32.5%)** were on a **cost-share program**, where either the patient or an institution co-paid

These figures suggest that for oncology patients at a specialist centre with active pharmaceutical industry relationships, free or subsidised access is available more often than not. But the equity implications are significant: access depends on:

- Whether a specialist has relationships with relevant pharmaceutical company representatives
- Whether the company has established a formal compassionate program for that medicine
- Whether the patient is at a major metropolitan centre (where programs are concentrated)
- Whether the patient is enrolled at an institution actively seeking such arrangements

Rural patients, patients treated in regional hospitals, and patients without specialist support are systematically less likely to know about or access these programs. There is no centralised register of available compassionate access programs in Australia, no standardised eligibility criteria, and no enforceable right of access.

### 6. The Growing Private Medicine Market: Beyond the PBS

The most recent systemic evidence on the scale of non-PBS pharmaceutical access comes from IQVIA's May 2025 white paper *Evolving Beyond the PBS: Exploring Australia's Private Medicines Market*.

The data shows a structural shift over five years:

| Year | PBS share of retail prescriptions | Non-PBS share | Pre-PBS (SAS/CAP) share |
|------|-----------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------|
| 2019 | 92% | 9% | 1.0% |
| 2024 | 85% | 15% | 1.4% |

Non-PBS retail prescription sales **more than doubled** between 2019 and 2024, with the primary drivers being anti-obesity medicines (GLP-1 receptor agonists) and medicinal cannabis. Wesfarmers, Woolworths, and telehealth platforms like Eucalyptus now offer telehealth consultations and direct dispensing of private prescriptions in a model that operates entirely outside the PBS ecosystem.

IQVIA notes that this trend is being reinforced by pharmaceutical company behaviour: payer pressure (including the increasing use by government of confidential rebates and special pricing arrangements under PBS managed entry agreements), coupled with the US Inflation Reduction Act's impact on global pricing strategy, is prompting companies to explore non-PBS revenue streams for medicines in mass-market chronic disease areas — particularly obesity and Alzheimer's disease — where listing on the PBS at cost-effective prices would require large discounts from international reference prices.

The result is a two-tier pharmaceutical market: subsidised access through the PBS for listed medicines, and a growing private market where access is determined by the ability to pay hundreds of dollars per month.

### 7. The 2022 Reforms and Ongoing Reform Pressures

The 2022 SAS reform — effective 3 July 2022 — was the most significant structural change to the framework in many years. Key changes included:

- **Online portal:** All SAS-A, B, and C applications and notifications moved to an online system, replacing paper submissions
- **Expanded SAS-C list:** More medicines with established safety profiles were added to the streamlined notification pathway
- **Expanded AP scheme:** Streamlined authorisation for Authorised Prescribers, reducing per-patient application burden

By October 2024, the SAS guidance was further updated to cover therapeutic vapes for smoking cessation, reflecting the rapid expansion of vaping as a prescription category following the 2024 regulatory changes.

The AMA's May 2025 submission to a separate TGA consultation raised structural concerns about the SAS being exploited by vertically integrated, direct-to-consumer telehealth entities — primarily in the medicinal cannabis space — operating in ways inconsistent with the scheme's clinical intent. The submission argued for stronger governance, clearer clinical standards, and enforcement mechanisms to prevent the SAS from functioning as a lightly regulated private prescribing channel.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**The equity paradox.** The SAS provides a legal route to medicine access, but that route is only navigable by patients with financial resources, specialist connections, or the luck of being enrolled at a centre that runs compassionate programs. There is no means-testing, no right of access, and no systematic equity mechanism.

**The compassionate access vacuum.** Australia has no national framework governing pharmaceutical company compassionate access programs — no registry, no mandatory disclosure, no eligibility standardisation, no equitable access requirement. Patients' ability to access pre-PBS medicines via company programs is entirely dependent on geography, the treating specialist, and the company's commercial decisions.

**When the safety valve becomes structural.** The SAS was not designed to handle 264,000+ annual submissions. The growth reflects both genuine patient need (the PBS access gap) and the emergence of entirely new markets (medicinal cannabis, private obesity prescribing) that use the SAS infrastructure for routine commercial activity. Reform to one thread may not address the others.

**The private medicine market and inequality.** At $460/month for Wegovy, effective obesity pharmacotherapy is available only to Australians who can afford it. The PBAC has recommended listing; the listing has not yet happened. Every month of delay is a month of inequitable access. The same logic applies to dozens of other medicines awaiting PBS entry.

**Company-driven access and information asymmetries.** Which patients know about compassionate programs? Typically, those who are at major urban specialist centres, with physicians who have relationships with industry. Rural, remote, and First Nations patients — who face higher disease burdens and less access to specialist care — are structurally less likely to receive information about or access to these programs.
