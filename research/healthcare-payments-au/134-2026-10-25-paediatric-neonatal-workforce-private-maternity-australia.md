# Paediatric and Neonatal Workforce in Private Maternity: The Twin Workforce Gap Completing the Picture of Private Maternity's Collapse

**Summary:** Private maternity care requires three specialist rosters operating around the clock: obstetricians, anaesthetists, and paediatricians or neonatologists. The previous report documented the anaesthesia workforce bottleneck. This report examines the paediatric and neonatal side of the same constraint — equally essential, less publicly discussed, and in some regions arguably the binding limit on whether a private maternity unit can continue operating at all. Paediatric workforce gaps have been explicitly named in multiple private maternity closures, the RACP reports wait times of 12–36 months for children in NSW and up to 24 months in Tasmania, and the private sector competes poorly against public hospitals for the same sparse supply of trained specialists.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 133 documented how anaesthetist shortages are a structural factor in private maternity unit closures. This report completes the twin-workforce picture: paediatricians and neonatologists are the other mandatory 24/7 presence, and their workforce pressures are at least as severe — and in regional areas, potentially more so.

---

## Background: The Regulatory Requirement for 24/7 Paediatric Cover

Like anaesthesia, paediatric and neonatal cover is not an amenity in private maternity — it is a clinical safety requirement with regulatory teeth. Every birthing unit in Australia is assessed against a *level of care* or *clinical services capability* framework that sets minimum staffing standards by clinical complexity.

Australia's neonatal services are classified on a six-tier scale under state-based Clinical Services Capability Frameworks (CSCFs), with Level 1 covering uncomplicated well-baby care and Level 6 reserved for national-level neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). Most private maternity hospitals operate at Level 4 — meaning they can care for babies born from around 32 weeks gestation and manage moderately unwell newborns — with a small number at Level 5 (capable of ventilated care for babies from 28–32 weeks). Level 6 services are exclusively public tertiary facilities.

The Queensland CSCF Level 4 neonatal requirements, for example, specify that a medical practitioner with paediatric experience must be reachable within 5 minutes when an infant is unstable or ventilated. The Level 4 also requires documented transfer arrangements with a higher-level public facility, and ongoing 24/7 paediatric-cover credentialling as a condition of hospital licensing.

This means a private hospital with a Level 4 SCN needs — at minimum — a credentialled paediatric on-call roster that can guarantee near-immediate response around the clock. As with the anaesthesia roster (Report 133), this is a fixed overhead that does not scale with birth volume. A unit doing 400 births per year has nearly the same 24/7 paediatric coverage requirement as one doing 1,200.

Private hospitals in Queensland also licence their Level 4 neonatal services separately from their overall maternity capability — meaning the SCN must meet its own standalone workforce and infrastructure standard. In practice, this means the paediatric roster is a separate accreditation risk from the midwifery and obstetric rosters: a hospital can fail its SCN re-accreditation independently of the rest of its maternity service.

## Key Findings

### 1. Paediatric and Neonatal Workforce Is Thin and Regionally Skewed

The available national data on paediatric and neonatal specialists in Australia is dated but directionally clear.

The most recent national snapshot from the National Health Workforce Dataset (2016) counted approximately **2,059 paediatricians** employed in Australia, of whom:
- **26.4% worked exclusively or primarily in the private sector**
- The remainder were in public hospitals or dual-sector roles.

Neonatologists are a sub-specialty within paediatrics. The most recent NSW-specific data (NSW Health neonatal/perinatal physician workforce modelling, 2019) reported:
- **47 neonatal/perinatal physician specialists** in NSW in 2019
- **26 advanced trainees** per year
- The modelling projected 2035 supply requirements would be met based on those 2019 trainee numbers — a projection that has not been updated, and that pre-dates the COVID-era workforce disruptions, the accelerating private maternity closure pattern, and the demographic pressure from Australia's ageing specialist cohort.

Nationally, the RACP does not publish current neonatologist headcounts. The RACP's August 2026 Workforce Insights report documents an ongoing regional specialist shortage across all physician types but does not provide neonatology-specific data. The RACP has repeatedly stated that it **does not cap trainee numbers or fund training positions** — meaning the pipeline is determined by hospital training posts and Commonwealth Specialist Training Program (STP) funding, not college intake caps.

As of October 2026, there is no public current national neonatal workforce census, no federal government neonatal-specific workforce modelling (comparable to the ASA anaesthesia modelling published in 2024), and no national neonatology workforce strategy.

*(Sources: Health Workforce Division NHWDS factsheet; NSW Health physician neonatal-perinatal workforce modelling factsheet, 2019; RACP Workforce Insights August 2026)*

### 2. Paediatric Wait Times Are Already Extreme, Compressing Private Availability

The RACP's June 2025 submission to the NSW Inquiry on Access to Quality Healthcare states directly: wait times to see a paediatrician in NSW, both in the public and private sectors, are often between **12 months and 3 years**, with the problem more pronounced in regional, rural and remote communities.

In Tasmania, the RACP's July 2025 media release called for urgent action, citing families waiting **up to 24 months** for paediatric care. The RACP identified the North West of Tasmania as a region with particularly acute specialist gaps — the same region served by the now-closed Hobart Private Hospital maternity unit.

These wait times describe outpatient paediatric care. Inpatient and on-call cover in private maternity is a different service, but it is delivered by the same sparse specialist cohort. Where demand for paediatric outpatient services is already running years beyond capacity, private hospitals recruiting for after-hours on-call rosters are competing for the attention of specialists who are already fully committed.

Recruitment listings confirm the mismatch. A RACP-listed advertisement for a visiting medical officer (VMO) in paediatrics/neonatology reflects the shift-to-model that private hospitals increasingly use when they cannot find a paediatrician willing to provide the traditional private practice arrangement alongside SCN cover. These VMO posts typically pay between $2,200 and $3,000 for a 24-hour shift — a rate that works for individual sessions but cannot build the stable roster of 4–6 specialists a unit needs for continuous cover.

*(Sources: RACP NSW submission, July 2025; RACP Tasmania media release, July 2025; RACP Workforce Insights August 2026; RACP VMO Paediatrics/Neonatology listing)*

### 3. Paediatric Gaps Have Been Named Directly in Private Maternity Closures

Unlike anaesthesia — where the connection to closures is documented but often inferred — paediatric and neonatal workforce gaps have been explicitly cited in the public announcements of several private maternity unit closures:

**Darwin Private Hospital (Healthscope, closed June 2025):** Healthscope's FAQ named "limited access to paediatricians, obstetricians, midwives, and at times anaesthetists to cover on-call and in-patient services safely" as a direct operational reason for closing. Darwin had no remaining private obstetrician by early 2026. The Northern Territory had no neonatologist outside the public Royal Darwin Hospital.

**St John of God Bunbury, WA:** The closure announcement cited "a chronic shortage of midwives and a lack of obstetricians, GP-obstetricians and paediatricians locally, and an overall decline in demand for private maternity services" — with paediatric workforce named directly alongside obstetric and midwifery supply.

**Glengarry Private Hospital, WA (Ramsay Health):** Ramsay consolidated Glengarry's maternity services into Joondalup Private in 2024, citing a "sustained shortage of on-call anaesthetists and paediatricians" at the Glengarry site — a clear statement of the twin-workforce dynamic from Report 133 and this report.

**Epworth Geelong, Victoria (2023):** The closure was disputed politically, with the Epworth group citing difficulties attracting paediatricians as a contributing factor. Independent analysis noted that the excuse of "not enough paediatricians in Geelong" reflected a real regional scarcity, even if local clinicians disputed it.

**St Vincent's Private Toowoomba (October 2025):** The hospital's special care nursery was temporarily closed for approximately one week due to staffing shortages — illustrating that even operational units face periodic paediatric coverage crises.

Private Healthcare Australia's CEO summarised the pattern in media coverage of the closure wave: the barriers to private maternity services include "the shortage of obstetricians, midwives, anaesthetists and paediatricians in some parts of Australia."

*(Sources: Darwin Private Hospital FAQ; St John of God media statements; Medical Forum WA; ABC News St Vincent's Toowoomba October 2025; Nous Group maternity closures analysis 2026)*

### 4. The Level-of-Care Ratchet: SCNs as a Liability Rather Than an Asset

For most private hospitals, the special care nursery (SCN) is not primarily a revenue generator — it is a clinical safety requirement for the maternity unit above it. A hospital cannot safely operate a birthing unit without the ability to manage a compromised newborn; the SCN is the facility that provides that capacity.

But maintaining an SCN accreditation creates costs disproportionate to the revenue it generates:
- **Infrastructure:** A Level 4 SCN requires 8–14 cots with appropriate monitoring, warming, and resuscitation equipment.
- **Nursing:** SCN nursing is a specialist post-registration competency, and SCN nurses are scarcer than general ward nurses.
- **Medical cover:** The credentialled paediatric on-call roster is a non-delegable requirement. Private hospitals cannot substitute a GP or a registrar for a credentialled consultant paediatrician in the on-call role for a Level 4 unit.
- **PHI complexity:** Under Australian PHI rules, a baby is only admitted in its own right — and only charges against PHI — if it requires treatment in a special care nursery or NICU. Routine newborn care (observation, feeding support, uncomplicated jaundice) is bundled into the mother's admission. This means the SCN must be operating at a meaningful clinical threshold to generate PHI revenue, and many admissions that a nurse or midwife might prefer to keep in-room are instead managed without triggering a SCN admission.

The result is that the SCN represents a fixed cost with irregular, hard-to-forecast PHI revenue that depends on sick baby admission patterns. When SCN admission rates drop (as they do when birth volumes fall), the unit becomes a net financial liability — and the paediatric roster that backs it consumes specialist goodwill for minimal commercial return.

Private Health Care Australia's August 2026 submission to the Department of Health's Private Health Reforms consultation explicitly identified this gap: it asked for insurer coverage of specialist and nurse practitioner services for newborns in the first three months after birth, regardless of whether care is delivered in hospital or in a community setting — a reform that would extend PHI value for neonates beyond the narrow "admitted to SCN" trigger.

*(Sources: QLD Health CSCF Level 4 neonatal templates; Mater Hospital PHI billing advice; PHA submission to Private Health Reforms Consultation Paper 1, August 2026)*

### 5. The National Maternity Workforce Strategy 2026–2036 Does Not Address Paediatrics or Neonatology

In December 2024, the Department of Health published the National Maternity Workforce Review Phase 1 report, led by Queensland Health, as an environmental scan of current maternity workforce strategies. This fed directly into the National Maternity Workforce Strategy 2026–2036, published in 2026.

The Strategy explicitly **excludes industrial matters and models of care**, and its primary focus is on midwives, obstetricians, GPs, and allied health professionals. Paediatricians and neonatologists are not named in its scope. There is no national neonatal workforce component to the Strategy.

This is a significant gap. As the analysis above makes clear, a national maternity workforce strategy that excludes the paediatric and neonatal specialist workforce cannot fully address the structural causes of private maternity closure. The three-workforce problem — obstetricians, anaesthetists, and paediatricians — requires a three-workforce solution, and current policy architecture only addresses two of the three.

The strategy does include recommendations on: improved workforce data collection, regional and rural supply incentives, and cultural safety in services. None of these mechanisms reach the specific structural problem of 24/7 private paediatric on-call roster viability.

*(Sources: National Maternity Workforce Review Phase 1, August 2024; National Maternity Workforce Strategy 2026–2036, health.gov.au)*

### 6. The Federal Response Has Been to Compensate the Public System, Not Repair the Private One

When private maternity units close, the immediate government response has been to fund the public facilities that absorb the displaced births — not to address the workforce or funding conditions that drove the private closure.

- **Darwin:** Following Darwin Private Hospital's June 2025 maternity closure, the federal government committed **$10 million** to upgrade the birthing, maternity, and neonatal intensive care unit at Royal Darwin Hospital, with construction expected to complete in late 2027. This expanded capacity for approximately 250 additional births per year in the public system — the number that had been delivered privately.

- **Hobart:** Following Healthscope's August 2025 closure of Hobart Private Hospital's maternity unit, the federal government committed **$6 million** to the Tasmanian Health Service and Calvary Lenah Valley to manage the expected increase in public demand.

Both responses demonstrate that Treasury and the Department of Health have implicitly accepted private-to-public birth transfers as an outcome to manage, rather than a market failure to prevent. Catholic Health Australia's September 2025 position paper on private maternity characterised this stance as a slow-motion collapse of a system: **18 private maternity units have closed since 2018, 13 in just the past three years**.

The federal government's July 2026 Private Health Sector Reform Consultation Paper 1 named maternity and mental health as the two most acute viability pressures in the private system — but did not propose paediatric workforce measures or SCN funding reforms. Consultation closed August 2026; no response had been published as at October 2026.

*(Sources: PM media release $10M Darwin, July 2026; CHA media release Hobart funding; CHA collapse of private maternity position paper; DoHDA Private Health Reforms Consultation Paper 1, July 2026)*

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Is paediatrics or anaesthesia the binding constraint?** In different locations, the answer differs. In Darwin, paediatric coverage was explicitly cited alongside anaesthesia; in Hobart, midwifery was the precipitating issue. In regional WA, the Glengarry closure cited both. There is no national data that would allow a ranking — and that data absence is itself a policy failure, given that the Commonwealth is funding public system responses without understanding which private workforce gap it is actually responding to.

**The double disadvantage of small private birth volumes:** As private birth volumes fall (down ~22% since 2012), each remaining private maternity unit has fewer births across which to spread the fixed cost of its paediatric roster. A paediatrician credentialled for SCN cover in a unit doing 400 births per year faces more unsocial-hours on-call per working week, and lower clinical volume per call, than they would in a larger public unit. This makes the private offer progressively less attractive as volumes fall — a self-reinforcing spiral.

**Who is training the next neonatologists?** Fellowship training in neonatal and perinatal medicine occurs almost entirely in large public Level 5 and 6 units. The Royal Children's Hospital, Royal Women's Hospital, and equivalent metropolitan public NICUs hold the accredited training posts. Private hospitals do not provide neonatology training. This means the pipeline for the specialist workforce that private hospitals need is entirely within the public system — and public sector employers have first claim on its output.

**The transfer capacity question, unanswered:** When a private Level 4 SCN closes or transfers a baby, they go to the nearest Level 5 or 6 unit — in practice, the major public tertiary hospital. But public NICU and Level 5 capacity is not unlimited, and neither the Commonwealth nor any state has published current modelling of public neonatal transfer-absorptive capacity. The 2019 NSW Health neonatal workforce model projected adequacy on 2019 assumptions; whether it holds in 2026 after 13 private maternity closures is unknown.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHI reform for private maternity and newborns**: The July–August 2026 consultation on Private Health Reforms explicitly addressed maternity and newborn coverage — examining what structural changes to insurer hospital contracts and newborn benefit design could revive private maternity viability.
2. **International models for funding neonatal on-call cover**: How the NHS (England), Canada, and New Zealand fund neonatal and paediatric on-call cover in birthing units — and whether a Commonwealth direct payment for 24/7 neonatal cover outside the MBS is feasible.
3. **ANZCA training pipeline and obstetric anaesthesia case requirements** (from Report 133): Whether accreditation frameworks for both anaesthesia and neonatology training are producing enough specialists with obstetric and neonatal-specific confidence.
4. **The public system absorption capacity**: State-by-state analysis of public NICU and Level 5 neonatal capacity and the plausibility of absorbing further private-to-public birth transfers, particularly in Tasmania and the NT.
5. **Birthing on Country and First Nations neonatal outcomes**: The intersection of the private maternity collapse with already-stretched regional services serving First Nations communities, and the federal government's Birthing on Country program's scope and funding.
