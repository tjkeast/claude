# Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder: Australia's $16 Billion Preventable Crisis and the Underfunded Response

**Report 066 — 2026-08-11**

**Summary:** Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) affects an estimated 3.64% of Australians — roughly 900,000 people — making it the most common preventable cause of neurodevelopmental disability in the country. A landmark June 2025 study by Tsang et al. placed Australia's general-population FASD prevalence at approximately one child in every classroom. The total economic burden is estimated at $16 billion per year. Yet the Commonwealth's primary FASD infrastructure — the FASD Hub Australia — has operated on funding that expires in July 2026, the government took more than three years to respond to the 2021 Senate inquiry's 32 recommendations, mandatory pregnancy warning labels on alcohol products achieved only 36% correct-design compliance within the RTD market two years after their introduction, and the diagnostic pathway remains expensive, sparse in rural areas, and opaque in the MBS. FASD is the direct downstream consequence of the alcohol use disorder treatment gap documented in Report 065 — a preventable disability generated in part by a healthcare payment system that provides almost no financial signal to identify and treat hazardous drinking in pregnant women.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 065 documented that only 2.9% of Australians with alcohol use disorder receive PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy, that there is no dedicated MBS brief intervention item, and that the average delay from AUD onset to treatment is 18 years. Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder is the direct population-level consequence: when alcohol dependence and hazardous drinking in women of reproductive age go unidentified and untreated, and when the public understanding of in-utero alcohol risk remains poor, FASD births are the inevitable result. This report examines the prevalence, funding architecture, diagnostic pathway, and prevention system for Australia's most under-resourced major neurodevelopmental condition.

## Background

### What Is FASD?

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder is an umbrella term for the range of conditions arising from prenatal exposure to alcohol — a teratogen and neurotoxin for which no safe lower limit of exposure during pregnancy has been established. FASD is not a single diagnosis but a spectrum: it includes conditions formerly described separately (fetal alcohol syndrome, partial FAS, alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder, alcohol-related birth defects) that are now unified under the FASD classification under the 2025 Australian Guidelines for Assessment and Diagnosis released 13 May 2025.

The defining features of FASD are **neurodevelopmental impairment** — affecting memory, attention, executive function, language, adaptive behaviour, and motor skills — rather than the physical features (small head circumference, facial features) that characterised older, narrower clinical definitions. This shift is clinically significant: most people with FASD do not have visible physical differences, making them "invisible" to untrained observers, profoundly misunderstood by teachers, justice workers, and health professionals, and chronically undiagnosed.

**Key characteristics:**
- Lifelong condition with no cure; effects are not reversible
- 100% preventable — exclusively caused by prenatal alcohol exposure
- Neurodevelopmental impairment affects nine functional domains under the 2025 guidelines; diagnosis requires severe impairment in at least three
- High comorbidity: ADHD, anxiety, depression, conduct disorder, intellectual disability
- Dramatically elevated risk of secondary adverse outcomes: school failure, disengagement from employment, homelessness, justice system contact, suicide

### The 2025 Diagnostic Guidelines

On **13 May 2025**, the **Australian Guidelines for Assessment and Diagnosis of Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder** were released, approved by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC). These guidelines — developed by the Child Health Research Centre at the University of Queensland through extensive stakeholder consultation — supersede the 2016 guidelines (updated 2020) and include:

- **40 good practice statements** and **18 implementation considerations**
- 11 embedded statements from people with lived experience of FASD
- A novel GRADE-based approach to evidence-based diagnostic criteria
- Explicit support for shared decision-making and strengths-based, person-centred assessment
- Expanded recognition of FASD across the lifespan (including adults, not only children)

These guidelines are the most significant clinical development in Australian FASD policy in a decade. However, their implementation requires multidisciplinary diagnostic teams and specialist services that are sparse outside major metropolitan centres — and the payment system provides no dedicated remuneration for FASD-specific assessment.

## Key Findings

### 1. Prevalence: One Child in Every Classroom

The most significant FASD research development in Australia in 2025 was the publication of **Tsang et al. (2025)** in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (published June 2025; doi:10.1111/dar.14082). Led by the late Dr Tracey Tsang from the University of Sydney, this landmark study provided the **first systematic estimate of FASD prevalence in the general Australian population**.

**Method:** The study combined published Australian data on rates of alcohol consumption during pregnancy with an international meta-analysis of 78 studies (conducted between 1975 and 2018) using Monte Carlo simulations to determine confidence intervals.

**Headline finding:** Estimated FASD prevalence in the general Australian population: **3.64%** (95% confidence interval: 2.91%–4.41%).

This equates to approximately:
- **1 in 28 Australians** — or one child in every average-sized school classroom
- Approximately **900,000 people** currently living with FASD in Australia (based on adult and child population aged 0+)
- A higher prevalence estimate than Canada (estimated 2–3%), reflecting Australia's comparatively high rates of alcohol consumption during pregnancy

The study authors — including Professor Elizabeth Elliott from the University of Sydney and researchers from the Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care — were careful to note that the estimate is modelled rather than case-ascertained, and that it is likely an underestimate given the known limitations of self-reported prenatal alcohol exposure data.

**Population sub-groups with substantially higher prevalence:**

The general population estimate of 3.64% masks extreme variation:

- **Youth detention:** The landmark 2018 study at Banksia Hill Detention Centre in Western Australia (Bower et al.) found that **36% of young people in youth detention** had FASD — one of the highest detention-population rates recorded globally. Of the broader detention cohort, **89% had at least one form of severe neurodevelopmental impairment**.

- **Remote Aboriginal communities:** The Lililwan Project, conducted in the Fitzroy Valley region of Western Australia, found a FASD prevalence of **19.44 per 100 children** (approximately 1 in 5) — Australia's only case-ascertained prevalence study in a defined community. The Lililwan Project was led by Aboriginal community leaders from the Fitzroy Valley in collaboration with The George Institute for Global Health and the University of Sydney.

- **Child protection:** Research has found that approximately **70% of children with FASD** in clinical samples have had contact with the child protection system.

- **Adults in contact with justice system:** People with FASD are estimated to be up to **19 times more likely to be incarcerated** compared to their peers without neurodevelopmental disability.

### 2. The Economic Burden: $16 Billion Per Year

The most recent comprehensive estimate, cited by **NOFASD Australia** (updated June 2025), places the **total economic burden of FASD in Australia at approximately $16 billion per year**. This figure encompasses:

- Direct healthcare costs: hospitalisation, assessment, treatment of FASD-related conditions, comorbidity management
- Education system costs: special educational needs, teacher aides, modified curricula, non-completion
- Criminal justice costs: disproportionate incarceration, legal aid, court processes, supervision
- Social welfare costs: income support, homelessness services, residential care
- Lost economic productivity: employment exclusion, underemployment, carer burden

The contrast with prevention investment is stark. Research synthesised by the Australian Drug and Alcohol Research Connections found that **$14 is returned in health and social care savings for every $1 invested in preventive health programs** — a ratio that makes FASD prevention among the most cost-effective possible health investments. Yet the total Commonwealth investment in FASD programs across the life of the 2018–2028 Strategic Action Plan has amounted to tens of millions of dollars — a rounding error relative to the annual $16 billion economic impact.

Australian researchers at the Telethon Kids Institute have been working to produce a formal Australian economic burden study, as recommended in the 2021 Senate inquiry report — but as of mid-2026, a comprehensive published cost-of-illness analysis specific to Australia remains outstanding.

### 3. The Prevention System: Mandatory Labels, Awareness Campaigns, and Their Limits

FASD is 100% preventable. The Australian Government's NHMRC guidelines (2020) state clearly: **no safe level of alcohol consumption during pregnancy has been identified**. The practical prevention question is: how does the payment and regulatory system support this message?

#### 3a. Mandatory Pregnancy Warning Labels — Compliance Failure

In **July 2020**, after more than two decades of self-regulation under voluntary industry schemes (primarily DrinkWise), Australia and New Zealand introduced **mandatory pregnancy warning labels** on packaged alcoholic beverages. This followed a December 2019 ministerial decision after extensive lobbying from public health advocates and against coordinated industry opposition.

The mandatory label requires a specific design: a circular graphic with a pregnant figure crossed out, accompanied by the text "Any amount of alcohol can harm your baby." The label must meet minimum size and contrast requirements.

**Compliance has been suboptimal:**

- A 2024 study in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Sträuli et al., doi:10.1111/dar.13758) examined ready-to-drink (RTD) products two years into the three-year implementation period. Of the 94% of products that had *some* form of pregnancy warning, **only 36% displayed the correct mandatory design**. The remainder used non-mandatory labels — 74% of non-compliant products used the DrinkWise voluntary label, which researchers have consistently found to be suboptimally sized, grey-scale, and insufficiently clear.

- A June 2025 study in the *Australian and New Zealand Journal of Public Health* found that **11% of products displaying the mandatory label did not do so correctly**, with compliance lowest for spirits (73% correct design), followed by wine (90%), beer (94%), and premix (97%).

- Research on alcohol industry submissions to the labelling consultation found that industry actors made systematic arguments against mandatory labels that public health researchers characterised as misrepresenting evidence and exaggerating commercial costs.

**Significance:** The mandatory label is a population-level signal that FASD prevention relies on at the point of sale and consumption. Its widespread non-compliance — with industry favouring less prominent voluntary labels — means the primary regulatory prevention tool is being systematically undermined. There is no financial penalty in the public health system for this failure; enforcement falls to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) under the Food Standards Code, not to the health payment system.

#### 3b. The Strong Born Campaign

**Strong Born** is a First Nations-led national FASD prevention campaign developed in collaboration with the National Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation (NACCHO) and participating ACCHOs. Published research (PMC, 2024) describes Strong Born as a first-of-its-kind national campaign designed to be culturally safe and community-directed.

The campaign reflects the particular importance of culturally appropriate FASD prevention in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where rates of prenatal alcohol exposure and FASD prevalence are substantially higher than the national average, and where standard public health campaigns have limited reach or cultural resonance.

#### 3c. Prevention Funding as a Proportion of Total Spend

Prevention spending in Australia has remained below **2% of the total health budget** throughout the 2018–2028 Strategic Action Plan period — despite the National Preventive Health Strategy 2021–2030 proposing to lift this to 5% of health spend by 2030. FASD-specific prevention programs receive a fraction of this already-underfunded prevention budget.

### 4. The Diagnostic Pathway and Payment Architecture

#### 4a. Who Diagnoses FASD?

Under the **2025 Australian Guidelines**, FASD diagnosis requires a **multidisciplinary team (MDT)** assessment encompassing:

- A **medical practitioner** (paediatrician, child psychiatrist, or general practitioner with FASD training) to confirm prenatal alcohol exposure history and rule out other conditions
- A **psychologist** to assess cognitive and neurodevelopmental function across the nine domains
- Often additional clinicians: speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, physiotherapist (depending on the profile of impairment)

The full MDT assessment process typically spans **multiple appointments over weeks or months**, involves extensive standardised testing, and generates comprehensive clinical documentation needed for diagnosis and NDIS or education system access.

**Barriers to MDT assessment:**
- Highly specialised — there is no dedicated training pathway for FASD clinicians in Australia; expertise is concentrated in researchers and clinical champions
- Geographically concentrated: MDT diagnostic clinics exist primarily in capital cities and major regional centres, funded by a mix of public hospital funding (state and territory), research grants, and out-of-pocket private fees
- Waiting lists: available evidence suggests multi-month waits for FASD diagnostic services in publicly funded clinics; no national waiting list data is collected

#### 4b. MBS Coverage for FASD Assessment

From **1 March 2023**, FASD was added to the list of eligible disabilities for the **MBS complex neurodevelopmental conditions and eligible disabilities items**. This is a significant recent reform that enables:

- Specialist and paediatric assessment items to be billed for FASD-related assessments
- Allied health services (psychology, occupational therapy, speech pathology) to attract Medicare rebates when attached to a qualifying medical practitioner item
- From **1 July 2025**, psychology assessment sessions attract a rebate of approximately **$101.55 per 50+ minute session** for individuals under 25

However, the MBS coverage is partial and complex:
- There is no **dedicated FASD assessment item** — FASD is accessed as an "eligible disability" within a general neurodevelopmental framework shared with autism, intellectual disability, and ADHD
- The allied health rebate covers individual sessions but not the full cost of a comprehensive MDT assessment (which may involve many hours of testing, report-writing, and coordination time not fully captured in individual session items)
- Out-of-pocket costs for a comprehensive private FASD assessment can still reach **$2,000–$5,000**, depending on the complexity and number of professionals involved
- **GPs have no dedicated MBS item** for FASD screening, history-taking for prenatal alcohol exposure, or referral coordination — a structural gap parallel to the absent AUD brief intervention item documented in Report 065

#### 4c. NDIS Access for FASD

FASD is classified under the NDIS as a congenital condition causing permanent impairment. Access rules:

- **Under 7 years:** Automatic NDIS eligibility under the Early Childhood Early Intervention (ECEI) pathway. No formal FASD diagnosis required — developmental concerns trigger support without a diagnosis.
- **Over 7 years:** NDIS access requires evidence of **severe functional impairment** across multiple domains. This requires a formal FASD diagnosis and a detailed functional impact assessment.

The NOFASD Australia submission to the **NDIS Amendment Bill 2026** raised concerns that proposed changes to NDIS eligibility criteria and the introduction of more stringent "needs assessment" frameworks could make it harder for people with FASD — particularly those with "invisible" presentations without physical disability markers — to access and maintain NDIS plans. The submission argued that:

- The episodic and context-dependent nature of FASD's functional impacts is poorly captured by standardised assessment tools designed for more static disabilities
- Adults with undiagnosed FASD (a substantial population, given the 3.64% prevalence in the general population vs the number of formal NDIS participants with FASD) are particularly at risk of exclusion from reformed eligibility frameworks
- The interaction between FASD and commonly co-occurring disabilities (ADHD, ASD, intellectual disability) creates complexity in NDIS plan design that requires specialist understanding that assessors may not have

### 5. The Senate Inquiry and Government Response

The **Senate Community Affairs References Committee** ran an inquiry into "Effective approaches to prevention, diagnosis and support for Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder" between 2019 and 2021, releasing its final report on **17 March 2021**. The committee described FASD as an "invisible epidemic" in Australia — well known to researchers but poorly understood in the community, health system, justice system, and school system.

**32 recommendations** covered:
- Establishment of a national FASD prevalence study
- Independent economic burden study
- National public education campaign
- Expanded FASD diagnostic capacity, particularly in rural and remote areas
- Development of culturally safe First Nations FASD services
- Long-term data collection including a national FASD case register
- Mandatory pregnancy warning labels (predating the 2020 implementation)
- NDIS recognition and access reform
- Training for criminal justice, child protection, and education workforces

**Government response — released December 2024**, more than **three and a half years** after the report:

The response acknowledged the 32 recommendations and set out the government's positions — agreed, agreed in principle, partially agreed, and noted. Key elements:

- Agreed in principle to a **national FASD prevalence study** — but the Tsang et al. 2025 study, a modelling study rather than case ascertainment, partially addresses this recommendation
- Agreed in principle to an **independent economic burden study** — but no study had been funded and published as of mid-2026
- Noted the launch of the **$37.2 million** FASD awareness and diagnostic program (announced by Minister Greg Hunt), the largest single FASD funding commitment in Australian history
- Confirmed ongoing funding for the FASD Hub Australia (to July 2026) and the updated diagnostic guidelines process (completed May 2025)
- Additional $2.1 million for the PATCHES diagnostic services program in NSW youth justice (2021–22 onwards)

The delay in government response — 3.5 years — was criticised by NOFASD Australia and the broader FASD advocacy community as emblematic of the low political priority accorded to FASD relative to its scale and economic impact.

### 6. The FASD Hub Australia: Infrastructure at Risk

The **FASD Hub Australia** is the primary national FASD knowledge translation and information infrastructure, funded by the Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care and operated through The Kids Research Institute Australia (formerly Telethon Kids Institute). It provides:

- Clinical guidelines and resources for practitioners
- Information for families and people with lived experience
- A listing of FASD diagnostic services across Australia
- Training and professional development resources

**Funding concern:** The FASD Hub's government funding extended to **July 2026**. A **FASD Hub Australia Strategic Plan 2026–2030** was published in January 2026, outlining a four-year trajectory of stabilisation, growth, and optimisation — but the confirmation of government funding for this next phase had not been publicly announced as of mid-2026. The Hub was operating on the assumption of continuity; the advocacy community was watching closely.

### 7. Structural Tensions and Open Questions

**The abstinence-only prevention message and its limits**

The "no safe level" message — accurate — creates a binary communication that may paradoxically reduce engagement among women who have already consumed alcohol early in pregnancy before knowing they are pregnant (as many do). Approximately 40% of pregnancies in Australia are unplanned; alcohol consumption before pregnancy recognition is common. Public health communicators and practitioners grapple with how to balance the accuracy of "no safe level" with the anxiety and stigma this can generate in women who have already been exposed — potentially deterring them from disclosing to clinicians or accessing support.

**Under-detection in the health system**

GP clinical training on FASD remains inadequate. The diagnostic guidelines were released in May 2025; how quickly practising clinicians adopt them is unclear. There is no routine prenatal screening for alcohol use (equivalent to antenatal depression screening) embedded in the MBS antenatal items. The AUDIT-C — the standard brief screen for alcohol misuse — is not systematically administered in antenatal care despite evidence supporting its use and the NHMRC guidelines' "no safe level" advice.

**The criminal justice system as FASD's principal de facto funder**

With 36% of young people in youth detention meeting FASD criteria and people with FASD up to 19 times more likely to be incarcerated, the criminal justice system is, in effect, Australia's largest FASD support service — one that provides none of the therapeutic structure FASD requires and all of the criminogenic risk factors that FASD makes people vulnerable to. The annual cost of incarcerating a young person in Australia ($1,000+ per day) vastly exceeds the cost of specialist FASD support, accommodation, and therapeutic services.

**First Nations communities: the intersection of FASD and systemic disadvantage**

The 19% FASD prevalence in the Fitzroy Valley is not simply a product of high prenatal alcohol exposure — it reflects the intersection of systemic disadvantage, limited access to AUD treatment, geographic isolation, socioeconomic marginalisation, and the downstream effects of colonisation on health behaviours. The FASD rate in remote First Nations communities is a diagnostic marker for the failure of AUD treatment and prevention systems documented throughout this series.
