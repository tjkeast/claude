# Australia's Tobacco and Nicotine Payment System: Excise Collapse, Vaping Disruption, and the Cessation Access Gap

**Report 070 — 2026-08-15**

**Summary:** Australia built the world's most effective tobacco control system over four decades — plain packaging, high excise taxes, mass media campaigns, Quitline, and subsidised pharmacotherapy — and it drove adult daily smoking from 24% in 1991 to 8.3% in 2022–23. But the payment and regulatory architecture of that system is now fracturing simultaneously on two fronts: a black market tobacco crisis in which illicit cigarettes account for an estimated 25–80% of the market, costing the government $7–12 billion annually in evaded excise; and a vaping disruption in which Australia's prescription-only therapeutic framework is simultaneously suppressing youth nicotine initiation and creating barriers to adult cessation access. This report maps both crises, examines the PBS and MBS payment architecture for smoking cessation, and identifies where the system's incentive structure is failing.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 069 examined the ACT's drug decriminalisation scheme and its key structural tension: that redirecting people from criminal justice into healthcare only works if the health system has capacity. The same structural logic applies to tobacco: Australia's world-leading regulatory approach works when the formal system can reach people — but when the legal supply chain is displaced by black market cigarettes and enforcement is overwhelmed, the public health gains begin to unravel in ways the healthcare payment system cannot easily correct.

---

## Background: Australia's World-Leading Tobacco Control Architecture

### The Four Pillars

Australia's tobacco control system is built on four mutually reinforcing pillars, all of which involve healthcare payments or government expenditure:

**1. Excise taxation:** Australia taxes tobacco at among the highest rates in the world. From March to August 2026, the rate for cigarettes not exceeding 0.8g of tobacco per stick is **$1.53 per stick**. Excise rates are indexed twice yearly to the Average Weekly Ordinary Time Earnings (AWOTE) and received additional 5% above-indexation increases from September 2023 for three years. A pack of 25 cigarettes at the full excise rate carries an embedded tax of approximately $38 — though the retail price, which varies with brand positioning, typically runs $40–55 for a branded pack of 25 in legal channels.

**2. Plain packaging:** Introduced on 1 December 2012, Australia was the first country in the world to mandate standardised plain packaging for all tobacco products. Combined with enlarged graphical health warnings (GHWs), plain packaging is now widely credited with a 3.7% relative reduction in adult smoking prevalence, equivalent to approximately two years of additional trend decline. The WTO upheld Australia's plain packaging measures in 2018 after a landmark dispute initiated by Ukraine, Honduras, Dominican Republic, Cuba, and Indonesia (with US industry backing). Over 25 countries have since implemented plain packaging or standardised packaging.

**3. Mass media campaigns and Quitline:** The Commonwealth funds mass media antismoking campaigns and subsidises state-based Quitlines through the Department of Health and Aged Care. Quitline services — telephone counselling with evidence-based brief interventions and often free NRT mailed to participants — are co-funded by states and territories. Their funding levels vary significantly across jurisdictions, creating unequal cessation support access.

**4. PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy:** The Commonwealth subsidises three categories of smoking cessation pharmacotherapy through the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS): nicotine replacement therapy (NRT, including patches, gum, lozenges, and mouth spray), varenicline (brand name: Champix), and bupropion (brand name: Zyban). All three are listed on the PBS subject to clinical criteria, meaning a concession-card holder pays $7.70 per prescription course, and a general-patient pays $31.60 (post-January 2026 co-payment rates). Each pharmacotherapy category is available for up to 12 weeks per year (extended to 24 weeks for varenicline under a 2024 PBS change).

### The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030

The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030, released by the Commonwealth in May 2023, sets two headline targets:
- **Less than 10%** adult daily smoking prevalence by 2025
- **5% or less** adult daily smoking prevalence by 2030
- For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people: **27% or less** daily smoking by 2030 (from a 2018–19 baseline of 37.4%)

The strategy identifies six priority areas: preventing uptake; supporting cessation; protecting people from tobacco smoke; regulating tobacco and nicotine products (explicitly including e-cigarettes); addressing illicit tobacco; and reducing inequalities. The vaping and illicit tobacco crises are both explicitly within the strategy's remit — but the strategy does not come with dedicated new Commonwealth funding. Instead, it relies on existing program budgets plus the enforcement commitments made separately in successive federal budgets.

### Smoking Rate Trajectory

The long-term decline in daily smoking is one of Australia's most significant public health achievements:
- 24% of Australians aged 14+ smoked daily in 1991
- 11.0% in 2019 (NDSHS)
- **8.3%** in 2022–23 (NDSHS, published February 2024)
- **5.6%** in 2025 (cited in government media releases citing AIHW monitoring data — preliminary, and representing a significant acceleration in decline)

The 2022–23 figure placed Australia fifth lowest among OECD nations for adult daily smoking prevalence (compared to the OECD average of 14.8%). If the 2025 preliminary figure of 5.6% is confirmed in the next NDSHS, Australia will have substantially met its 2030 target ahead of schedule — a remarkable outcome.

But this headline success story sits alongside two systemic failures that are eroding the fiscal and regulatory foundations of the model.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Illicit Tobacco Crisis: A $7–12 Billion Black Market Eating the Excise Base

Australia's high-excise model has an Achilles heel: as the legal retail price of cigarettes rose into the $40–55 per pack range, the price differential between legal and illicit products created a criminal market of extraordinary scale.

**The scale of the problem:**
- The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) estimates illicit tobacco accounted for approximately **25% of total tobacco consumed** in 2023–24, up from about 8% in 2018–19
- Independent estimates, including analysis published by the *Reason* magazine in June 2026, put the illicit share as high as **80% of cigarettes consumed** in 2025–26 — a claim the government disputes but which reflects the collapse of excise revenue
- The **excise tax gap** on illicit tobacco in 2024–25 is estimated by the ATO at the lower bound, with independent analysts estimating **$7.7–$11.8 billion** in annual excise duty foregone
- Illicit cigarettes retail for **$10–20 per pack** (versus $40–55 legal), making them price-competitive at roughly one-quarter to one-half the regulated price

**The revenue collapse:**
- At peak (2019–20), tobacco excise revenue reached approximately **$16 billion**
- By 2024–25, it had fallen to approximately **$7 billion**
- The 2025–26 Budget projection is just **$4.9 billion** — barely one-third of the peak
- The Commonwealth Treasury has downgraded tobacco excise revenue by approximately **$8 billion** over the forward estimates

This is fiscal damage that is difficult to overstate: the tobacco excise base was one of Australia's most reliable revenue streams for decades. Its collapse is structurally connected to the excise policy itself — as rates increased by ~50% between 2020 and 2025, they simultaneously compressed the legal market and expanded the criminal incentive to supply illicit alternatives.

**What the illicit market looks like:**
- Illicit tobacco arrives primarily from Southeast Asia and the Middle East, often as loose-leaf "chop-chop" or pre-rolled counterfeit branded cigarettes
- Supply chains are deeply entangled with organised crime: the ATO and Home Affairs have documented that most illicit tobacco syndicates operating in Australia maintain diversified criminal businesses across drug trafficking, money laundering, and extortion
- Domestic cultivation has also been suppressed (ATO Illicit Tobacco Team operations between 2018–2025 located, seized, and destroyed tobacco crops across 647 acres), but import smuggling through sea freight containers and postal channels has expanded faster than enforcement capacity

**The government response:**
- The **Illicit Tobacco Taskforce** — a multi-agency collaboration involving the ATO, Australian Border Force (ABF), Australian Federal Police (AFP), and Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions (CDPP) — has been operating since 2018
- Between July 2018 and June 2025, the Taskforce completed 97 operational activities with an estimated $782 million in excise duty equivalent seized or prevented
- The 2025–26 Budget allocated an additional **$156.7 million** to strengthen enforcement powers, including asset and profit seizure authorities
- Total government investment in illicit tobacco enforcement since the problem emerged: approximately **$345 million**
- ABF's **Operation PRINTWALL** in March–April 2026 surpassed one kilotonne of illicit tobacco detected, with 87 tonnes seized in a single week in April 2026

The enforcement investment, however, remains dwarfed by the revenue loss: $345 million in cumulative enforcement against a $7–12 billion per year revenue gap represents about 3–4 cents in enforcement for every dollar lost. The government has also reached a bilateral agreement with China (June 2026) to cooperate on border enforcement targeting illicit tobacco and e-cigarette smuggling — the first such mention in a joint communiqué.

**The policy dilemma:**
An independent analysis by the e61 Institute (a Canberra-based economic research body) argued in 2025 that the government should consider *reducing* tobacco excise rates to close the price differential that makes illicit supply profitable. The government rejected this: reducing excise rates would signal retreat on tobacco control, potentially re-normalise smoking, and undermine Australia's international reputation as a leader in tobacco harm reduction. The alternative — sustained enforcement without excise reduction — requires a multi-year commitment of resources that, so far, has not arrested the trend.

### 2. The Vaping Disruption: Therapeutic Framework vs. Youth Epidemic vs. Cessation Tool

Australia's response to the e-cigarette challenge has been among the most aggressive regulatory responses in the world — more restrictive than the UK, EU, US, or New Zealand — and the results are mixed.

**The regulatory chronology:**
- **October 2021:** TGA classified nicotine e-cigarettes as therapeutic goods, requiring a prescription for personal importation of nicotine vaping products. This made Australia the first major jurisdiction to require prescriptions for nicotine vapes
- **January 2024:** Import ban on all nicotine-containing disposable vapes; from 1 March 2024, import licence required from the Office of Drug Control for all e-cigarettes including non-nicotine products
- **July 2024:** Single-use (disposable) vapes banned from sale in Australia; therapeutic vaping products for cessation/nicotine dependence restricted to pharmacy supply only (removed from tobacconists, convenience stores)
- **October 2024:** Products with ≤20mg/mL nicotine can be supplied by pharmacists without prescription (pharmacist-assessed supply), for adults aged 18+ seeking to quit smoking or manage nicotine dependence; higher concentrations still require a prescription
- **July 2025:** Strengthened TGA product standards (TGO 110) came into force — non-compliant products removed from the notified vape list; flavours restricted to mint, menthol, and tobacco only; maximum nicotine concentration 50mg/mL; child-resistant packaging; no promotional colouring or branding

**The youth vaping impact:**
The peak of Australia's youth vaping crisis occurred in 2022–23, driven almost entirely by cheap, brightly-coloured, heavily-flavoured disposable vapes imported from China, sold through convenience stores and tobacconists without age verification. The NDSHS 2022–23 found that:
- **30%** of secondary school students had ever vaped
- **16%** had vaped in the past month (up from 3% in previous years)
- Over 1 in 4 people aged 14–17 had used e-cigarettes at least once in their lifetime
- Nearly 1 in 2 people aged 18–24 had used them at least once

Post-ban data shows significant improvement. Government monitoring from early 2025 reports:
- Daily vaping among 14–17-year-olds fell from approximately 18% in early 2023 to **15%** in early 2025
- Daily vaping among 18–24-year-olds fell from 20% to 18%
- SAHMRI data for South Australia showed vaping rates among 15–29-year-olds reduced by approximately **one-third** between 2023 and 2024
- Peer exposure to vaping among young people fell from 14.9% (2023) to 7.6% (2025) — a stronger indicator of normalisation reversal
- TGA and ABF seized **more than 10 million illicit vapes** between January 2024 and early 2025, with an estimated street value of approximately **$500 million**

The government and Health Minister Butler described this as proof that Australia's laws were working. Independent research published in the *Medical Journal of Australia* in 2025 (interrupted time series analysis of adolescent smoking and vaping trends 1999–2023) confirmed that vaping had interrupted the long decline in adolescent smoking — a "turning back" of public health gains — but that post-ban monitoring shows the trajectory reversing.

**The therapeutic cessation problem:**
Here the policy creates a genuine tension. The 2024–25 regulatory framework is designed to preserve vaping as a legitimate cessation tool for adult smokers while eliminating recreational use and youth access. But several structural problems undermine this:

- **No TGA product approval:** As of mid-2026, no vaping goods manufacturer has submitted a product dossier to the TGA for full inclusion in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG). The TGA has not evaluated *any* therapeutic vaping good for safety, quality, effectiveness, or performance through its standard evidence-based approval process. Pharmacy supply is authorised under a separate "notified" pathway — a lower regulatory standard that allows supply without full TGA evaluation
- **No PBS listing:** Therapeutic vaping products are not listed on the PBS. An adult smoker who purchases a therapeutic vaping product through a pharmacy pays the full retail cost out of pocket — approximately $20–60 per device, with ongoing cartridge or e-liquid costs. A smoker using PBS-listed NRT, varenicline, or bupropion pays $7.70–$31.60 per prescription. The cost differential creates a two-tier cessation market where the least-studied cessation tool carries the highest out-of-pocket cost
- **Second-line status in clinical guidelines:** The RACGP and clinical guidelines position therapeutic vaping as a **second-line** cessation support — to be considered only for patients who have been unable to quit using first-line therapies (NRT combination therapy, varenicline). This is clinically appropriate given the evidence base, but it means vaping occupies an unusual regulatory space: legal and accessible via pharmacy, but not recommended as a first choice and not subsidised
- **GP prescribing hesitancy:** An NDARC survey of Australian GPs found widespread uncertainty about prescribing intentions for therapeutic vaping products, even after the pharmacy supply change. GPs reported concerns about the absence of TGA approval, uncertainty about product quality, and lack of clinical training. The pharmacist-assessed pathway partially bypasses this, but creates its own concerns about assessment quality in busy retail pharmacy contexts

**The clinical evidence:** A 2025 RACGP guide for health professionals acknowledged that nicotine vaping products can be effective for smoking cessation in adults who have not responded to approved first-line therapies, citing the evidence base from the UK (where vaping is an NHS-endorsed cessation tool) and multiple RCTs. However, long-term safety data for vaping products remains limited, and Australia's regulatory caution reflects this uncertainty.

### 3. PBS Cessation Pharmacotherapy: What's Subsidised, What Isn't, and the Gap

The PBS currently funds three smoking cessation pharmacotherapy classes:

| Medicine | PBS Status | Typical PBS Co-pay (2026) | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| NRT (patch, gum, lozenge, spray) | Listed | $7.70 (conc.) / $31.60 (gen.) | Up to 12 weeks/year; retreatment course added post-2024 PBAC recommendation |
| Varenicline (Champix/generic) | Listed | $7.70 (conc.) / $31.60 (gen.) | Up to 24 weeks/year (extended from 12 weeks) |
| Bupropion (Zyban) | Listed | $7.70 (conc.) / $31.60 (gen.) | Up to 12 weeks/year |

**What is not PBS-listed:**
- **Cytisine:** A plant-derived alkaloid used successfully in Eastern and Central Europe for 50+ years, with a strong evidence base as an effective, inexpensive cessation aid. An Australian RCT comparing cytisine to varenicline failed to demonstrate non-inferiority, complicating a PBAC submission. Cytisine remains unlisted in Australia despite its low cost and established international use
- **Therapeutic nicotine vaping products:** Not on the PBS; full out-of-pocket cost for patients
- **Combination NRT** (simultaneous use of patch + fast-acting formulation): Now explicitly recommended in guidelines and supported by a 2024 PBAC recommendation for expanded access, but the listing parameters are still being updated

**The coverage access gap:** Even with PBS subsidies, a significant majority of Australian smokers who want to quit do not access pharmacotherapy. Australia has approximately 1.6 million daily smokers as of 2025 estimates. Annual PBS prescriptions for all three cessation pharmacotherapy categories are in the hundreds of thousands — a meaningful treatment gap. Barriers to pharmacotherapy access include: GP consultation costs (a gap fee to see the GP who writes the PBS prescription); low awareness of PBS subsidies among smokers; and an evidence-practitioner gap where GPs do not routinely discuss cessation at every consultation.

A systematic issue identified in the National Tobacco Strategy is that Australia's model historically relied heavily on taxation to reduce demand, and less on active cessation investment. The UK spends roughly £10 per capita on smoking cessation services annually; Australia's comparable figure, across PBS pharmacotherapy and state Quitlines, is estimated to be significantly lower on a per-smoker basis.

### 4. The Fiscal Architecture: Who Pays for Tobacco Control?

The tobacco control payment system involves multiple government actors with distinct funding streams:

**Federal government:**
- Collects excise (currently ~$4.9 billion/year, declining)
- Funds PBS-listed cessation pharmacotherapy
- Funds mass media campaigns (expenditure not publicly disaggregated from total health promotion budget)
- Funds enforcement (Illicit Tobacco Taskforce, ABF border operations)

**State and territory governments:**
- Co-fund Quitlines (with Commonwealth)
- Fund quit-smoking campaigns at state level
- Regulate retail point-of-sale environments
- Prosecute tobacco-related offences under public health legislation

**Private sector / patients:**
- Smokers pay the world's highest tobacco excise, embedded in cigarette prices — a de facto health tax collected through the tobacco industry
- Patients pay PBS co-payments for pharmacotherapy
- Patients pay out of pocket for therapeutic vaping products (no subsidy)
- The illicit tobacco market transfers a portion of what would be excise revenue into criminal enterprises

The structural irony is significant: Australia's tobacco control model is funded substantially *by* smokers themselves through excise revenue. As the smoking rate falls (a policy success) and as the illicit market expands (a policy failure), the revenue base for tobacco control shrinks — potentially at the moment when cessation support for the remaining, typically harder-to-reach smoker population is most needed.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Excise Trap — Can You Tax a Product Out of Existence Without Losing the Tax?

Australia's tobacco excise policy has been theoretically sound: high prices reduce consumption. But at $1.53/stick, the excise rate has crossed the threshold where the price differential with illicit alternatives is so large that organised crime can sustain a nationwide distribution network at scale. The 50% excise increase between 2020 and 2025 produced a 56% *fall* in revenue — the inverse of the intended fiscal relationship. The policy debate is genuinely unresolved: lowering excise risks re-normalising smoking; maintaining it sustains the criminal market. There is no evidence-backed precedent for managing this specific combination of very high legal prices and a mature, entrenched illicit supply network.

### Tension 2: The Prescription-Vaping Paradox — Restricting Access While Recommending the Tool

Australia's framework treats nicotine vaping as a therapeutic good — requiring pharmacist involvement or prescription — on the grounds that this prevents youth recreational use. But the same framework imposes costs and access barriers on adult smokers for whom vaping may be an effective cessation tool. The UK took the opposite approach: NHS England actively promotes vaping as a cessation tool for adult smokers, on the grounds that harm reduction is the priority for existing nicotine dependents. Australia's public health community is divided on this, with tobacco control advocates supporting the restrictive approach and harm reduction advocates arguing it denies help to a vulnerable population of current smokers.

### Tension 3: No PBS Subsidy for Vaping, but No Evidence-Based Pathway to Get One

For a vaping product to receive PBS listing, it would typically require: TGA approval of a specific product (through the full ARTG pathway), PBAC cost-effectiveness assessment, and a ministerial decision. No manufacturer has taken the first step. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: manufacturers won't invest in full TGA submission without certainty of PBS listing; PBAC won't assess cost-effectiveness without a TGA-approved product; the TGA won't receive an application that hasn't been submitted. The result is a legal cessation product with a functioning pharmacy supply chain that receives no healthcare system subsidy.

### Tension 4: Residual Smokers — Who Are They and Can the System Reach Them?

The smokers remaining in the 2025 cohort (approximately 1.6 million daily smokers at 5.6% of the adult population) are systematically different from the smokers of 1991. They are disproportionately: low-income, with high PBS co-payment sensitivity; experiencing mental health conditions (smoking prevalence among people with mental illness is 2–4 times higher than general population); Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (37.4% daily smoking in 2018–19, target of 27% by 2030, almost certainly not on track given structural barriers); in regional and remote areas with limited cessation support access; or in the aged care population (where cessation advice is often not provided to residents). Standard PBS pharmacotherapy and Quitline are not well-calibrated for these sub-populations. The cessation gap is widening between a mainstream population approaching very low smoking prevalence and marginalised populations for whom neither excise price nor regulatory access has moved the needle.

### Tension 5: The Revenue Cliff and What It Means for Tobacco Control Funding

If tobacco excise revenue continues to fall — whether from declining smoking rates (a success) or expanding illicit markets (a failure) — the implicit cross-subsidy relationship between tobacco tax revenue and health system costs will eventually require explicit replacement. Australia has never formally hypothecated tobacco excise to health spending (unlike Victoria's Mental Health Levy, examined in Report 048), but the political economy has always been that tobacco taxes fund a portion of the health costs of tobacco-related illness. As that revenue base erodes, the question of how to sustain cessation programs, campaigns, and pharmacotherapy subsidies without a growing excise base will become fiscally unavoidable.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **First Nations tobacco: the 27% by 2030 target and why it's not on track.** The National Tobacco Strategy sets a specific First Nations smoking target, but Daily smoking prevalence among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians (~37.4% in 2018–19) has not declined as rapidly as the general population. The Tackling Indigenous Smoking program, ACCHO-based cessation supports, and the specific cultural and socioeconomic barriers to cessation in First Nations communities constitute a separate and largely untold payment story.

2. **The illicit tobacco economy in depth: organised crime, crop destruction, and whether enforcement can work.** The ATO's Illicit Tobacco Taskforce operations, the political economy of illicit supply chains from Southeast Asia, and the question of whether excise reduction is an option deserve a dedicated treatment.

3. **UK NHS vaping model vs. Australia's prescription approach: a comparative study.** The UK's approach — NHS-funded "swap to stop" schemes, vape starter kits supplied free to smokers who want to quit, GP prescription of vaping products as cessation tools — represents the opposite regulatory philosophy. A direct comparison would illuminate the cost-effectiveness and equity trade-offs of each model.

4. **Tobacco-related disease and the healthcare cost of smoking in 2026.** What are the current estimates of tobacco-attributable disease burden (cancer, COPD, cardiovascular disease, FASD-adjacent perinatal effects of smoking in pregnancy) and the direct healthcare costs? This would quantify the cost-benefit story for cessation investment.

5. **Primary Health Networks and tobacco cessation commissioning.** PHNs commission GP-based cessation programs and Quitline referral pathways at regional level. A report on how PHNs prioritise tobacco cessation in their commissioning plans, what data they use, and how this interacts with both the PBS pharmacotherapy system and the vaping regulatory framework would fill a gap in the mid-level policy picture.
