# The Genetic Counselling Workforce: Australia's Binding Constraint on Hereditary Disease Programs

**Report 101 — 2026-09-19**

**Summary:** Genetic counsellors are the enabling profession for every hereditary disease program covered in this series — Lynch syndrome, BRCA/HBOC, FH cascade testing, DNA Screen, Mackenzie's Mission, and reproductive carrier screening. Yet Australia has only approximately 346 FTE genetic counsellors in clinical practice across the whole country, genetic counselling services cannot be billed directly to Medicare (there are no dedicated standalone MBS items for genetic counsellor professional services), and wait times of up to nine months for non-urgent cases are routine. The HGSA's December 2024 cost-benefit analysis and 2025-26 Pre-Budget Submission make the case for Medicare listing; the National Health Genomics Policy Framework 2026–2030 acknowledges the workforce as a priority; but the fundamental payment architecture problem — a workforce that is mandated by clinical guidelines but not funded by the national insurance system — remains unresolved.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 100 documented that FH cascade testing items added to the MBS in May 2020 saw a mean of only 12 cascade tests per year in NSW. Report 99 described the National Health Genomics Policy Framework. Reports 94–98 traced genomic testing gaps across cancer genetics. The same workforce constraint — too few genetic counsellors, concentrated in tertiary centres, working in a system that does not pay them directly through Medicare — recurs across every one of these programs.

---

## Background: What Genetic Counsellors Do and Why They Are Non-Substitutable

Genetic counsellors are health professionals trained (at master's level) to:

- Interpret genetic test results and their implications for patients and families
- Explain risk probabilities in hereditary conditions (autosomal dominant, recessive, X-linked, polygenic)
- Facilitate informed consent for genetic testing, particularly for conditions with high psychological burden (cancer predisposition, neurological conditions, reproductive decisions)
- Coordinate cascade testing — identifying and contacting at-risk relatives
- Navigate the psychosocial dimensions of a genetic diagnosis: implications for insurance, employment, family planning, relationships
- Liaise between patients, GPs, specialists, and laboratories to ensure appropriate test ordering and result communication

They occupy a specific niche between clinical geneticists (medical specialists) and general nurses: they bring the communication and psychosocial skills needed for complex genetic conversations without the full medical training (and cost) of a geneticist. Internationally, genetic counsellors are recognised as essential to the safe and equitable delivery of genomic medicine at scale. MSAC item descriptors for numerous genetic tests — FH, BRCA, Lynch, reproductive carrier screening — require or recommend that testing occur alongside genetic counselling. In practice, however, there is no Medicare funding mechanism to pay for that counselling.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Size of the Workforce: Smaller Than Almost Any Other Clinical Profession

The most recent comprehensive workforce data comes from the **2022-23 HGSA and Australian Genomics Australasian Professional Genetic Workforce Census**, which surveyed 252 genetic counsellors across Australia and New Zealand.

Key figures:

- **Estimated 346 FTE genetic counsellors** in clinical practice across Australasia in 2022 — up from approximately 220 FTE in 2017, an increase of 57% in five years. This growth reflects expanded genomic programs but remains far below demand.
- **Approximately 630 trained genetic counsellors** exist across Australasia, but only around 400 work in clinical practice. The remainder work in research, industry (pharmaceutical/diagnostic companies), or have left the clinical workforce.
- **The Australasian Society of Genetic Counsellors (ASGC) has over 500 members** — covering both Australia and New Zealand, giving a rough sense of the total certified workforce. Australia's share (excluding New Zealand) would be approximately 350–400 certified counsellors.
- **HGSA service level calculations for 2023 estimate a demand of approximately 418 FTE genetic counsellors** — already exceeding estimated supply.

For comparison: Australia has approximately **37,000 GPs**, **13,000 psychiatrists and psychologists** registered under AHPRA, and more than **90,000 registered nurses**. The genetic counselling workforce is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than almost any other health profession with dedicated clinical responsibilities.

### 2. Training Throughput: Growing But Insufficient

Australia has two accredited Master of Genetic Counselling programs:

- **University of Melbourne** — the longer-standing program; received full accreditation in 2024; has doubled student intake following Melbourne Genomics Health Alliance investment.
- **University of Technology Sydney (UTS)** — full accreditation granted March 2021.

Combined, these programs graduate approximately **30–45 students per year** across Australia and New Zealand. However:

- The pipeline cannot close the demand gap at this rate. If demand is for 418+ FTE and current workforce is approximately 346 FTE, closing the gap requires several years' output of graduates retained in clinical practice.
- **Employment positions are the real bottleneck**, not training places. HGSA data indicates that up to 45 new graduates per year are trained, but not 30–40 new clinical positions exist to absorb them. This creates a mismatch: graduates who cannot find clinical roles leave for industry or research, reducing the effective clinical workforce despite growing training capacity.
- Training is concentrated in Victoria and New South Wales. Genetic counsellors trained in major cities overwhelmingly seek employment there, exacerbating the rural and regional access gap.

### 3. The Medicare Gap: A Profession Mandated but Not Funded

This is the structural core of the problem.

**Current MBS position:** There are **no standalone MBS items** for genetic counselling services delivered by professional genetic counsellors. Genetic counsellors cannot directly bill Medicare for their consultations. This is in contrast with allied health professions such as psychologists (via Better Access MBS items), dietitians (via Chronic Disease Management items), and physiotherapists — all of whom have clearly defined Medicare billing mechanisms.

**What the MBS does fund:** The MBS funds specific genetic tests (ordered by medical specialists or GPs under specific item descriptors — such as FH cascade testing items 73294, BRCA testing items 73295–73297, Lynch syndrome somatic testing items). Many of these item descriptors state that genetic counselling "should be provided" before testing — but there is no attached item to fund the counselling itself.

**The consequence:** Genetic counselling currently operates almost entirely within the **public hospital system**, where it is funded through state and territory health budgets. Patients who need genetic counselling and are not in the public system — or who are on a public waitlist — must either:
- Pay out-of-pocket for private genetic counselling (typically $200–$400 per session, with no Medicare rebate)
- Wait months for a public clinic appointment
- Forgo counselling entirely, which carries clinical and ethical risk

The public funding model concentrates the workforce in major hospitals in capital cities and creates a structural barrier to primary care delivery of hereditary disease programs. GPs who are supposed to initiate FH cascade testing, Lynch surveillance, or BRCA referrals cannot access a genetic counsellor to assist their patients without the patient entering the public hospital referral pathway.

### 4. The MRAC Review (2022): Recommended MBS Listing, Not Yet Implemented

The **MBS Review Advisory Committee (MRAC)** Genetic Counselling Working Group conducted a formal review in 2022, releasing its final report in November 2022. The review found:

- **Evidence clearly demonstrates the need for increased access** to genetic counselling services
- **Long wait times** in the public sector: approximately 1 week for pregnant women requiring urgent testing, 4 weeks for urgent matters, and **up to 9 months for non-urgent matters** — with some regional and rural areas reporting waits exceeding 2 years
- The MRAC recommended the creation of MBS items for professional genetic counsellors

However, as of mid-2026, **dedicated MBS items for genetic counsellor professional services have not been implemented**. The recommendation sits unfunded in the policy pipeline.

### 5. The HGSA Pre-Budget Submission 2025-26 and Econisis Cost-Benefit Analysis (December 2024)

Frustrated with the slow pace of implementation, HGSA commissioned an independent economic analysis (Econisis, December 2024) and submitted a detailed Pre-Budget Submission for 2025-26 seeking MBS items for genetic counsellors.

Key economic findings from the Econisis cost-benefit analysis:

- Genetic counsellors conduct an estimated **140,192 client sessions annually** across Australia
- Currently only approximately **8.5% of genetic tests** involve a genetic counsellor — a proportion far below what clinical guidelines recommend
- **Proposed MBS fee: $276.70 per session** (based on CPI adjustment from the original MRAC-proposed rate of ~$240 in 2021 dollars)
- **Projected MBS cost: $49.02 million in 2024-25**, rising to $59.27 million annually by 2035 and $71.67 million by 2045 as demand for genomic services grows
- The economic case for MBS listing rests on: (a) preventing costly acute cardiovascular, cancer, and obstetric events through earlier detection; (b) avoiding duplicative specialist consultations that currently substitute for genetic counselling; and (c) enabling scale delivery of genomic medicine programs whose cost-effectiveness has been demonstrated (FH cascade testing at AU$4,155/QALY; BRCA testing with follow-through prevention saving lives and health system costs)

### 6. The Reproductive Carrier Screening Demand Surge

The Medicare listing of reproductive genetic carrier screening for three conditions (cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, fragile X syndrome) from **1 November 2023** created a new demand spike for genetic counselling. Approximately **300,000 couples per year** in Australia are in the reproductive planning age group. Even with the three-gene panel, a proportion of couples will receive positive carrier results requiring interpretation and counselling.

The critical problem highlighted by Mackenzie's Mission data: **80% of carrier couples identified in the national study were carriers for conditions outside the three-gene screen**. As pressure mounts to expand the screened gene panel toward the 750+ conditions tested in the Mackenzie's Mission research program, the genetic counselling demand will expand dramatically.

A June 2025 Australian Genomics report on expanding reproductive carrier screening toward a national program — covering up to 750 genes — models the genetic counselling workforce requirements as one of the primary implementation constraints. The report concludes that the current workforce cannot safely absorb a universal expanded screening program without dedicated funding infrastructure for genetic counselling.

### 7. Geographic Maldistribution and Rural Access

The genetic counselling workforce is concentrated in major metropolitan teaching hospitals:

- Victoria and New South Wales account for the majority of clinical positions
- Queensland, South Australia, and Western Australia have limited services, concentrated in their capital cities
- **Rural and regional Australia** is the most acute access gap. A submission to the NSW Parliament from Murrumbidgee Local Health District described having **1.0 FTE genetic counsellor** covering all genetics referrals across the entire region
- Many families in regional areas wait **over 2 years** for a clinical genetics appointment
- Telehealth has partially addressed geographic barriers post-COVID, but rural genetic counselling capacity remains inadequate

### 8. Professional Regulation: Self-Regulation, Not AHPRA

Genetic counsellors are not registered under the **Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA)** framework, which governs 16 health professions including medicine, nursing, psychology, and physiotherapy. Instead, they are self-regulated through the HGSA Board of Censors for Genetic Counselling. Full membership of the **National Alliance of Self-Regulating Health Professions (NASRHP)** was granted in March 2020.

The regulatory gap matters for two reasons:
1. **MBS billing** — AHPRA registration is often a prerequisite for MBS billing rights. Without AHPRA regulation, establishing a Medicare billing mechanism for genetic counsellors is structurally more complex
2. **Title protection** — without mandatory AHPRA registration, anyone can theoretically call themselves a "genetic counsellor," creating patient safety risks as genomic services expand into commercial and direct-to-consumer settings

HGSA has not pursued AHPRA registration, instead strengthening the NASRHP pathway. Whether this regulatory framework is adequate to support the profession's scale-up under national genomic programs is an open debate.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Will MBS items for genetic counsellors be funded in Budget 2026-27?** The HGSA submission is on the table. The MRAC recommended listing in November 2022. The cost-benefit analysis is complete. But the 3.5-year gap between recommendation and implementation reflects the compressed federal health budget environment — $49m/year is not trivial — and the absence of a politically compelling moment (like Mackenzie's Mission provided for reproductive carrier screening) to drive action.

**2. Is the employment bottleneck, rather than training, the real constraint?** Australia may be producing enough graduates but not enough clinical positions. Without Medicare funding to support private-practice genetic counsellors, all clinical employment depends on state hospital budgets — which are constrained and do not uniformly prioritise genomics. MBS listing would allow genetic counsellors to establish private practices in primary care settings, potentially unlocking distributed delivery models that the hospital-only model cannot provide.

**3. Does every genomic program need dedicated genetic counsellors, or can new roles partially substitute?** Melbourne Genomics' "associate genetic counsellor" model — training nurses and other health professionals in foundational genetic counselling skills — is one innovation. This allows routine pre-test counselling for lower-complexity programs (reproductive carrier screening, FH, Lynch) to be delivered by a larger workforce, reserving qualified genetic counsellors for complex cases. But the scope, accreditation, and funding model for these roles remain undeveloped at a national level.

**4. Should genetic counsellors be registered under AHPRA?** The HGSA/NASRHP pathway is workable but creates complexity. If expanded genomic programs generate a large commercial market (direct-to-consumer testing, private clinics), title protection and mandatory competency standards may require stronger statutory backing. Several professional societies have called for AHPRA consideration; HGSA has resisted it for reasons of professional autonomy.

**5. The equity dimension: who waits longest?** Rural and regional patients, First Nations patients (for whom genetic testing access is already documented as inequitable across Lynch, BRCA, and newborn screening programs — Reports 87, 97, 98), and patients using the public system face the most severe waits. A Medicare-funded genetic counselling model would not by itself solve geographic maldistribution — it would need to be combined with rural incentive structures analogous to the Modified Monash Model for GPs (Report 12).

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Mackenzie's Mission and the case for universal reproductive carrier screening at scale** — The June 2025 Australian Genomics report recommends a national program covering 750+ conditions. What would it cost, who would deliver it, and what Budget decision is required?

2. **Cardiovascular genomics beyond FH: the polygenic risk score debate** — MSAC is beginning to consider PRS testing submissions for coronary artery disease risk. This represents a qualitatively different genomic medicine model — population-scale risk stratification rather than single-gene cascade testing.

3. **The AHPRA question: should genomic medicine professions be nationally regulated?** — Genetic counsellors, genetic pathologists, and variant scientists all operate in rapidly expanding clinical roles under self-regulatory or discipline-specific frameworks that were designed for much smaller workforces.

4. **National Heart Foundation guidelines and cardiovascular prevention MBS architecture** — Australia's 2023 absolute cardiovascular risk guidelines specify LDL-C targets and treatment algorithms. What MBS items fund the risk assessment and follow-up, and are they used?

5. **The state-federal split in genetics funding** — Genetic services are primarily funded by states through hospital budgets, but genomic tests are funded by the Commonwealth through the MBS. This split creates systematic gaps. What does a genuinely integrated funding model look like?
