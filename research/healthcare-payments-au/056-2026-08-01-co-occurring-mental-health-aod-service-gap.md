# Co-Occurring Mental Health and Alcohol and Other Drug Conditions: Australia's Invisible Comorbidity Crisis and the Funding Architecture That Keeps Two Systems Apart

**Report 056 — 2026-08-01**

**Summary:** At least 55% of Australians with an alcohol or other drug (AOD) use disorder have a co-occurring mental health condition, and roughly 60% of people with a mental health disorder are also experiencing AOD dependence — yet Australia's healthcare payment architecture treats these as entirely separate systems, with separate funding streams, separate workforces, separate service models, and no dedicated Medicare Benefits Schedule items for AOD treatment. A 2025 UNSW study estimated that treatment resources need to be doubled to meet the unmet demand for AOD services alone. The National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 expires at the end of this year, the National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement expires in June 2027, and a parliamentary inquiry into AOD's health impacts completed its public hearings in May 2026 — creating a rare simultaneous policy window that governments could use to redesign how these two systems meet. History suggests they will not.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 055 examined the Productivity Commission's verdict that the National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement is "not fit for purpose" and detailed the renegotiation process now underway. A central PC recommendation was that the successor Agreement should include a dedicated schedule for people experiencing co-occurring mental ill health and problematic AOD use — a population the Commission found "particularly poorly served" by both systems.

## Background

### Two Systems, One Population

Australia's mental health and AOD systems share a very large population. The clinical literature has characterised co-occurring mental health and substance use disorders as "the expectation, not the exception" in both treatment settings — a framing first used in a landmark Australian Senate inquiry into mental health (2006) that is still being cited two decades later, because the underlying service configuration has changed so little.

The reasons for co-occurrence are multiple and bidirectional. People experiencing mental ill health may use alcohol or other drugs to relieve symptoms (self-medication), while substance use can trigger or exacerbate mental health conditions including psychosis, anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation. Shared social determinants — trauma, poverty, housing instability, childhood adversity — independently increase risk for both. The relationship is not simply causal in one direction: it is a dynamic interaction that treatment systems designed around single diagnoses are poorly equipped to address.

Australia's systems are structured around exactly this diagnostic separation:

- **Mental health** is primarily funded through the Medicare Benefits Schedule (GPs, psychiatrists, psychologists via Better Access), state and territory community mental health services (block-funded), private health insurance, and the NDIS.
- **AOD treatment** is primarily funded through the Commonwealth's Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP), delivered via Primary Health Networks (PHNs), plus state/territory hospital-based and community AOD services funded through public hospital and community health block grants.

There is no Medicare item number for an AOD treatment consultation. A GP conducting a mental health assessment including drug and alcohol screening can claim a mental health care plan (MBS Item 2700/2701), but there is no equivalent pathway for a focused AOD treatment plan, no AOD-specific better access equivalent, and no Medicare rebate for AOD counselling as a standalone service. A patient with co-occurring depression and alcohol use disorder will find that their mental health is MBS-eligible but their alcohol dependence is treated as a social/welfare matter — unless they live in an area served by a PHN-commissioned service, they are on medication-assisted therapy (methadone or buprenorphine via the PBS and state opioid treatment programs), or they access a hospital-based service.

### The Scale of the Population

The 2020–2022 Australian National Study of Mental Health and Wellbeing (N=15,893, ABS) found that approximately **46% of Australians with any mental or substance use disorder in the past 12 months experienced two or more diagnosable conditions**. Critically, young people aged 16–24 were significantly more likely to experience co-occurrence in 2020–22 compared with the same age group in 2007 — the comorbidity picture is worsening among the generation now entering the treatment-seeking years.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (Marel et al., *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry*) found that among clients of mental health treatment settings in Australia, the pooled prevalence of past-year AOD use ranged from 5% to 58% depending on the substance — with tobacco use particularly prevalent (58% past-year use; 53% problematic use), cannabis at 38% any use and 37% problematic, and alcohol at substantial rates across studies. The authors' central finding: **"Co-occurring and problematic AOD use should be expected among a considerable proportion of clients of mental health treatment settings"** — yet standard mental health treatment protocols do not require AOD assessment or integrated AOD treatment capacity.

The converse is equally true: AOD treatment services report that the majority of their clients have co-occurring mental health conditions. The Alcohol and Drug Foundation's synthesis of available evidence suggests at least 55% of people with an AOD use disorder have a co-occurring mental health condition.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Treatment Gap: AOD Services Are Chronically Under-Resourced

A 2025 study published in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Ritter et al., UNSW) — using the Drug and Alcohol Services Planning Model — produced the most current estimate of Australia's AOD treatment gap:

- **752,812 to 1,291,119** Australians met the criteria for a substance use disorder in 2023
- The estimated potential treatment population (those who would seek and benefit from treatment) was **406,697 to 668,497 people**
- Actual treatment received (all sources, not just publicly funded): **198,731 people**
- Treatment gap: **207,966 to 469,767 people**
- Only **30–48%** of the population who would benefit from AOD treatment received any

The study's headline finding was stark: **treatment resources would need to be doubled to close this gap**.

This is the AOD system on its own, before incorporating the additional demand from people with co-occurring mental health conditions — who are systematically harder to treat, more likely to disengage from services, and more likely to experience treatment that doesn't address both conditions simultaneously.

The publicly funded AOD treatment system treated **131,900 clients** in 2023–24 (AIHW), with nearly 241,000 treatment episodes from 1,304 agencies. The largest drugs of concern were alcohol (42% of episodes), amphetamines (26%), cannabis (16%), and heroin/opioids (4.3%). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people represented approximately 18% of clients — roughly four times their share of the general population, reflecting the disproportionate burden of AOD-related harm in First Nations communities.

### 2. Where Australia's Drug Budget Actually Goes

The Drug Policy Modelling Program at UNSW Sydney estimated that Australian governments spent approximately **$5.45 billion on illicit drug issues** in 2021–22. The allocation breakdown reveals a system oriented towards law enforcement, not health:

- **Law enforcement: 64%** ($3.5 billion) — policing, prosecution, courts, corrections related to drug offences
- **Treatment: 27%** (~$1.5 billion) — publicly funded AOD treatment services
- **Prevention: 7%** ($363 million) — education and community prevention programs
- **Harm reduction: 1.6%** ($89 million) — needle and syringe programs, naloxone, drug checking services

This allocation is broadly out of step with the evidence base, which consistently finds that treatment and harm reduction produce substantially better health outcomes per dollar than law enforcement. The 64:27:7:1.6 split has been largely stable for years. A 2024 UNSW analysis of Australia's "drug budget" asked where the money is going and found the composition has not materially shifted despite two decades of advocacy from health and peak bodies.

By contrast, Australia's total mental health expenditure in 2023–24 reached **$13.8 billion** (AIHW) — roughly 9 times the AOD treatment component of the drug budget. The payment architecture reflects this asymmetry: mental health has the MBS (open-ended patient entitlement), the PBS (medicines), the NDIS (for psychosocial disability), and a network of Commonwealth-funded primary care services. AOD treatment has PHN-commissioned community services, state block-funded hospital programs, and medication-assisted treatment via the PBS and state opioid programs — but no equivalent of the MBS's universal subsidy for individual treatment consultations.

### 3. The PHN-DAP Funding Model and Its Limits

The Commonwealth's primary vehicle for funding AOD treatment services is the **Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP)**, administered through PHNs. PHNs commission locally appropriate AOD treatment services based on population need — in principle allowing flexible, place-based service design. The DAP allocated approximately **$412.1 million** through PHNs in 2023–24 (AIHW).

Through the 2025–26 Mid-Year Economic and Fiscal Outlook (MYEFO), the government consolidated the DAP's funding into a single ongoing program — ending the uncertainty of year-to-year terminating funding that had afflicted the sector. This was widely welcomed by peak bodies.

But the model has structural limitations:
- PHN-commissioned services vary enormously by geography — there is no national entitlement and no consistent service standard
- Services funded under the DAP cannot be claimed through Medicare, meaning they do not count toward Medicare Safety Nets and are invisible to the MBS data infrastructure
- PHN contracting cycles (typically 3–5 years) create workforce instability, particularly for smaller NGO providers
- The model does not create any MBS billing pathway for individual practitioners providing AOD treatment — a psychologist treating alcohol use disorder cannot claim a Medicare rebate for that treatment unless they attach it to a mental health diagnosis

### 4. The Opioid Treatment System: A Different Funding Layer

One corner of AOD treatment does intersect with Commonwealth payment mechanisms: **opioid pharmacotherapy**. Methadone, buprenorphine (sublingual), and long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB) are listed on the PBS, providing a subsidised medication pathway for opioid dependence:

- **PBS co-payment for ODT medicines:** $7.30 per 28 days (concession card holders); $30 per 28 days (general patients)
- **Community Pharmacy Program:** pharmacists can claim fees for dispensing ODT medicines and administering LAIBs — one of the few AOD service delivery points with a Commonwealth payment mechanism at the individual transaction level
- **State opioid treatment programs:** state/territory governments fund program oversight, prescriber authorisation, and sometimes absorb co-payments for disadvantaged clients

But the opioid pharmacotherapy system reaches only a subset of the AOD-affected population and does not extend to a broader Medicare billing model for AOD treatment. NHMRC-funded research at Monash University (2025) is underway to "transform opioid dependence treatment" — suggesting the field is still searching for its payment model rather than operating a settled one.

National opioid pharmacotherapy statistics (AIHW) show the system treating approximately 50,000 people at any given time — a figure that has grown but still represents a minority of the estimated opioid-dependent population, with prescriber shortages and geographic access barriers significant constraints particularly in rural and regional Australia.

### 5. The Funding Silo Effect on Co-Occurring Conditions

For people with co-occurring mental health and AOD conditions, the payment architecture produces a predictable outcome: **neither system fully claims responsibility**.

The pattern described in the literature (and in submissions to the parliamentary inquiry) is one of "diagnostic triage" — where the presenting problem determines which system the person enters, and that system then treats the condition within its scope while either ignoring the co-occurring condition or referring onward. Referral pathways between mental health and AOD services are frequently absent, poorly designed, or not followed through. The population that falls through the gap is by definition the most complex: people with both conditions severe enough to need treatment for each.

A 2022 PMC-published study on the experience of Australian AOD nurses found that when service integration was attempted, it was often experienced by AOD workers as a "hostile takeover" — with the AOD model of care subordinated to a mental health model, and AOD-specific funding streams reduced. This reflects a power asymmetry: mental health services, with their MBS and hospital funding, typically have larger resource bases than AOD services, and "integration" in practice has often meant AOD services being absorbed into mental health structures without genuine co-design.

Victoria has gone furthest in addressing this. The Victorian Royal Commission on Mental Health and Wellbeing (final report February 2023) included **Recommendation 35**: that all Mental Health and Wellbeing Services provide integrated treatment, care and support to people living with mental illness and substance use or addiction. Victoria's Department of Health published a 2022 guidance document with 11 expectations for service providers covering integrated assessment, care planning, and treatment. The two systems are to "remain distinct but work more closely together." Twenty postgraduate scholarships (worth up to $13,000 each) are being offered to AOD practitioners in 2025–26 to support their capacity to provide integrated care. This is promising, but it is a state-level, workforce-focused response to what is fundamentally a national payment architecture problem.

### 6. The Parliamentary Inquiry: A Critical Policy Window

The House of Representatives Standing Committee on Health, Aged Care and Disability completed its final public hearings into the health impacts of alcohol and other drugs on 28–29 May 2026. The inquiry — originally established in 2024, lapsed at the dissolution of Parliament before the May 2025 federal election, and re-referred in August 2025 — represents a significant opportunity for parliamentary pressure on the AOD funding model.

A final report is expected in the current Parliament. Key themes throughout the submissions (based on coverage of public hearings) include:
- The need to rebalance the drug budget from law enforcement towards health
- The absence of any MBS pathway for AOD treatment
- The workforce shortages in AOD services, exacerbated by funding uncertainty
- The particular failure of the system for people with co-occurring conditions
- Stigma as a barrier to care-seeking and funding advocacy

### 7. The 2026 Policy Convergence

Three major policy frameworks that shape AOD and mental health service delivery are simultaneously expiring or being renegotiated:

1. **National Drug Strategy 2017–2026** — expires at end of 2026; final COAG report due 2027; successor framework consultation not yet announced
2. **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement** — expires 30 June 2027; renegotiation underway with proposed co-occurring conditions schedule
3. **Community Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP)** — expires 30 June 2027; provides non-clinical psychosocial support to 25,000 adults with severe mental illness not eligible for NDIS

This creates an unusual simultaneous window to redesign the interface between the two systems — if governments choose to use it. The Productivity Commission recommendation for a dedicated co-occurring conditions schedule in the new Mental Health Agreement is one vehicle. A successor National Drug Strategy that explicitly addresses funding parity between law enforcement and health, and proposes an MBS pathway for AOD treatment, would be another.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Can AOD treatment be brought into the MBS?** The MBS is an entitlement-based system — it cannot ration AOD services in the way that PHN block grants can. Unlimited Medicare billing for AOD treatment would cost significantly more than the current PHN model. But the absence of any MBS pathway creates a two-tiered system where AOD treatment is less legitimate, less remunerative for practitioners, and less visible to health data infrastructure than mental health treatment. The Better Access model, for all its flaws, created a viable billing pathway for allied mental health professionals. A parallel AOD treatment plan mechanism — or an extension of Better Access to explicitly include AOD treatment — is technically achievable. Whether it is politically viable is a different question.

**2. Who funds the co-occurring population?** If a person has depression and alcohol use disorder, the mental health component is fundable through Medicare and better access, but the AOD component is not. Which level of government bears responsibility for treating the whole person? States argue this is a Commonwealth-funded primary care problem; the Commonwealth argues that complex, co-occurring presentations belong in state-funded community health and hospital services. The person in the middle receives neither.

**3. Is the "integration" framing itself the problem?** There is a substantial literature — and strong advocacy from AOD peak bodies — arguing that "integration" has historically meant the absorption of AOD services by mental health, with the loss of AOD-specific expertise, harm reduction culture, and person-centred recovery models. Some advocate instead for "parallel service delivery" — robust, well-funded AOD services working alongside but not merged with mental health services, with strong cross-referral protocols and care coordination. The payment architecture is probably more important than the service model: if AOD treatment had its own viable Medicare pathway, it would attract and retain a larger workforce and a more stable service network.

**4. The law enforcement budget: political economy of the drug budget.** The $3.5 billion law enforcement component of Australia's drug budget is spent by government departments (police, corrections, courts) with their own institutional interests. No government has ever seriously proposed shifting a significant portion of this allocation towards health — the political economy pushes strongly in the direction of maintaining enforcement funding, and any proposal to redirect it is vulnerable to "soft on drugs" attack. Yet the health case for rebalancing is overwhelming.

**5. AOD workforce stigma and attrition.** AOD workers are among the lowest-paid health professionals in Australia, working largely in NGO-funded services on short-term contracts without the career pathways available in mental health or nursing. The workforce is characterised by high burnout and turnover. This is not separable from the funding model: when AOD services are funded through block grants rather than fee-for-service, there is less revenue for competitive salaries, and training pathways are less developed. Addressing co-occurring conditions requires a workforce that is competent in both — but the pay differential between mental health (MBS-billable) and AOD (grant-funded) practitioners creates a recruitment disadvantage that training scholarships alone cannot solve.
