# PHI, Colonoscopy Out-of-Pocket Costs, and NBCSP Follow-Up: The Cost Architecture That Leaves 14% Behind

**Summary:** Australia's colonoscopy funding model creates a three-tier system — free-but-slow in the public system, subsidised-but-complex with private health insurance, and prohibitively expensive without it. This architecture helps explain why 14% of Australians who return a positive bowel cancer screen never complete their follow-up colonoscopy, with the gap widening significantly in remote and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 088 detailed the Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025 and its geographic access atlas data showing 18-fold variation in colonoscopy rates across Australia. A significant driver of that variation is the payment system itself — specifically, how private health insurance (PHI) coverage works (and doesn't) for colonoscopy, what patients actually pay out-of-pocket, and how cost interacts with the 14% non-completion rate for NBCSP positive screens.

---

## Background

In Australia, colonoscopy is the definitive diagnostic test following a positive iFOBT from the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP). Access is theoretically universal — no person with a positive NBCSP result should face financial barriers to follow-up. In practice, however, the payment system fragments into three distinct pathways, each with radically different cost and access profiles:

1. **Public hospital as a public patient**: No out-of-pocket cost, but waiting times of 132–202 days (2024 AIHW data), with only 14.6% of participants receiving a colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day benchmark.
2. **Private hospital with PHI**: Faster access (typically 2–3 weeks), but a complex cost architecture involving specialist gap fees, hospital excess payments, anaesthetist fees, and PHI waiting periods.
3. **Private hospital without PHI**: Total costs of $2,000–$5,000, which is effectively prohibitive for most Australians.

This structure has major implications for NBCSP program integrity. A program whose value depends on completing the diagnostic pathway after a positive screen is being undermined by a follow-up access system that is neither fast nor free for a significant share of the population.

## Key Findings

### 1. The MBS Schedule Fee and the Gap Architecture

Colonoscopy is billed under MBS items 32222–32228 (introduced 2019, replacing the previous 32090/32093 structure). The most relevant item for NBCSP positive-screen patients is:

- **Item 32222**: Colonoscopy for asymptomatic patients following a positive iFOBT/FOBT — MBS schedule fee **$721.70** (2026)
- **Item 32223**: Moderate/high-risk family history — $721.70
- **Item 32228**: Once-per-lifetime item where patient history is unavailable — $721.70

For a private patient in a private hospital:
- **Medicare pays 75%** of the MBS schedule fee = approximately $541.28
- **The PHI fund must pay at least 25%** of the schedule fee = approximately $180.43 (by law)
- **Gap fee** = whatever the gastroenterologist charges above $721.70

According to Medical Costs Finder data (2023–24), **76% of privately insured colonoscopy patients had no out-of-pocket specialist cost**. For the remaining **24%, the average gap was $200**. However, this figure covers only the specialist (gastroenterologist) component. Total out-of-pocket costs also include:

- **Hospital excess**: The fixed amount the patient pays toward hospital admission — typically $250–$750 per admission under most PHI policies. This is payable even in a no-gap procedure because it is a policy cost-sharing mechanism, not a specialist fee.
- **Anaesthetist fees**: Separate from the gastroenterologist and billed independently. Under no-gap schemes, anaesthetists accept Medicare + fund rebate as full payment. Under known-gap schemes, the patient's out-of-pocket is capped at **$500 per anaesthetist per procedure** — but only if the anaesthetist participates in the fund's gap cover scheme. Non-participating anaesthetists have no cap.
- **Pathology fees**: Polyp specimens sent to pathology generate additional MBS-billed services that may attract gap fees.

In aggregate, a privately insured patient with a no-gap specialist but a hospital excess and a known-gap anaesthetist might pay **$0–$700 out-of-pocket** for a colonoscopy — a range wide enough to be a meaningful barrier for cost-sensitive households.

### 2. PHI Coverage: Category Requirements and Waiting Period Barriers

Colonoscopy falls under the **gastrointestinal endoscopy** treatment category in PHI. Under the tiered hospital product framework (Basic, Bronze, Silver, Gold):

- **Basic and Bronze products** typically **do not cover** gastrointestinal endoscopy, which means a positive NBCSP result will not trigger PHI benefits for the follow-up colonoscopy on these products.
- **Silver and Gold products** must include gastrointestinal endoscopy coverage by regulatory requirement.

This creates a structural problem: approximately **3.9 million Australians** hold Basic or Bronze hospital cover (as of 2024 APRA data), primarily younger and lower-income subscribers attracted by lower premiums. A positive NBCSP result in this population produces a recommendation for colonoscopy that their PHI will not fund.

The second structural barrier is the **waiting period**. Most PHI policies impose:
- A **2-month waiting period** for new gastrointestinal endoscopy coverage (minimum new entrant waiting)
- A **12-month waiting period** for pre-existing conditions

The 12-month pre-existing condition rule is particularly consequential for NBCSP. If a person who has never held relevant PHI cover receives a positive NBCSP result — effectively a new colorectal cancer finding — the insurer may classify the underlying condition as pre-existing, requiring 12 months of premiums before any hospital benefit is payable. (The pre-existing condition definition is regulated but contested, and patient advocates regularly cite this as a source of confusion and financial hardship.)

### 3. The Public System Default: Theoretically Free, Practically Constrained

For Australians without PHI, or with Basic/Bronze cover, the default is the public system as a public patient — **no out-of-pocket cost, but long waits**.

The 2024 AIHW NBCSP Monitoring Report found:
- **Median time from positive screen to diagnostic assessment: 62 days** nationally
- **Only 14.6%** received colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day benchmark
- Wait times varied from **132 to 202 days** depending on location
- **Very remote areas: 81.6%** follow-up rate (vs 86% national average)
- **Remote areas: 84.9%** follow-up rate
- **Low socioeconomic areas**: consistently lower follow-up rates than high-SEA areas

This means the public pathway — nominally free and universally available — fails the target follow-up window so comprehensively that the "free" access is largely illusory in terms of the clinical benefit that the 30-day standard was designed to protect.

### 4. Geographic and Socioeconomic Inequity in Practice

The ACSQHC Atlas Focus Report (released with the Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025, September 2025) documented that colonoscopy rates in the most affluent areas were **nearly double** those in the most socioeconomically disadvantaged areas: **3,067 versus 1,675 per 100,000 people**. Geographic variation was equally stark: an **18-fold difference** in early repeat colonoscopy rates across Statistical Area Level 3 regions.

This inequity operates through at least three mechanisms related to the payment system:

1. **PHI coverage distribution**: PHI hospital cover participation rates vary substantially by income and location. Higher-income, urban populations are far more likely to hold Gold or Silver cover. Low-income, remote, and Indigenous populations are more likely to be uninsured or hold minimal-category products.

2. **Direct cost sensitivity**: Even when PHI covers the specialist gap, the hospital excess (typically $250–$750) and potentially a non-participating anaesthetist's gap may combine to produce a meaningful out-of-pocket cost for households operating close to the margin. Research consistently shows that even modest financial barriers create significant deterrents to healthcare utilisation among lower-income groups.

3. **Foregone private speed premium**: In rural and remote areas, the choice between public hospital (long wait, free) and private (short wait, costly) is often not available at all — there may be no private endoscopy facility within accessible distance. The public system is the only option, and its capacity constraints mean long waits regardless of the patient's ability to pay.

### 5. No-Gap Programs: Genuine Solutions with Narrow Reach

Several mechanisms exist to eliminate or substantially reduce out-of-pocket colonoscopy costs:

- **NSW Direct Access Colonoscopy (DAC)**: NBCSP positive-screen participants can be referred directly for a public-hospital colonoscopy with **no out-of-pocket cost** for the pre-procedure consultation or the procedure itself. This model bypasses the GP-to-specialist-to-colonoscopy referral chain and is theoretically the closest thing Australia has to a guaranteed-free follow-up pathway. The limitation is that DAC capacity does not resolve the wait time problem.

- **Medibank No Gap Endoscopy Program**: Medibank members can access no-out-of-pocket endoscopy (including colonoscopy) through participating surgeons at participating hospitals. This is a fund-specific contracted arrangement and is not available to members of other funds or outside participating provider networks.

- **PHI Fund No-Gap and Known-Gap Schemes**: All major funds (Bupa, HCF, Medibank, nib, HBF) operate gap cover schemes for participating specialists. The key limitation is participation: specialists who choose not to join a fund's gap scheme may charge unlimited gaps, and not all funds' gap data is publicly visible (the Medical Costs Finder provides averages but not provider-level guarantees).

The combination of state-specific DAC programs, fund-specific no-gap schemes, and variable specialist participation means that "free colonoscopy" exists in theory but requires navigating a system of conditional access that many patients — particularly those with lower health literacy, lower geographic density of options, and fewer resources — are poorly positioned to navigate.

### 6. The PHI Waiting Period vs. NBCSP Timeline Problem

The NBCSP detection-to-diagnosis timeline is critical because cancer stage at diagnosis is the primary determinant of survival. The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025 target is colonoscopy within 30 days of a positive FOBT result. Cancer Council Australia's guideline permits up to 120 days before stage progression risk becomes significant.

Against these benchmarks, the PHI 12-month pre-existing condition waiting period is indefensible as a policy design choice for positive-NBCSP patients. A person who receives their first positive NBCSP result and wants to use newly acquired PHI to access a colonoscopy faster is structurally barred from doing so for up to a year — by which point:
- Any cancer present has had 12 months to progress
- The NBCSP follow-up window has long passed
- Stage migration from operable to inoperable can occur in 6–12 months for moderately aggressive colorectal tumours

No policy exemption exists for NBCSP-triggered colonoscopies within the standard PHI waiting period framework. SaferCare Victoria's March 2025 report on best-practice colonoscopy explicitly identified "inequity of access" as a key concern requiring action, but waiting period reform was not among its recommendations, which focused on clinical practice rather than insurance architecture.

### 7. Cost of Non-Completion

The NBCSP delivers its population health benefit only when the chain of screen → positive result → colonoscopy → treatment is completed. The 14% non-follow-up rate represents approximately **12,000 Australians per year** (based on ~86,000 positive screens in 2023) who do not proceed to diagnostic assessment. Even if only a fraction of non-completers have cost as a primary barrier, the downstream cost of delayed cancer diagnosis at later stages dwarfs any savings from avoided colonoscopies.

Research modelling published in *The Lancet* (cited in Report 079) projected that optimal NBCSP follow-up rates would generate AU$2.1 billion in savings over 10 years from earlier-stage diagnosis and treatment. The payment system's contribution to the 14% non-completion rate is an uncosted drag on that projected benefit.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **PHI waiting period exemptions**: Should positive-NBCSP results trigger an exemption from the 12-month pre-existing condition waiting period, analogous to emergency admissions? No government proposal currently addresses this.

2. **Excess waiver for screening-indicated procedures**: Some advocates have called for PHI excess waivers for screen-positive diagnostic colonoscopies — treating them like preventive care rather than elective admissions. This is not current policy in any fund.

3. **Gap fee transparency post-Medical Costs Finder**: The Medical Costs Finder Bill 2026 (covered in Report 016) mandated specialist fee disclosure, but real-time, patient-specific fee guarantees remain unavailable before a booking is confirmed. Patients face a "quote after booking" problem that is particularly acute for colonoscopy, where the gastroenterologist, anaesthetist, and hospital may have separate billing arrangements.

4. **DAC expansion vs. PHI reform**: There is a policy fork between expanding DAC (a public-system, state-funded solution requiring no PHI reform) and reforming PHI waiting periods and excess rules (a Commonwealth-level change to insurance regulation). These are complementary, not mutually exclusive, but they have different political constituencies and funding sources.

5. **Bowel prep costs**: Uninsured and independent of PHI, the bowel preparation medications required before colonoscopy are not on the PBS. Patients bear 100% of this cost, which typically ranges from $20–$100. While small, this cost can compound with transport, time off work, and the hospital excess to create a material total burden for low-income households.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **NBCSP 20-year performance record**: The NBCSP began full national rollout in 2014–2019 after a decade of gradual age/frequency expansion. A retrospective analysis of what the program has actually achieved in terms of mortality reduction, participation rates over time, and cost-effectiveness would provide the evidence base for whether the current model (biennial iFOBT for 50–74) is still fit for purpose.

2. **PHI structural reform: Gold/Silver minimum for NBCSP coverage**: A targeted policy proposal — requiring that any PHI product marketed to the 50+ age group cover gastrointestinal endoscopy — would address the category-coverage gap identified in this report without broad PHI architecture reform.

3. **Nurse navigator/patient navigation programs for NBCSP follow-up**: The colonoscopy access literature consistently identifies patient navigation as the highest-return intervention for improving follow-up rates after positive screens. The payment model for nurse navigators (MBS vs block grant vs PHN commissioning) is a specific open question.

4. **Colonoscopy workforce capacity: gastroenterologist supply and training numbers**: The bottleneck on the public system follow-up pathway is proceduralist supply. Understanding how gastroenterologist training pipeline numbers are set — and whether the college bottleneck identified in Report 028 applies here — would explain why wait times persist despite rising demand.

5. **Private hospital sector viability and colonoscopy provision**: Private hospitals provide ~40% of all colonoscopies performed nationally. The 2024 private hospital financial viability review flagged serious concerns about hospital profitability; if private hospitals exit regional markets, the private-vs-public cost trade-off collapses in those areas, pushing all patients onto a public system already at capacity.
