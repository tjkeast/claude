# The CPSP 2027 Cliff: Australia's 25,000-Person Psychosocial Support Gap and the Race to Build What Comes Next

**Report 053 — 2026-07-29**

**Summary:** The Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP), which funds non-clinical mental health support for approximately 25,000 Australians with severe mental illness who do not qualify for the NDIS, expires on 30 June 2027 with no confirmed successor architecture. A $272.1 million two-year extension from MYEFO 2024-25 bought time — and an independent evaluation running until mid-2027 — but the government has not yet announced how the post-CPSP landscape will be structured. Against a backdrop of 230,500 unmet severe cases and an NDIS that is simultaneously tightening its criteria, the 2027 transition is becoming one of Australia's most consequential unresolved mental health policy questions.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 052 examined NDIS-funded psychosocial recovery coaching — the formal, market-priced support for the 65,000 NDIS participants with primary psychosocial disability. The CPSP is the parallel system for everyone who doesn't qualify for the NDIS: same population of people with severe mental illness, profoundly different funding infrastructure, and a deadline in eleven months that could strip 25,000 people of their only non-clinical support.

---

## Background

Australia's approach to non-hospital, non-clinical mental health support has been characterised for fifteen years by a sequence of programs without a settled architecture.

The precursor programs established in the early 2010s were substantial. **Partners in Recovery (PIR)**, launched in 2013, supported 35,000 people with severe and persistent mental illness (SPMI) through a national coordination model. Alongside it ran **Personal Helpers and Mentors (PHaMs)** and **Support for Day to Day Living in the Community (D2DL)** — both funded through the Commonwealth, both delivering community-based psychosocial support to people with severe mental illness.

The NDIS rollout from 2013 was supposed to absorb many of these clients into the scheme. That assumption proved wrong at scale. Many people with psychosocial disability — including those with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or severe, recurrent depression — either did not meet the NDIS's functional impairment threshold, could not navigate the access process, or were served by programs not designed to transition into the NDIS model. The result was a transfer problem: programs wound down as NDIS entered regions, but clients did not follow.

In 2019, the **National Psychosocial Support Transition (NPS-T)** program ran for twelve months (1 July 2019 – 30 June 2020) to test former PIR, PHaMs, and D2DL clients for NDIS eligibility and bridge those who didn't qualify. This was followed by the **National Psychosocial Support (NPS) measure** in 2020-21, before the government consolidated remaining Commonwealth-funded psychosocial support into the **Commonwealth Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP)** via a 2021-22 Budget allocation of $171.3 million for two years to June 2023.

The pattern was set: successive two-year extensions, each buying time for a policy decision that has not been made. The 2024-25 Mid-Year Economic and Fiscal Outlook (MYEFO) provided the most recent extension — $272.1 million to 30 June 2027.

## Key Findings

### 1. What the CPSP Is — and What It Is Not

The CPSP is a **non-clinical, low-intensity, community-based support program** for adults with severe mental illness and associated psychosocial functional impairment who are:
- **Not eligible for the NDIS**, or
- Not able to access supports through public mental health services

Delivered through **Primary Health Networks (PHNs)**, which commission local community-managed organisations to run services, the program offers:

- Connecting people with clinical care and other services
- Building capacity in managing day-to-day activities (budgeting, cooking, routines)
- Strengthening social skills and community connections
- Supporting educational and vocational engagement

The program is explicitly **not clinical care** — no therapy, no prescribing, no case management in the clinical sense. It is also explicitly **not long-term**: the design intent is for supports of up to 12 months, with participants expected to either transition to NDIS, stabilise into mainstream services, or exit.

In practice, the 12-month cap is a persistent tension. Severe mental illness is rarely resolved in a year; clients exiting the CPSP often return, and service providers in the sector report that revolving engagement is common.

The $272.1 million MYEFO extension covered:
- $267.9 million for PHN funding arrangements (two-year extension)
- $1.8 million for sector support and capacity-building
- $0.3 million for Mental Illness Fellowship of Australia (MIFA) advocacy activities

At approximately $272 million over two years, the program costs around $136 million per year, or approximately **$5,440 per person per year** for 25,000 clients — a fraction of what NDIS participants with psychosocial disability receive ($88,700 average in 2024-25).

### 2. The Scale of Unmet Need the CPSP Addresses — Partially

On 16 August 2024, Australian Health and Mental Health Ministers released the **Final Report of the Analysis of Unmet Need for Psychosocial Supports Outside of the National Disability Insurance Scheme** (Health Policy Analysis, 2024). The figures were stark:

- **230,500 people with severe mental illness** would benefit from psychosocial supports but cannot access them through either the NDIS or existing programs
- **263,100 people with moderate mental illness** are in a similar position

The CPSP's 25,000 clients represent approximately **10% of the severe cohort** alone. The program is not bridging the gap — it is providing triage-level coverage to a sliver of the population that needs it.

Federal Health Minister Mark Butler acknowledged this figure publicly: 230,000 Australians living with severe mental illness currently receive no support through either the NDIS or psychosocial support programs. The CPSP, even at full operation, leaves the vast majority of this cohort unserved.

### 3. The 2026-27 Budget: Extension of the Agreement, Silence on CPSP's Future

The 2026-27 Federal Budget (May 2026) provided $277.5 million to extend the **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement** to 30 June 2027. This agreement governs Commonwealth-state mental health funding responsibilities, and its extension — like the CPSP extension — is a twelve-month stay of execution rather than a structural settlement.

The CPSP itself did not receive a new allocation in the 2026-27 budget. This is technically expected: the MYEFO extension already funds the program to June 2027. But it means there is no announced architecture for what happens on 1 July 2027 — neither a continuation of CPSP nor a confirmed replacement.

The budget's headline contribution to the post-2027 question was a **$3 billion Commonwealth commitment for Foundational Supports outside the NDIS**. However:

- $2 billion of this is earmarked for **Thriving Kids**, a program for children with developmental needs
- The remaining $1 billion for adult foundational supports — which would be the natural CPSP replacement vehicle — is held in **contingency**, pending state and territory matching agreements
- The adult psychosocial foundational support model has not been publicly designed or detailed
- States and territories must match the Commonwealth's investment, and no bilateral agreements have been announced

The RANZCP, commenting on the budget, noted that as government reforms the NDIS, it must ensure people with psychosocial disability are not pushed from one overstretched system to another, and that savings within disability systems must be matched by real investment in foundational psychosocial supports.

### 4. The Foundational Supports Framework: A Promised Architecture Without a Blueprint

The **NDIS Review Final Report** (December 2023) proposed a fundamental redesign of Australia's disability support ecosystem. A core recommendation was the creation of **Foundational Supports** — a new tier of jointly funded, universally accessible supports outside the NDIS that would serve people with disability who don't qualify for the scheme or need assistance below NDIS intensity.

For psychosocial disability specifically, foundational supports represent the clearest post-CPSP pathway. The December 2023 National Cabinet agreement committed both levels of government to a **$10 billion total foundational supports investment** ($5 billion Commonwealth plus matching from states and territories). But the details remain thin:

- **Thriving Kids** ($2 billion Commonwealth commitment) is the most advanced piece — targeted at children with developmental concerns, autism, and support needs below the NDIS threshold
- **Adult psychosocial foundational supports** — the piece relevant to CPSP clients — are still in consultation design phase
- The target commencement date is **July 2027**, which is the same month the CPSP expires

The coincidence of timelines is the central policy risk. If adult psychosocial foundational supports are not operational by 1 July 2027, and if the CPSP is not extended again, 25,000 people will transition into a gap that has no structure.

The 2026-27 budget documents describe the remaining foundational supports funding as subject to "jurisdictional consultation" — bureaucratic language indicating that state and territory matching agreements are not yet in place. National Cabinet must agree; individual state treasuries must commit; and the design of services must be finalised before any dollar reaches a community organisation.

Eleven months is a very short time to complete that process.

### 5. What an NDIS Tightening Means for the CPSP Population

Simultaneous with the CPSP transition uncertainty, the NDIS is in the middle of its own reform cycle. The 2023 Review's sustainability concerns have translated into a more restrictive access framework:

- **New Framework Planning commences April 2027** — a redesigned planning process with functional capacity assessments as the primary basis for access
- **Functional capacity assessments become mandatory for access from 1 January 2028**
- The Access to Disability Support (ADS) legislation, passed in 2025, is designed to reduce the number of people accessing the NDIS for conditions that can be addressed through mainstream services

For people with psychosocial disability, this creates a pincer dynamic: the NDIS is tightening its access criteria for this cohort at exactly the moment that the CPSP — the non-NDIS safety net — is facing expiry without a confirmed replacement. The RANZCP and sector bodies have warned explicitly that NDIS tightening must be matched by investment in the foundational supports tier, or the net effect will be a reduction in total system capacity for the most vulnerable.

### 6. The History of Serial Extensions

It is worth mapping the pattern of CPSP predecessor extensions to understand the structural dynamic:

| Program | Period | Commonwealth Funding | Clients |
|---|---|---|---|
| PIR + PHaMs + D2DL | 2013–2019 | ~$530M over five years | ~35,000 |
| NPS-T (transition) | 2019–2020 | Short-term | ~25,000 |
| NPS | 2020–2021 | Interim | ~25,000 |
| CPSP (Budget 2021-22) | 2021–2023 | $171.3M | ~25,000 |
| CPSP (extended) | 2023–2025 | ~$130M | ~25,000 |
| CPSP (MYEFO 2024-25) | 2025–2027 | $272.1M | ~25,000 |

The client number has remained essentially static across a decade: 25,000 people receiving support from a program that has never achieved a stable policy basis. Each extension is presented as buying time for a longer-term solution; each longer-term solution has not materialised.

This pattern has direct implications for the community sector. NGOs commissioned to deliver CPSP services operate on short funding horizons, making it structurally difficult to build specialist psychosocial workforces, develop innovative service models, or invest in infrastructure. The workforce instability documented in Report 050 (NGO mental health wages and burnout) is partly a consequence of this rolling-extension model.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Can adult psychosocial foundational supports be designed, negotiated, and operational by July 2027?** The timeline requires National Cabinet agreement on cost-sharing, finalisation of the adult model design, bilateral agreements with eight jurisdictions, and commissioning of services — all in under twelve months. There is no public evidence that design is complete enough to meet this timeline. If it slips, the CPSP will need a further extension — or 25,000 people will lose support.

**2. The 10% coverage problem.** The CPSP serves 25,000 of 230,500+ people with severe unmet need. Even a perfect handover to foundational supports does not resolve the fundamental underfunding of this tier. The foundational supports architecture, if it arrives, will face immediate pressure to cover a far larger population than the CPSP ever served.

**3. The 12-month limit is in tension with the population.** Severe mental illness is lifelong. A program designed for short-term support transitions has served the same 25,000-person cohort for a decade, because the people who need it are not resolving and moving on. Any successor program must reckon honestly with whether a time-limited model fits the population.

**4. State and territory matching is unresolved.** The $10 billion foundational supports commitment requires $5 billion from states and territories. Not all jurisdictions are in the same fiscal position; some have significant mental health reform obligations underway (Victoria's Royal Commission implementation, NSW's community mental health shortfalls). A Commonwealth contingency fund that only unlocks when state matching is confirmed creates a coordination risk.

**5. Structural displacement from NDIS tightening.** If functional capacity assessments from January 2028 result in NDIS exits for people with psychosocial disability, and if foundational supports aren't operational or adequately funded to absorb them, the sector will face a displacement wave — people losing NDIS plans who have no immediate alternative.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **NDIS plan management market and the psychosocial participant experience** — plan managers handle over $3 billion in NDIS funds; how their commercial incentives shape support choices, including recovery coaching uptake, for participants with psychosocial disability
2. **National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement renegotiation** — the current Agreement expires June 2027 alongside the CPSP; what the new Agreement needs to resolve about Commonwealth-state boundary, and what it means for community sector funding
3. **The Medicare Levy and health hypothecation** — raised approximately $31 billion in 2024-25 but is consolidated revenue; how the debate about dedicated mental health funding (raised acutely by the Victoria levy in Report 048) intersects with the CPSP/foundational supports gap
4. **NDIS cost trajectory and the psychosocial cohort** — total NDIS expenditure approaching $50 billion annually; how the psychosocial disability cohort ($5.7 billion) contributes to cost growth and what tightening access means for scheme sustainability vs human outcomes
5. **The role of housing in psychosocial support** — severe mental illness correlates with housing instability; the CPSP and its predecessors all noted the inadequacy of mental health supports disconnected from stable housing, raising whether foundational supports need a housing interface to work
