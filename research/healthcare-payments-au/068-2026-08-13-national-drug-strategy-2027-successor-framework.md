# The National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 Is Expiring: What Comes Next?

**Report 068 — 2026-08-13**

**Summary:** Australia's National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 — the overarching national framework for drug and alcohol policy — expires at the end of 2026. Its successor has not been announced. What is underway is a parallel, operationally urgent reform: the Commonwealth's Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP), which funds the NGO sector delivering most treatment services, is being redesigned for a new three-year grant round commencing July 2027. A $727 million investment is locked in, but the program logic, key performance indicators, and needs-based funding model are all in active consultation. This report maps what the current strategy achieved and failed to achieve, the structural funding imbalance that the successor must confront (64% of Australia's drug budget goes to law enforcement, 1.6% to harm reduction), and the major contested issues that will define whether the next framework is a genuine reform or a reconfigured status quo.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 067 documented that Australia has no dedicated MBS item for alcohol brief intervention — a primary care gap it called "a political economy failure driven by the alcohol industry's lobbying power, the low clinical status of addiction medicine, and the persistent underfunding of prevention." That gap is one thread in a larger structural failure: the whole of Australia's drug and alcohol policy architecture — what services exist, how they are funded, and what the government is trying to achieve — is governed by the National Drug Strategy, which is about to expire. The successor framework and the DAP redesign will determine whether an MBS brief intervention item, better First Nations pharmacotherapy access, and a more balanced pillar-funding model can actually be embedded in the system.

## Background: The National Drug Strategy Framework

### History and Structure

Australia has had a coordinated national drug policy framework since 1985. The current iteration — the **National Drug Strategy 2017–2026** — represents Australia's fifth such framework. It is a high-level, 10-year policy document agreed between the Commonwealth and all states and territories through the **Ministerial Drug and Alcohol Forum (MDAF)**, formerly the Ministerial Council on Drug Strategy (MCDS).

The NDS 2017–2026 is built on **three pillars**, each representing a different approach to reducing drug-related harms:

1. **Demand reduction** — reducing the uptake of harmful drug use and motivating cessation; includes prevention, education, brief intervention, and treatment
2. **Supply reduction** — disrupting the production, supply, and availability of illicit drugs; primarily law enforcement, border control, and criminal justice
3. **Harm minimisation** — reducing harm to individuals, families, and communities from drug use regardless of whether use continues; includes needle and syringe programs, naloxone distribution, supervised injecting facilities, and pharmacotherapy

The strategy commits the Australian government to "balance" these three pillars. Its stated aim is reducing the "harmful effects of alcohol and other drugs" — which positions it primarily as a *public health* framework, not a *criminal justice* framework — even though, as the spending data shows (see below), it has never been funded that way.

**Six sub-strategies** sit beneath the NDS 2017–2026, each targeting a specific substance or population:
- National Alcohol Strategy 2019–2028 (separately indexed; covered in prior reports)
- National Ice Strategy (methamphetamine)
- National Tobacco Strategy
- Workforce Development Strategy
- National Syringe Programs Strategy
- National Youth Alcohol and Drug Framework

### Governance

The NDS is overseen by the MDAF, a ministerial-level body that meets annually and includes Commonwealth and state/territory health and law enforcement ministers. Below it, an **Intergovernmental Committee on Drugs (IGCD)** handles technical and implementation matters. The MDAF can direct revisions to sub-strategies and commission national monitoring through the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW).

The **Midpoint Scorecard for the NDS 2017–2026** was produced in 2021, covering the first five years of the strategy's implementation. It assessed progress against 21 priority areas and provided evidence on key indicators.

## Key Findings

### 1. Where Australia Actually Spends Its Drug Budget

The most revealing analysis of the NDS in action comes from the **Drug Policy Modelling Program (DPMP)** at UNSW Sydney, which has tracked Australia's aggregate drug policy expenditure since 2002. The most recent report, "The Australian Drug Budget 2021–22," released in 2024, provides the definitive picture of how the three-pillar balance plays out in practice:

**Total government drug policy expenditure (2021–22): $5.45 billion**

| Pillar / Category | Expenditure | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Law enforcement** (supply reduction) | $3.50 billion | **64.3%** |
| **Treatment** (demand reduction) | $1.48 billion | 27.4% |
| **Prevention** (demand reduction) | $363 million | 6.7% |
| **Harm reduction** | $89 million | **1.6%** |

The spending breakdown reveals a fundamental structural mismatch between the strategy's stated three-pillar balance and its actual funding priorities:

- **Harm minimisation — the distinctively Australian contribution to global drug policy — receives less than 2 cents in every dollar.** This proportion has *fallen* since the last comparable report (2009–10), when it stood at 2.2%, which itself had fallen from 3.9% in 2002–03 — the early years of Australia's world-leading needle and syringe program expansion.

- **Prevention spending has also declined**, from 9.5% in 2009–10 to 6.7% in 2021–22. This despite consecutive National Drug Strategies committing to increasing investment in early intervention and demand reduction.

- **Law enforcement's share has barely moved** — 64.9% in 2009–10, 64.3% in 2021–22 — and is effectively the structural baseline around which all other categories fluctuate.

This data matters enormously for the successor framework. If the NDS 2017–2026 committed to balancing three pillars but produced a 64:34:2 spending ratio, the question for the NDS successor is whether there will be any mechanism to actually shift the balance — or whether "harm minimisation" will remain a rhetorical pillar, proportionally shrinking with each successive strategy.

### 2. What the Midpoint Scorecard (2021) Found

The 2021 midpoint scorecard assessed progress against 21 NDS priority areas. The assessment was largely qualitative, reflecting the NDS's weak performance measurement architecture. Key findings relevant to the payment and funding analysis:

- **Brief intervention in primary care** remained a persistent implementation gap. Rates of alcohol and drug brief interventions delivered by GPs and pharmacists were well below clinically indicated levels — the same finding later confirmed by the House Standing Committee Inquiry.
- **Workforce shortages** in the AOD sector were identified as a critical emerging constraint. The specialist AOD workforce was insufficient to meet demand, particularly in rural and remote areas and for First Nations communities.
- **Data quality** remained a limitation: the NDS lacked measurable, time-bound targets against which progress could be assessed. Most indicators relied on AIHW NDSHS (National Drug Strategy Household Survey) data, which is 3-yearly — meaning the NDS has only had two full NDSHS cycles (2019 and 2022–23) within its ten-year span.
- **Coordination between pillars** was assessed as inadequate: supply reduction activities (law enforcement seizures, arrests) were not consistently connected to demand reduction services, and people exiting criminal justice frequently had no pathway into treatment.

### 3. The Treatment System: More Agencies, Fewer Patients

AIHW's **Alcohol and Other Drug Treatment Services in Australia Annual Report 2024–25** reveals a counterintuitive structural dynamic:

- In 2024–25, **1,316 publicly funded agencies** delivered AOD treatment nationally — a **66% increase** from 791 agencies in 2015–16.
- Despite this expansion, **127,800 people** received treatment in 2024–25 — **down 8.2%** from 139,295 in 2019–20.
- Total treatment episodes were **244,400**, delivered by a mix of government and non-government services.

The apparent paradox — more agencies but fewer people in treatment — reflects several dynamics: pandemic disruption to service patterns, changes in how agencies are counted, and, most significantly, a treatment system that has fragmented into many small providers unable to generate the scale economies and throughput of larger services. The NGO AOD sector (which delivers approximately 50% of all treatment nationally, and a higher proportion in some states) is characterised by short-term grant-funded services, high administrative overhead per patient, and high staff turnover due to uncompetitive wages.

The most common treatment types across non-government services were:
- Counselling: 32%
- Support and case management: 22%
- Assessment only: 15%

The most common across government services were:
- Assessment only: 36%
- Counselling: 31%
- Withdrawal management (detox): 13%

Notably absent from the top treatment types at scale: **residential rehabilitation** (a high-cost, high-impact intervention increasingly reserved for severe presentations) and **pharmacotherapy** (naltrexone, buprenorphine/methadone, acamprosate — covered in detail in Reports 058 and 065). The treatment system's modal intervention is counselling and assessment, not evidence-based pharmacotherapy.

### 4. Drug Use in Australia: The 2022–23 Household Survey

The most recent NDSHS (2022–23; 21,663 respondents; fieldwork by Roy Morgan Research) provides the demand-side context for the successor framework:

- **17.9%** of Australians aged 14+ used an illicit drug in the past 12 months — up from 16.4% in 2019, the highest recorded rate in the survey's history.
- **Cannabis**: 11.5% of the population (~2.5 million people) — the most used illicit drug, stable trend.
- **Cocaine**: 4.5% (~1 million people) — the second-most used illicit drug; significant increase over the decade.
- **MDMA/ecstasy**: 3.3%
- **Methamphetamine/amphetamines**: 1.0% (~200,000 people) — lower than cocaine by population share, but the most clinically complex and socially damaging drug in Australia's treatment system. Methamphetamine (as "ice" — high-purity crystalline methamphetamine) accounted for 50.1% of injections at Sydney's MSIC as of 2025 (up from 2–7% in 2002–2011), reflecting the structural shift from a heroin-era to a stimulant-era drug treatment system.
- **Prescription drug misuse** (pharmaceuticals used non-medically): 4.2% — an area of growing concern, particularly opioid analgesics (covered in Report 059).

Despite Australia having avoided the fentanyl catastrophe that has killed hundreds of thousands in North America — largely because tight PBS prescribing controls and border interdiction have limited fentanyl import — the emerging risk of synthetic opioid contamination of illicit drug markets remains the most dangerous unresolved threat the successor strategy must address (covered in Reports 060–061).

### 5. The DAP Reform: The Operational Successor Framework

While a formal successor NDS document has not been announced, the most consequential structural reform underway is the redesign of the **Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP)** — the Commonwealth's primary grant mechanism for funding NGO AOD services.

Through the **2025–26 Mid-Year Economic and Fiscal Outlook (MYEFO)**, the Australian Government:
- **Consolidated** all DAP terminating and ongoing funding into a single, ongoing program
- **Committed $727 million over three years** from 2026–27 (approximately $242 million per year), followed by **$244.2 million annually from 2029–30 onwards** as a permanent ongoing appropriation
- **Extended all current DAP grants** due to expire in June 2026 for one year, to provide sector stability while the redesign is completed
- Committed to a **new round of three-year grant agreements commencing 1 July 2027**

The DAP redesign consultation ran in **three stages**:
- **Stage 1** (February 20 – March 13, 2026): Open public consultation on the current program, sector needs, and broad principles
- **Stage 2** (March 16 – April 13, 2026): Consultation on updated program logic, key performance indicators (KPIs), and data inputs for a **needs-based funding model**
- **Stage 3**: Further refinement and grant design

Critically, the Department of Health stated that the redesign **"will not change the fundamental objectives or service types"** — meaning the DAP is not being reconceptualised from scratch, but refined in its targeting, measurement, and distribution mechanisms.

The shift to a **needs-based funding model** is the most significant structural change proposed. Currently, grant allocations largely reflect historical funding patterns — prior grantees receive renewals regardless of whether their catchment population's needs have changed or whether their model of service delivery remains optimal. A needs-based model would theoretically direct funding toward populations with the highest unmet need, which DPMP and sector peak bodies have consistently argued means: rural and remote communities, First Nations populations, communities experiencing high methamphetamine prevalence, and populations with co-occurring mental health and AOD conditions.

### 6. Peak Body Positions: NADA's April 2026 Call for Overhaul

In April 2026 — at the close of the Stage 2 consultation — **NADA (Network of Alcohol and other Drugs Agencies)**, the NSW peak body for the non-government AOD sector, publicly called for a funding overhaul. Key positions:

- The NSW AOD sector needs an additional **$13 million per year** for five years just to address the existing treatment gap in NSW alone
- On any given day, more than **1,800 people are waiting for NGO services** in NSW
- NGO services deliver **nearly 50% of all AOD treatment** in NSW but operate on short-term, precarious funding that creates structural service instability and high staff turnover
- For every dollar invested in NGO AOD services, **$7 is saved** in downstream health, justice, policing, and community services costs
- The DAP consultation should produce grants of minimum five-year duration (currently three years) to allow workforce planning and service development

These positions mirror submissions from NADA's national counterparts (including ATODA in the ACT, VAADA in Victoria, and peak bodies in other states) and reflect a sector in structural distress — high demand, inadequate funding, workforce exodus, and funding cycle uncertainty.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Three Pillars on Paper, One in Practice

The most persistent tension in Australian drug policy is the gap between the NDS's stated three-pillar balance and the 64:34:2 spending reality. Every NDS since 1985 has proclaimed harm minimisation as a core, co-equal pillar. No mechanism has ever required the spending ratio to reflect this balance.

The successor framework will need to decide whether to:
(a) Retain the three-pillar framework as a rhetorical commitment with no funding floor guarantees (status quo)
(b) Embed a minimum harm reduction spending floor (e.g., 5% of total drug-related government expenditure, matching Australia's stated prevention ambition)
(c) Restructure the framework to explicitly acknowledge that 64% on law enforcement is a political choice, not a public health one, and begin shifting it

Option (b) or (c) would represent genuine reform. Option (a) is what every previous strategy has done.

### Tension 2: Synthetic Opioids and a New Threat Architecture

The NDS 2017–2026 was designed for a drug threat landscape dominated by heroin, methamphetamine, cannabis, and pharmaceutical opioids. It predates:
- The emergence of **illicit fentanyl contamination of non-opioid drug supplies** in North America, which has killed over 100,000 Americans annually since 2021
- The significant uptake of **naloxone distribution reform** in Australia (Report 061)
- The emergence of **drug checking services** as a harm reduction modality (Report 060)
- The stimulant dominance of the injecting drug scene in Australian capital cities

A successor strategy that does not explicitly address synthetic opioid risk, fentanyl strip testing infrastructure, and the clinical management of stimulant-era injecting drug use will be structurally obsolete on release.

### Tension 3: MBS Integration — The Unbridged Gap

Every prior report in this series has encountered the same structural gap: the Commonwealth's clinical payment system (Medicare/MBS) and its community services funding system (DAP grants, PHN commissioning, NDIS) operate in disconnected silos. A GP providing alcohol brief intervention cannot bill a dedicated MBS item. A peer support worker cannot bill Medicare. A community AOD counsellor working under a DAP grant is invisible to the MBS data system.

The successor NDS and the DAP redesign represent an opportunity to explicitly design MBS integration points — particularly for alcohol and cannabis brief intervention, pharmacotherapy prescribing incentives, and GP-to-AOD-service care coordination. Without a policy mechanism that explicitly bridges the MBS and the grant-funded community sector, the stepped care model that brief intervention is supposed to sit within cannot function.

### Tension 4: First Nations AOD Services — Persistent Structural Failure

As documented in Reports 013, 014, and 058, First Nations Australians bear disproportionate AOD-related harm while the funding and service architecture for culturally safe, community-controlled AOD services remains inadequate. The DAP redesign's move toward needs-based funding should theoretically direct more resources to First Nations communities with the highest unmet need — but only if the needs assessment methodology adequately weights First Nations-specific indicators (remoteness, co-occurring trauma, cultural safety requirements, ACCHO versus mainstream service access) rather than relying on simple population counts.

The **National Alcohol and Other Drug Workforce Development Strategy** (a sub-strategy of the NDS) has been chronically underresourced in building First Nations AOD workforce capacity. Without a pipeline of culturally safe, First Nations-led services, needs-based funding directed at those communities will default to funding mainstream providers — potentially undermining community control principles.

### Tension 5: The Mental Health Interface — Still Not Resolved

Reports 047–056 in this series documented the fragmented boundary between mental health and AOD services. The NDS 2017–2026 largely treated AOD as a health issue distinct from mental health, despite approximately **50% of people with severe mental illness also having a co-occurring AOD condition** (the "missing middle" documented in Report 017). The NDS's successor must explicitly address the mental health–AOD interface — including the role of Primary Health Networks in commissioning integrated services, the MBS mental health treatment plan's limited utility for patients with co-occurring AOD presentations, and the funding responsibility gap between the NDIS (for psychosocial disability) and state AOD services (for treatment episodes).

### Tension 6: Does the NDS Successor Even Get Released?

A formal successor National Drug Strategy requires agreement from all state and territory governments through the MDAF. Historically, these negotiations have been slow and contentious — reflecting genuinely different state and territory policy positions (particularly on supervised injecting facilities and decriminalisation, where the ACT has diverged sharply from NSW and Queensland). The NDS 2017–2026 was itself delayed; its predecessor (the 2010–2015 strategy) was extended multiple times before the current document was finalised.

It is plausible that the successor NDS will not be released before mid-2027. In the meantime, the **DAP redesign** and the **House Standing Committee's final report** (expected H2 2026) will together constitute the de facto successor policy architecture — a pattern where operational grant funding decisions precede, and effectively substitute for, the strategic framework document.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Primary Health Networks (PHNs) as AOD commissioning agents:** PHNs are the Commonwealth's primary mechanism for commissioning regional AOD services under the DAP. How do they work, how much do they spend on AOD services, and how do their commissioning decisions connect to (or bypass) First Nations community-controlled organisations? This is the mid-level between the national DAP framework and individual service providers.

2. **The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030:** Australia's global reputation for tobacco harm reduction (plain packaging, high excise) is being tested by the vaping crisis — a two-front challenge involving youth initiation into nicotine through disposable e-cigarettes and the disruption of therapeutic nicotine vaping as a cessation aid. How is this being funded and what does it mean for MBS cessation support items?

3. **Synthetic opioid readiness — Australia's preparedness for a fentanyl crisis:** Reports 060–061 documented naloxone and drug checking. This report would assess the *whole system* readiness: treatment capacity surge, emergency department protocols, forensic toxicology capacity, and what the successor NDS would need to commit to in order to avoid Australia replicating the North American experience.

4. **ACT decriminalisation — one year on:** The ACT's personal drug possession decriminalisation scheme took effect in October 2023. By mid-2026 there are approximately 30 months of real-world data on deflections, treatment uptake, arrest data, and community outcomes. A report examining the ACT evidence would inform whether the successor NDS should recommend national decriminalisation of personal possession as a demand reduction strategy.

5. **The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030 and vaping payment flows:** Vaping regulation reform (TGA scheduling of nicotine vapes, PBS listing of therapeutic vaping, and the illicit disposable vape market) represents one of the most significant AOD policy shifts of the current government. How is it funded and how does it connect to the NDS framework?
