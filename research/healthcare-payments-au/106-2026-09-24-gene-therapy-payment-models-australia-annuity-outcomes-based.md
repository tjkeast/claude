# Gene Therapy Payment Models in Australia: The Million-Dollar Medicine Problem

**One-line summary:** Australia has approved the world's most expensive medicines via PBS while its HTA framework, pricing infrastructure, and payment architecture are still being redesigned to accommodate them.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 105 showed enzyme replacement therapies for lysosomal storage diseases costing up to USD $2–6 million per patient per year indefinitely — gene therapies promise to replace those ongoing costs with a single curative dose, but at an upfront price that the current PBS payment model was not built to handle.

---

## Background

Australia listed its first PBS gene therapy — Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec) for spinal muscular atrophy — in February 2023 at approximately AU$2.5 million per treatment. At the time, it was the most expensive single medicine ever listed on the PBS.

That record is unlikely to hold. A pipeline of gene therapies is advancing through TGA and MSAC/PBAC assessment, including treatments for haemophilia B, beta-thalassaemia, sickle cell disease, and further lysosomal storage diseases. Several of these carry international list prices of USD $3–5 million per dose. For ultra-rare LSDs, as examined in Report 105, gene therapy candidates in development could displace ERT expenditure of USD $2–6 million per patient per year — but only if a viable reimbursement pathway exists.

The structural question Australia is now confronting is not whether to fund gene therapies; it has already done so. The question is whether the current PBS framework — built around recurrent prescriptions, a 5% discount rate, and an implicit ICER threshold of approximately AU$50,000/QALY — can systematically accommodate a new generation of one-time, high-upfront-cost, potentially curative medicines without requiring case-by-case political interventions.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Discount Rate Problem

Australia's PBAC uses a 5% discount rate in pharmacoeconomic evaluations. For a recurrent therapy, this matters relatively little — costs and benefits both accrue over time and discount similarly. For a gene therapy with a one-time upfront cost and benefits accruing over a patient's lifetime, the asymmetry is severe.

Medicines Australia describes this as a "double whammy": the full upfront cost is counted at face value, while the long-term health benefits are discounted back to a present value that often makes the therapy appear economically unattractive. A therapy delivering 30 years of benefit at 5% discount has its Year 20–30 health gains valued at roughly 40–50% of their undiscounted worth.

In 2022, the PBAC reviewed its discount rate. By September 2025, the PBAC's interim advice recommended reducing the base-case rate to no lower than 3.5–4% per year for technologies with upfront costs and long-term benefits, including gene therapies and some vaccines. Medicines Australia had originally sought 1.5% — in line with Canada and England — and described the PBAC recommendation as a step in the right direction while questioning the delay in implementation. As of the HTA IAG's interim report (September 2025), the discount rate change had not been formally implemented but was included in the reform roadmap.

### 2. Australia's Current Gene Therapy Payment Architecture

Australia has funded gene therapies via three routes:

**PBS (PBAC pathway):** Used for Zolgensma. As of June 2025, there were 316 active deeds of agreement between the Commonwealth and pharmaceutical companies covering special pricing and risk-sharing arrangements. The Zolgensma agreement is confidential, but the PBAC's published recommendation specified that any outcomes-based risk-sharing arrangement should include rebates for the cost of treatment over at least five years post-listing where patients die, are placed on invasive permanent ventilation, or require subsequent PBS-subsidised treatment with nusinersen or risdiplam. This is Australia's most detailed public description of an outcomes-based gene therapy agreement.

**HST (MSAC pathway):** Used for CAR-T therapies (Kymriah, Yescarta, Tecartus, Carvykti) and Luxturna. The Highly Specialised Therapies program is joint Commonwealth-state funded and restricted to public hospitals. Patients treated in private hospitals face full commercial cost (AU$450,000–$700,000 for CAR-T), with PHI covering hospitalisation but not the therapeutic agent.

**LSDP:** Covers ultra-rare LSDs outside the PBS cost-effectiveness framework, as examined in Report 105.

### 3. The Hemgenix Rejection: A Structural Test Case

The most instructive recent case is Hemgenix (etranacogene dezaparvovec), a gene therapy for haemophilia B with a US list price of approximately US$3.5 million. The TGA provisionally approved Hemgenix in March 2024 for adults with severe or moderately severe haemophilia B.

MSAC did not support public funding. Its reasons are a precise catalogue of the structural problems facing gene therapy reimbursement in Australia:
- Short-term effectiveness was highly variable across patients
- Long-term safety and durability data were insufficient
- No validated neutralising antibody (NAb) test existed to identify eligible patients
- The very high price, combined with the above uncertainties, meant it did not provide value for money under current evaluation criteria

Italy's AIFA reached a different conclusion: in July 2025, AIFA approved Hemgenix for reimbursement under Italy's "payment by results" model, under which hospitals pay in instalments and do not make further payments if the therapy fails to achieve specified clinical milestones. The contrast illustrates how payment architecture shapes access decisions: Italy's outcome-contingent model accommodated clinical uncertainty by shifting financial risk to the manufacturer; Australia's framework, lacking an equivalent mechanism, defaulted to rejection.

### 4. The Roctavian Withdrawal: What Durability Uncertainty Looks Like

BioMarin's valoctocogene roxaparvovec (Roctavian), a haemophilia A gene therapy, received EMA and FDA approval and was briefly considered for multiple health systems including Australia. In Q4 2025, BioMarin withdrew Roctavian from the market following real-world evidence of declining factor VIII levels over time — the therapy's efficacy was not as durable as clinical trial data had suggested.

This is the durability risk that underpins gene therapy economics. The promise is a one-time cure; the risk is a one-time payment for a therapy that progressively fails. No outcomes-based or annuity payment model protects a payer against a therapy that is initially effective but wanes — unless the agreement explicitly includes long-duration performance milestones. The Zolgensma framework's five-year outcome review is an attempt to capture this, but five years is still short relative to a lifetime cure's value proposition.

### 5. Payment Models Available

The international evidence identifies four distinct approaches, all in use somewhere in 2025–26:

**Upfront lump sum with confidential rebate:** The Australian status quo for most PBS listings. The list price is often nominal; the real price is a confidential negotiated rebate. Administratively simple; budget impact in Year 1; no risk-sharing.

**Outcomes-based (performance-based) agreements:** Payment contingent on clinical milestones. Italy's model for CAR-T and Hemgenix; the Kymriah/CMS agreement in the US (payment only if response by Month 1). Australia's Zolgensma agreement approximates this for a gene therapy. Requires measurable, time-bounded outcome criteria and data infrastructure to adjudicate.

**Annuity/instalment payments:** The one-time cost is spread across multiple years, reducing Year-1 budget impact without changing the total or the underlying cost-effectiveness ratio. Relevant to NHS England's evaluation of Casgevy (CRISPR-based therapy for sickle cell disease, listed in January 2025 at a £1.65 million list price under a confidential commercial agreement and managed access scheme). NICE research has shown annuity models can expand access by keeping therapies under England's net budget impact test. They don't resolve cost-effectiveness uncertainty; they restructure cash flows.

**Securitization:** The most novel proposal. A 2026 simulation study published in *Gene Therapy* modelled a scenario in which government-backed securities fund gene therapies for orphan diseases, with 30-year performance-based annuity payments serviced from the healthcare budget over time. Securitization converts a healthcare budget problem into a capital markets problem. No country has implemented this at scale, but the mechanism is technically feasible and could be relevant for very small patient populations where conventional managed entry agreements are impractical.

### 6. HTA Reform and the IAG Agenda

The HTA Policy and Methods Review (September 2024) explicitly identified gene therapies and advanced therapeutics as a driver of reform urgency. The Implementation Advisory Group (IAG), established in November 2024 and chaired by the PBAC chair, consolidated the 50 review recommendations into five focus areas, of which "modernised assessment pathways" directly addresses gene therapy complexity.

Key reform elements expected in the roadmap to flow from the IAG's January 2026 final report include:
- Discount rate reduction to 3.5–4% for technologies with upfront costs and long-term benefits
- New flexible pathways for advanced therapeutic medicinal products (ATMPs), analogous to the EU's ATMP framework
- Enhanced outcomes-based reimbursement frameworks with data collection obligations
- MSAC/PBAC boundary clarification for codependent products (a therapy and its companion diagnostic)
- Workforce capacity investment for HTA evaluation of complex modalities

The 2026–27 Federal Budget committed AU$5.9 billion over five years for new and amended PBS/RPBS listings, signalling continued appetite for expensive listing decisions. But the reform roadmap — not just the funding commitment — determines whether future gene therapies navigate a coherent pathway or repeat the Hemgenix pattern of TGA approval followed by MSAC rejection.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Who bears the durability risk?** If a gene therapy wanes after five years (as Roctavian did), the patient and the health system both bear the cost of re-treatment. Outcomes-based agreements that only look at short-term response do not adequately price this risk.

**State-Commonwealth cost-sharing in HST.** CAR-T therapies are publicly funded only in public hospitals under joint Commonwealth-state arrangements. Patients in regions without public hospital access to these facilities face geographic inequity. No equivalent cost-sharing framework exists for PBS-listed gene therapies.

**The small-population data problem.** Regulatory agencies approve gene therapies on trials of dozens to hundreds of patients. PBAC's evidence requirements are calibrated for much larger populations. The result is systematic under-evidencing at the time of assessment.

**The LSDP/gene therapy intersection.** As gene therapies emerge for conditions currently managed via expensive ERT on the LSDP (Gaucher, Fabry, Pompe, MPS types), the question of whether gene therapy replaces ERT on the LSDP or requires a separate PBS pathway — and how savings are recycled — has not been resolved by policy.
