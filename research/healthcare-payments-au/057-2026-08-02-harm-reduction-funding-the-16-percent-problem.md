# Harm Reduction Funding: The 1.6% Problem — Australia's $89 Million in a $5.45 Billion Drug Budget

**Report 057 — 2026-08-02**

**Summary:** Australia spends $5.45 billion annually on illicit drug policy, but only 1.6% — $89 million — goes to harm reduction. This share has fallen from 3.9% in 2002-03, even as drug-induced deaths hit a record high of 2,091 unintentional deaths in 2024. The evidence base is unambiguous: needle and syringe programs return $1.30–$5.50 per dollar spent; take-home naloxone has a benefit-cost ratio of 16.4; the ACT's full harm reduction package delivers $10.10 in benefits for every $1 invested. Yet Australia operates only two medically supervised injecting centres, refuses to extend needle and syringe programs into prisons, and has a patchwork of drug checking services at constant risk of defunding. With the National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 expiring at the end of this year and no successor consultation announced, the case for rebalancing has never been stronger — and the political barriers have rarely been higher.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 056 examined Australia's co-occurring mental health and AOD service gap and revealed that 64% of Australia's drug budget goes to law enforcement and only 1.6% to harm reduction. This report examines what that 1.6% actually funds, what the evidence says it should fund, and the political economy that keeps the ratio frozen.

## Background

### What Harm Reduction Is

"Harm reduction" is a term of art in drug policy with a precise meaning: interventions designed to reduce the negative consequences of drug use without requiring abstinence. The defining characteristic is pragmatism — working with people where they are, rather than demanding behaviour change before services are available. Harm reduction does not endorse drug use; it acknowledges that it occurs and seeks to minimise the resulting damage to individuals, families, and communities.

The core harm reduction toolkit in the Australian context includes:

- **Needle and Syringe Programs (NSPs):** provide sterile injecting equipment to prevent transmission of blood-borne viruses — principally hepatitis C and HIV — among people who inject drugs
- **Take-Home Naloxone (THN):** distributes the opioid overdose antidote naloxone to people at risk of, or likely to witness, an opioid overdose
- **Medically Supervised Injecting Centres (MSICs):** fixed locations where people can inject pre-obtained drugs under medical supervision, enabling overdose response and health engagement without criminalisation of drug possession at the site
- **Drug Checking Services:** laboratory-based services that test drug samples for identity and contaminants, enabling users to make informed decisions and generating real-time public health intelligence about the drug market
- **Opioid Agonist Treatment (OAT):** while typically classified as "treatment," pharmacotherapy (methadone, buprenorphine) is also a harm reduction modality — it reduces overdose risk and enables engagement with other health services

Harm reduction sits within a tripartite policy framework established by Australia's National Drug Strategy: demand reduction, supply reduction, and harm reduction. In theory these three pillars are balanced. In practice, the funding allocation tells a different story.

### The Drug Budget: $5.45 Billion, 1.6% for Harm Reduction

The most authoritative mapping of Australia's drug budget is produced by the Drug Policy Modelling Program at UNSW Sydney. The 2021–22 estimate — the most recent comprehensive figure — found that Australian governments (Commonwealth, state, and territory combined) spent **$5.45 billion on illicit drug issues**. The allocation:

| Pillar | Amount | Share |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Law enforcement | $3.49 billion | 64.1% |
| Treatment | $1.47 billion | 27.0% |
| Prevention | $363 million | 6.7% |
| Harm reduction | $89 million | 1.6% |
| Other | $30 million | 0.6% |

The law enforcement total includes $1.8 billion on routine policing (drug detection and prosecution), plus courts, corrections, and customs. The harm reduction component — $89 million across all governments — covers NSPs, supervised injecting facilities, and some naloxone programs.

Critically, the harm reduction share has **declined steadily over time**: from 3.9% in 2002–03 to 2.2% by the early 2010s to 1.6% in 2021–22. As overall drug policy spending grew, harm reduction spending grew more slowly, and its proportional weight fell. Meanwhile, the death rate from unintentional drug overdoses rose to its highest level since data collection began in 2001.

## Key Findings

### 1. Drug-Induced Deaths Are at a Record High

Australia's overdose crisis has worsened significantly in the 2020s. The Penington Institute's *Annual Overdose Report 2024* documented **2,091 unintentional drug-related deaths** in 2022 (the most recent year for which final ABS cause-of-death data was available at publication) — the highest ever recorded, and the first time unintentional overdose deaths surpassed 2,000. The rate reached **7.6 deaths per 100,000 people** — the highest per-capita rate on record.

Key characteristics of Australia's overdose crisis:

- **Opioids dominate:** opioids were involved in approximately **2 in 5 unintentional overdose deaths** (around 1,038 deaths), with prescribed opioids — not illicit heroin — driving much of the mortality
- **Older Australians:** people aged 50–59 accounted for the highest number of unintentional overdose deaths (533 in 2022, up 19% from the prior year, and up over 300% from two decades ago) — a cohort that came of age during the heroin epidemic and has aged with entrenched dependency
- **Prescribed medicines:** beyond illicit opioids, prescribed opioids contribute to an estimated 75 hospitalisations and 2 deaths per day nationally
- **Speed of change:** the overdose death rate in 2022 was more than 50% higher than in 2010

This escalating death toll makes the 1.6% harm reduction allocation not merely an accounting anomaly but an active policy failure — harm reduction interventions are precisely the tools best suited to preventing overdose deaths.

### 2. Needle and Syringe Programs: Strong Evidence, Underfunding, and No Prisons

Australia's NSP network is the oldest and most established component of its harm reduction infrastructure. First introduced in 1987, NSPs are now one of the largest in the world relative to population.

**Current scale (2023–24, Kirby Institute NSP NMDC):**
- **4,708 NSP service points** across Australia, including 112 primary NSPs, 918 secondary outlets (pharmacies and other), and 3,220 pharmacy NSPs, plus syringe dispensing machines
- **54.6 million needles and syringes** distributed to **72,984 people** in 2023–24
- **749 needles per person** per year — reflecting the high frequency of injecting among this population
- The network has grown approximately 10% since 2020 (excluding machines), with machines up 24%
- **23% of NSP attendees** identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander — roughly seven times their share of the population, reflecting the concentrated disease burden in First Nations communities

**The evidence for NSPs is among the strongest in public health.** A seminal Monash/UNSW study estimated that Australia's NSP investment during 2000–2010 (costing $245 million) averted 192–873 HIV infections and 19,000–77,000 hepatitis C infections, generating 20,000–66,000 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). The investment yielded $70–220 million in healthcare savings during the period and will generate $340–950 million in additional future savings — a total return of **$1.30–$5.50 for every $1 invested**, with the investment fully recovered in healthcare savings by 2032.

**The prison gap:** Despite this evidence, Australia has **no needle and syringe programs in any prison** in any state or territory. This is a glaring anomaly: people who inject drugs before incarceration continue to inject in prison, often sharing injecting equipment in the absence of sterile supplies — a highly efficient transmission pathway for hepatitis C and HIV.

A landmark 2025 modelling study published in the *Medical Journal of Australia* (Houdroge et al.) projected that introducing a prison NSP covering 50% of people who inject drugs in Australian prisons by 2030 would:

- Avert **894 new hepatitis C infections** (a 30% reduction)
- Prevent **522 hospitalisations** for injection-related bacterial and fungal infections (a 17% reduction)
- Cost approximately **$12.2 million over 2025–30**
- Save **$31.7 million in healthcare costs** — a benefit-cost ratio of **2.6**

Every state and territory has declined to implement prison NSPs. The standard objection is security-based — that needles are potential weapons — despite decades of evidence from international prison NSP programs (operational in over 60 prisons across 12 countries) showing no increase in needle-related violence. The *MJA* study's accompanying commentary noted that Australia's refusal to implement prison NSPs is "inconsistent with the UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, which require health care in prisons equivalent to that in the community."

### 3. Take-Home Naloxone: A Policy Success, But Coverage Remains Patchy

Take-home naloxone — distribution of the opioid overdose antidote to people likely to witness an overdose — is arguably the most straightforward harm reduction investment available. Naloxone is cheap, safe, effective, and requires only a few minutes of training to administer.

Australia's national THN program was strengthened substantially in 2022–23:
- From **1 July 2022**, naloxone became available **free, without a prescription**, at pharmacies and other participating outlets nationwide — a significant policy shift from the previous model requiring a doctor's prescription
- The **2022–23 Federal Budget** allocated **$19.6 million over four years** for the national ongoing THN program
- The program estimates it saves approximately **3 lives per day**

From **July 2025**, a further policy change: all first aid courses accredited in Australia and New Zealand are now required to include instruction on administering intranasal naloxone. This is a meaningful population-level change — first aid training reaches employers, schools, community organisations, and individuals who may not otherwise have engagement with health services.

The ACT's comprehensive harm reduction cost-benefit analysis (March 2025) estimated a benefit-cost ratio of **16.4** for take-home naloxone scale-up — the highest ratio of any intervention modelled. This reflects naloxone's combination of very low cost and very high impact: preventing an overdose death saves substantial health system and societal costs.

However, distribution remains uneven. Take-up of free naloxone has been lower than hoped in some communities, and pharmacy-level dispensing without consultation can miss the populations most at risk. The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners and others have advocated for more proactive prescribing by GPs as part of clinical reviews for patients on high-dose opioids.

### 4. Medically Supervised Injecting Centres: Two Facilities for a Nation of 27 Million

Australia has exactly **two medically supervised injecting centres** — one in Sydney (opened 2001) and one in Melbourne (opened 2018):

**Sydney Medically Supervised Injecting Centre (MSIC), Kings Cross:**
- Operating for over two decades — the longest-running MSIC in Australia
- Estimated **80% reduction in ambulance call-outs** in the local area
- Average monthly opioid-related emergency department presentations have reduced by ~20% at adjacent hospitals
- No overdose death has ever occurred inside the facility in over 20 years of operation

**North Richmond Medically Supervised Injecting Room (MSIR), Melbourne:**
- Opened 2018 as a trial; made **permanent by Victorian legislation in 2023**
- Has safely managed more than **6,750 overdoses** since opening (to December 2022)
- Saved an estimated **up to 63 lives**
- Ambulance attendance involving naloxone administration has fallen by approximately **50% within 1km** of the MSIR
- A 2025 study (Stam et al., *Addiction*) analysing 1,474 witnessed heroin overdoses at the MSIR identified key risk patterns associated with the increasingly unpredictable drug supply, including the role of the unregulated market

The ACT government's March 2025 cost-benefit analysis modelled the economic case for drug consumption rooms in the ACT context and found benefit-cost ratios of:
- **2.7** for a nurse/peer-led drug consumption room
- **1.9** for a medicalised model

These are positive ratios — every dollar invested returns more than a dollar in savings — though lower than naloxone or NSPs because of the higher operating cost of staffed facilities. The ACT has not yet established an MSIC, and no other state or territory has announced plans to do so.

The 2020 independent panel recommendation for a City of Melbourne CBD facility has not been progressed, despite ongoing community pressure and evidence of drug-related harm in the CBD precinct. The combination of state-level support (the Victorian government has been broadly supportive) and Commonwealth criminal law constraints creates an ongoing legal and political barrier to expansion.

### 5. Drug Checking Services: Patchy, Underfunded, and Generating Critical Intelligence

Drug checking services — which test the composition of drug samples for identity and contaminants — have emerged as a significant harm reduction innovation in the 2020s. They serve two functions: individual risk reduction (a person who discovers their 'ecstasy' contains a dangerous adulterant may choose not to use it) and public health surveillance (services can identify novel substances entering the market before clinical presentations begin).

**CanTEST (Canberra), July 2022–June 2027:**
- Australia's first fixed-site health and drug checking service
- Operated by Directions Health Services and CAHMA (peer organisation), located in Civic
- By December 2025, had tested over **6,000 samples** since opening
- In year three (July 2024–July 2025): tested **2,072 samples**, issued **30+ public health alerts**
- **Global first:** In September 2024, CanTEST detected N-pyrrolidino isotonitazene (NPI) — a potent synthetic opioid — for the **first time globally**, enabling international public health alerts
- Identified an increase in novel benzodiazepines from January 2025, introducing new testing methods in response
- In January 2025: public health alert on dangerous drugs found in counterfeit "Xanax" in Canberra
- Funded to **June 2027** (extension granted in 2024) — but funding status beyond 2027 is not confirmed

**Victorian Pill Testing Service:**
- Mobile service operating at music festivals from **December 2024**
- Fixed site in **Fitzroy, Melbourne** from **August 2025**
- Operating as an **implementation trial until June 2026** — funding status thereafter uncertain
- Legislation passed in 2024 enabling pill testing; rollout has been cautious

**New South Wales:**
- **12-month trial of mobile drug checking** at festivals commenced **March 2025**
- Followed recommendations from the 2024 NSW Drug Summit and a 2019 Coroner's Court report
- Future is subject to trial outcomes

**Queensland:**
- In **September 2025**, Queensland became the **first Australian state to ban pill testing** — moving in the opposite direction to Victoria and NSW

The ACT cost-benefit analysis estimated a benefit-cost ratio for drug checking of **0.3 under normal market conditions** — below breakeven — but **14.0 under changed market conditions** (i.e., when the drug market contains particularly dangerous adulterants). This finding captures the surveillance value of drug checking: it is cheap to run on a day-to-day basis but delivers outsized returns when it detects a dangerous supply shift before people die. The CanTEST detection of a globally novel synthetic opioid is exactly this scenario.

### 6. The Political Economy: Why the Ratio Doesn't Change

The 1.6% allocation has remained remarkably stable despite decades of evidence that it should be higher. The political economy of harm reduction funding in Australia has several structural features that preserve the status quo:

**1. Electoral salience of "law and order."** Drug law enforcement is politically legible — police arrests and drug seizures generate news coverage and signal government action. Needle programs and safe injecting rooms are politically difficult to defend in the same terms, are frequently characterised as "enabling" drug use, and attract organised opposition from some community groups and conservative politicians.

**2. The "not in my backyard" problem.** Safe injecting facilities have faced sustained local opposition in both Kings Cross and North Richmond. The political capital required to site a new facility anywhere is substantial, and no government has committed to a third.

**3. Funding invisibility.** Most harm reduction spending flows through state and territory public health budgets, PHN contracts, and Commonwealth program grants — without a Medicare billing pathway or PBS co-payment, harm reduction services are invisible to the largest health data infrastructure and generate no individual entitlement.

**4. The moral objection.** A persistent strand of Australian politics holds that providing harm reduction services implies approval of drug use. This view is consistently rejected by health authorities but retains electoral traction in certain electorates.

**5. National Drug Strategy inertia.** The 2017–2026 National Drug Strategy nominally commits to a "balanced approach" across the three pillars. But it does not set funding targets or require reporting on proportional allocation. The "balanced approach" has historically meant any spending on harm reduction is sufficient to satisfy the policy commitment, regardless of whether the split is 50-25-25 or 64-27-1.6.

### 7. The ACT Model: A Blueprint the Other Jurisdictions Won't Follow

The Australian Capital Territory has the most comprehensive harm reduction service package in the country:
- Fixed-site drug checking service (CanTEST)
- Free take-home naloxone
- NSP network
- OAT services

The March 2025 ACT government cost-benefit analysis of maintaining the full package from 2026–2030 found:
- **Cost:** $24.6 million over five years
- **Benefits:** 454 opioid overdoses averted, 70 overdose deaths prevented, 215 emergency responses saved, 552 hepatitis C infections averted, 199 injection-related infections prevented
- **Economic benefits:** $250.1 million (based on healthcare cost savings and value of statistical lives)
- **Benefit-cost ratio: 10.1**

This is an extraordinarily strong return — $10.10 in economic benefit for every $1 invested. The ACT's analysis is among the most rigorous cost-benefit assessments of harm reduction published by an Australian government, and it makes an unambiguous case for the current investment, let alone for scaling it up.

Yet no other jurisdiction has produced an equivalent analysis, and the Commonwealth has not commissioned one for the national harm reduction program.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Can harm reduction be brought into the MBS?** The NSP model is predominantly state-funded and operates outside the Medicare billing architecture. Naloxone programs sit partly within the PBS (the medicine is subsidised via a PBS listing) and partly within Commonwealth program grants. None of these services generate MBS billings, which means they are invisible to the fee-for-service health data system, do not count toward Medicare Safety Nets, and are not funded by the universal health insurance model. A GP spending time prescribing naloxone and educating a patient on overdose response can claim a standard consultation item — but there is no MBS item specifically funded for this. This invisibility makes harm reduction services harder to defend at budget time: unlike GP consultations or hospital admissions, their cost and activity are not routinely visible in national health expenditure data.

**2. The National Drug Strategy successor.** The 2017–2026 Strategy expires at the end of this year. The Department of Health and Aged Care has not announced a consultation process for the successor framework, and no draft has been published. Harm reduction advocates are pressing for the successor to include **explicit funding commitments** for harm reduction — not merely rhetorical balance between the three pillars. Whether that argument will be heard in a successor framework that must also navigate Commonwealth-state relations, law enforcement budgets, and conservative political constituencies is deeply uncertain.

**3. Is the 2022 naloxone reform actually reaching the right people?** Free naloxone without prescription from pharmacies is the right policy — but pharmacy uptake requires the person at risk to walk into a pharmacy and ask. Research consistently shows that people at highest overdose risk have the lowest rates of healthcare engagement. The Penington Institute has called for proactive, peer-delivered naloxone distribution programs, including through NSPs, community organisations, and first responders, rather than relying solely on the pharmacy distribution model.

**4. Drug checking and the patchwork problem.** CanTEST is funded to 2027. Victoria's trial runs to June 2026. NSW's trial runs for 12 months. Queensland has banned it. There is no national, ongoing commitment to drug checking as a standing harm reduction service. This creates a paradox: a detection that is globally significant (as CanTEST's discovery of NPI demonstrated) depends on a service whose existence is perpetually contingent on short-term government funding decisions.

**5. First Nations harm reduction.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people represent 23% of NSP attendees — roughly seven times their population share. The evidence base for culturally safe harm reduction services delivered through community-controlled organisations is strong, but funding for such services is fragmented and often short-term. The compounding of high disease burden with inadequate service access makes the funding gap particularly consequential in First Nations communities.
