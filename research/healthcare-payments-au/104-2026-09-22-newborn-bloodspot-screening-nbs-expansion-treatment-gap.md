# Newborn Bloodspot Screening in Australia: The $107.3 Million Expansion, the National Consistency Milestone, and the Treatment-Detection Gap

**Report 104 — 2026-09-22**

**Summary:** Australia's newborn bloodspot screening (NBS) program — 60 years old and one of the most comprehensive in the world — is undergoing its most significant expansion in decades, backed by $107.3 million in federal investment. For the first time, all jurisdictions now share an agreed national list of conditions. But the program faces a structural tension that is sharpening as conditions become more detectable: screening can identify a child as affected before any healthcare professional has spoken to their parents, yet the treatment that makes early detection valuable may be unavailable, unaffordable, or still experimental. The cases of Pompe disease (rejected), MPS I (supported), and MPS II/Hunter syndrome (supported pending ministers) illustrate the competing ethical and economic logics at play. On the frontier, genomic NBS can now detect variants in 605 genes — expanding the candidate pool from ~25 to ~500+ conditions — but the pathway from a positive genomic screen to funded treatment is largely uncharted.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 103 documented the downstream gap in Australia's IVF/PGT-M pathway — couples who discover carrier risk through expanded screening face a user-pays cost barrier to accessing PGT-M rebates. This report examines the postnatal complement: what happens after a baby is born and a NBS heel-prick identifies a serious genetic condition? The same tension applies — detection infrastructure has outpaced treatment access infrastructure.

---

## Background: Sixty Years of Heel-Pricks

Australia's NBS program began in 1966 with phenylketonuria (PKU), a condition where early dietary intervention prevents severe intellectual disability. The model was simple and powerful: collect a few drops of dried blood from a newborn's heel within 48–72 hours of birth, send to a central laboratory, and identify conditions where early intervention changes outcomes dramatically.

Over six decades, the program expanded to include amino acid disorders, fatty acid oxidation disorders, organic acid disorders, congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), hearing screening, and — more recently — conditions at the frontier of treatment such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) and severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

**Delivery structure:** NBS is delivered by state and territory health departments. The federal government sets policy frameworks and funds expansion but does not run the laboratories or clinical follow-up directly. This federated delivery has historically produced inconsistency — different states screened for different conditions at different times, meaning a baby born in one state could be screened for 30+ conditions while a baby born in another was screened for only 25.

**The consistency milestone:** In April 2024, Australia's health ministers agreed on a national list of conditions for the first time in 60 years of NBS — ending the period where a baby's postcode determined what conditions they were screened for. The federal government committed $107.3 million from 2022–23 to 2027–28 to support this expansion and national consistency drive.

## Key Findings

### 1. The $107.3 Million Investment: What It Covers

The federal investment is structured in two tranches:

- **$39.0 million (Budget 2022–23):** Initial commitment including $25.3 million for states and territories to support expanding and standardising their NBS programs
- **$68.3 million (Budget 2024–25 and MYEFO 2024–25):** Second tranche including $43.3 million for states and territories

The investment funds:
- Laboratory infrastructure upgrades (tandem mass spectrometry, flow cytometry, molecular testing platforms)
- Workforce and clinical follow-up capacity
- The National Decision-Making Pathway for evaluating new conditions
- Transition to a nationally agreed condition list

**Current panel:** As of 2025, Australia's NBS programs collectively screen for approximately 34 conditions. The national list agreed in 2024 ended the previous inconsistency but is itself a floor, not a ceiling — the first public call for new candidate conditions ran from October 2025 to January 2026, with successful applications now progressing through the national pathway.

### 2. The SMA Case: Where NBS and Treatment Converge Perfectly

Spinal muscular atrophy is the paradigm case for why NBS matters. SMA is caused by deletions or mutations in the *SMN1* gene; without SMN protein, motor neurons die progressively. Before disease-modifying therapies existed, SMA type 1 was the leading genetic cause of infant death. The treatment window is narrow — early intervention, before motor neuron loss, produces dramatically better outcomes than treatment after symptom onset.

**The pilot:** From August 2018 to January 2021, NSW and ACT ran a NBS pilot for SMA covering 252,081 newborns. Results:
- 21 infants diagnostically confirmed with SMA
- Sensitivity: 100%; specificity: >99.9%; false-positive rate: <0.001%
- Six of the 21 screen-positive infants already showed clinical signs within the first 4 weeks of life — demonstrating how quickly the disease progresses even in a screened cohort

**The treatment pipeline that made NBS viable:**
- **Nusinersen (Spinraza):** PBS-listed from December 2020 for SMA; administered by intrathecal injection every 4 months for life
- **Onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma):** Listed on the PBS from May 2022 — the first gene therapy in Australia for babies under 9 months with SMA; a single intravenous infusion
- **Risdiplam (Evrysdi):** Oral small molecule, PBS-listed; suitable for older infants and children

**Cost-effectiveness of NBS for SMA (2025 analysis):** A 2025 study modelled the cost-effectiveness of the national SMA NBS program across approximately 295,906 annual live births in Australia. Key findings:
- Lifetime gain of **267 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)** at the population level
- **Incremental cost savings of AUD$3,983,263** — i.e., NBS for SMA is not merely cost-effective but cost-saving
- Every dollar invested in NBS for SMA saves hospitals **$3.69** in downstream costs
- Combining NBS with Zolgensma (gene therapy) was associated with the lowest overall costs and best outcomes — reflecting both the dominance of the one-off gene therapy over lifetime intrathecal injections, and the critical importance of presymptomatic treatment timing

SMA NBS is now operational across Australian jurisdictions. A 2025 guideline (UNSW/HGSA) formalised the national clinical pathway for managing screen-positive infants.

**The Mackenzie's Mission connection:** SMA is one of the three conditions now covered by Medicare's reproductive carrier screening (items 73451/73452 — Report 102). Carrier couples identified through that screen can use PGT-M to avoid affected pregnancies (Report 103). NBS is the backstop for families who either didn't screen preconceptionally, or where de novo mutations arise. The two systems are designed to be complementary, but their funding frameworks evolved independently and still lack formal coordination.

### 3. The 2025 MSAC Decisions: Three Applications, Three Different Outcomes

Three applications for NBS expansion were considered by MSAC in late 2024 and April 2025, illustrating the range of outcomes now possible in the expanded NBS decision-making pathway.

#### MPS I (MSAC Application 1775) — SUPPORTED

Mucopolysaccharidosis type I is a lysosomal storage disorder affecting the IDUA gene; it causes progressive multi-system damage including neurological, skeletal, cardiac, and respiratory involvement. The most severe form (Hurler syndrome) is typically fatal in early childhood without treatment; milder forms (Hurler-Scheie, Scheie) have longer natural histories.

- **Treatment available:** Enzyme replacement therapy (laronidase/Aldurazyme) and haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) — with HSCT being the definitive treatment for severe forms when undertaken early (ideally before 9 months)
- **MSAC outcome (April 2025):** Supported addition of MPS I to NBS programs, conditional on requirements being met before screening begins (relating to follow-up protocols and treatment access)
- **Rationale:** Early detection enables pre-symptomatic HSCT, significantly improving neurological and cognitive outcomes; benefits judged to outweigh harms

#### MPS II / Hunter Syndrome (MSAC Application 1776) — SUPPORTED (PENDING MINISTERS)

Hunter syndrome is X-linked recessive (affecting almost exclusively males) and caused by IDS gene mutations, leading to multi-system lysosomal storage disease. Clinical severity varies widely from attenuated to severe neurological involvement.

- **Treatment available:** ERT (idursulfase/Elaprase), potentially gene therapy in development; HSCT controversial for MPS II
- **MSAC outcome (April 2025):** Supported addition to NBS — the potential benefits outweigh harms. The case was complicated by variable expressivity (some males will have attenuated disease) and by the X-linked inheritance pattern triggering cascade testing of mothers and male siblings when a positive screen is detected
- **Next step:** MSAC's advice progresses to health ministers for the final implementation decision
- **Ethical note:** MSAC noted that screening MPS II would generate anxiety in families of affected newborns — particularly given uncertainty about prognosis for attenuated forms — and called for robust genetic counselling pathways before implementation

#### Pompe Disease / GSD II (MSAC Application 1774) — NOT SUPPORTED

Glycogen storage disease type II (Pompe disease) — caused by GAA gene mutations — causes progressive glycogen accumulation in muscle; infantile-onset is rapidly fatal, late-onset is progressive but highly variable.

- **Treatment available:** ERT (alglucosidase alfa / Myozyme; avalglucosidase alfa / Nexviazyme) is on the PBS but requires ongoing infusions
- **MSAC outcome (April 2025):** Did NOT recommend addition to NBS programs — potential harms outweigh benefits
- **Reasons for rejection:**
  - Approximately 58 screen-positive recalls per year, yielding ~1 infantile-onset and ~10 late-onset diagnoses (including variants of uncertain significance)
  - **Pseudodeficiency alleles** (enzyme activity reductions that do not cause disease) are common, especially in Asian populations — creating a substantial false-positive burden
  - Late-onset GSD II has **highly variable phenotypic expression** — a positive screen cannot reliably predict who will develop symptoms, when, or how severely
  - Harm from overdiagnosis: families of children with late-onset variants face anxiety, surveillance costs, and treatment decisions for conditions that may not manifest clinically for decades, if ever
- **Path forward:** MSAC advised reconsideration "when a test is available that accurately predicts infantile-onset GSD II" — effectively waiting for a genotype-based or biomarker refinement that can distinguish infantile from late-onset at birth

The Pompe case is instructive because it surfaces the core NBS tension explicitly: screening finds many more cases than those who will benefit from early intervention, and the harms of that overdetection — particularly in a condition with variable expressivity — may exceed the benefits to the genuinely affected minority.

### 4. The Treatment-Detection Gap: When Screening Finds More Than Treatment Can Treat

The Pompe rejection points to a structural issue that will intensify as NBS panels expand. The gap has two dimensions:

**Dimension 1: No treatment exists or is not funded.** If NBS detects a condition for which no effective treatment is listed on the PBS or available through public hospitals, parents learn their newborn has a serious condition — and then face either accepting untreatable diagnosis, accessing experimental trials, or paying privately for expensive therapies. This is analogous to the genetic counselling scenario (Report 101) — the detection capability has run ahead of the treatment access system.

**Dimension 2: Treatment exists but overdetection creates harm.** As with Pompe, even when ERT is available, identifying many patients who will never need it (late-onset, attenuated variants) creates long-term surveillance obligations, psychological distress, and potentially unnecessary treatment. A positive NBS result changes a family's life permanently — the ethical weight of that must be justified by a proportionate clinical benefit.

**The "screen-to-treatment pipeline" problem:** Australia's NBS program was historically designed around conditions where: (a) treatment is highly effective; (b) early treatment is dramatically superior to delayed treatment; and (c) the test has high positive predictive value. As conditions become more varied in their natural histories and treatment landscapes, this neat model breaks down. The national decision-making pathway attempts to manage this — but it lacks a formal mechanism to ensure that PBS listing of a treatment and NBS implementation are coordinated in time.

### 5. The Genomic NBS Frontier: BabyScreen+ and GenSCAN

The conventional NBS model uses targeted biochemical tests (enzyme assays, metabolite concentrations, hormone levels) for specific conditions. Genomic NBS — using whole-genome or whole-exome sequencing of dried bloodspot cards — can detect variants in hundreds of genes simultaneously. This changes the scale of the question from "should we add this one condition?" to "how do we decide which of 500+ detectable conditions should have clinical action taken?"

**BabyScreen+ (Murdoch Children's Research Institute):**
- Prospective cohort study of 1,000 Victorian newborns
- Whole-genome sequencing targeting 605 genes for early-onset, severe, treatable conditions
- **Results:** 16 infants (1.6%) identified with high-chance results; only 1 of these 16 was detected by standard NBS
- Clinical impact ranged from preventive surveillance to active management (including transplantation in one case)
- Average time to genomic result: 13 days (vs. 2–5 days for biochemical NBS) — a clinically important delay for some conditions
- Parental acceptance: very high; >99% thought genomic NBS should be available to all parents; decisional regret was low

**GenSCAN (Genomic Screening Consortium for Australian Newborns):**
- In 2022, the Medical Research Futures Fund (MRFF), Genomics Health Futures Mission (GHFM), and NHMRC awarded AUD$23 million across six projects to explore genomic NBS integration
- GenSCAN brings these projects together to share evidence and develop a collective knowledge base
- The consortium includes BabyScreen+ and five other projects covering different populations, conditions, and implementation questions

**The systemic tension genomic NBS introduces:**
Genomic NBS could detect 500+ conditions — but only a fraction have established, effective treatments available on the PBS or through public systems. For the rest, a positive genomic screen generates a diagnosis without a funded treatment pathway. This is the same gap identified in Report 94 (cancer genomics) and Report 101 (genetic counselling) — but applied to newborns, where parents have no time to prepare, and the ethical stakes are particularly high.

Healthcare professionals are divided: a 2025 paper in the *European Journal of Human Genetics* surveying Australian clinicians, scientists, and policymakers found significant concern about "jumping too far ahead" — implementing genomic NBS before treatment pipelines, counselling workforce, and ethical frameworks are ready.

### 6. Equity: Indigenous Australians and the NBS Participation Gap

Despite the program's 99% national participation rate, a 2026 paper in the *Medical Journal of Australia* (Garvey et al.) identified a critical gap: there is **no systematic evidence** on the participation rates, experiences, or outcomes of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families in NBS.

This gap matters because:
- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander newborns are disproportionately born in regional, remote, and very remote areas where follow-up pathways after a positive screen are longer and more complex
- The genetic variants of clinical significance in NBS panels have largely been characterised in European ancestry populations; the clinical significance of variants more common in First Nations populations may be uncertain
- As genomic NBS is considered, the community engagement question becomes critical — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities have extensive experience with the consequences of research and data systems that did not centre their perspectives or governance structures

The authors argue there is "a critical window of opportunity" to engage Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in designing any genomic NBS expansion — before the decisions are made, not after.

On rural access more broadly: the follow-up pathway after a positive NBS result typically involves referral to a metropolitan specialist centre (metabolic physician, clinical geneticist, haematologist). For families in regional and remote areas, this involves the same travel costs and geographic barriers documented in Reports 12 and 45 for other specialist services. There is no Modified Monash-equivalent incentive structure for the NBS follow-up pathway.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The screen-treatment coordination problem.** NBS expansion and PBS listing of treatments are managed by different committees (Health Ministers / national NBS pathway vs. PBAC). There is no formal mechanism to ensure that when a condition is added to NBS, a funded treatment pathway is simultaneously confirmed. Families can find themselves holding a positive screen for a condition with no accessible treatment.

**2. Variable expressivity and the Pompe problem.** As more conditions with heterogeneous natural histories are considered for NBS, the tension between sensitivity (finding all affected infants) and positive predictive value (ensuring detected infants will actually benefit from early intervention) will intensify. Genomic NBS amplifies this: many variants of uncertain significance will be detected, generating anxiety without clinical benefit.

**3. Genomic NBS: expand the panel or not?** BabyScreen+ demonstrates that genomic NBS finds real cases that biochemical NBS misses — and does so at a 1.6% hit rate. But scaling this to all 295,000+ annual Australian newborns requires: genomic sequencing infrastructure, a greatly expanded genetic counselling workforce (already under pressure — Report 101), a clear decision framework for which genes to act on, and a PBS/public hospital treatment pathway for detected conditions. None of these are currently in place at scale.

**4. Indigenous genomics governance.** Any expansion of NBS — especially genomic NBS — that does not explicitly engage Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities risks repeating the history of biomedical research imposing frameworks without consent. The 2026 MJA paper's finding that participation data does not even exist is itself a governance failure.

**5. The "screen now, treat later" gamble.** For conditions like MPS II and MPS I where early enzyme replacement and HSCT are beneficial, adding the condition to NBS before treatment access is fully formalised across all jurisdictions creates a risk that families in some states or territories learn of diagnosis but cannot access time-sensitive treatment within the required clinical window.

**6. The cost of counselling at scale.** Informed consent for NBS — and particularly for genomic NBS that detects hundreds of variants — requires pre-test information provision and post-test genetic counselling that the current workforce cannot supply. The BabyScreen+ study noted this challenge explicitly, as did Australia's Mackenzie's Mission (Report 102) and its report on genetic counselling capacity (Report 101).

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Lysosomal storage diseases and the high-cost treatment pipeline** — MPS I, MPS II, Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, and Pompe disease are all lysosomal storage diseases; all have enzyme replacement therapies (ERTs) that are among the most expensive medicines in the world. How does Australia's PBS handle these conditions? MSAC has separately assessed ERT listings; what is the relationship between NBS and PBS listing decisions for these conditions?

2. **Inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) — the core of the NBS panel** — Metabolic conditions such as PKU, MSUD, homocystinuria, and organic acidaemias are the historical backbone of NBS. Low-phenylalanine formula, special diets, and now some PBS-listed treatments (sapropterin for PKU) have transformed outcomes. What is the payment model for the metabolic dietary management that keeps these children healthy?

3. **The genetic counselling workforce crisis** — Reports 101–103 have all surfaced the inadequacy of Australia's genetic counselling workforce. This report adds NBS as a further demand driver. What is the actual workforce gap, what is the training pipeline, and what would an MBS-funded genetic counselling item for NBS follow-up look like?

4. **Genomic NBS governance: international comparisons** — The UK (Genomics England's Generation Study), the US (NIH Newborn Sequencing in Genomic Medicine program), and Japan have all moved further into genomic NBS. What governance models exist, and which is most applicable to Australia's federated, MSAC-driven system?

5. **Hearing screening as a NBS analogue** — Universal newborn hearing screening (UNHS) is a related program that identifies permanent childhood hearing loss, with cochlear implantation and hearing aids as downstream interventions. It operates under a separate funding framework. The equity and follow-up challenges are similar — and some rare genetic causes of deafness are now identifiable through genomic NBS.
