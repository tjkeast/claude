# Supervised Injecting Facilities: Australia's Two-Site Harm Reduction Model and the Expansion Stalemate

**Report 062 — 2026-08-07**

**Summary:** Australia has two supervised injecting facilities (SIFs) — Sydney's Uniting MSIC (operating since 2001) and Melbourne's North Richmond MSIR (2018) — that have collectively managed more than 22,000 overdoses with zero on-site fatalities. Despite one of the strongest evidence bases in harm reduction, expansion has been blocked in both states, and the ACT — despite already having enabling legislation — still has no facility. Both operating sites are funded entirely through state block grants with no Commonwealth contribution and no MBS mechanism: a payment architecture that keeps SIFs invisible to federal health systems and structurally dependent on state political will.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 061 documented that Australia's Take-Home Naloxone Program operates at $4.9 million per year against a toll of 2,272+ overdose deaths annually, that naloxone distribution gaps persist in rural and regional areas, and that the drug supply's growing complexity — nitazenes, xylazine — is outpacing the public health response. Supervised injecting facilities represent the most intensive point of the overdose response architecture: on-site medical supervision where naloxone and clinical care are immediately available. This report examines how they are funded, what the evidence shows, and why the model remains at two sites after 25 years.

---

## Background

### What Supervised Injecting Facilities Are and How They Work

Supervised injecting facilities (SIFs) — also called drug consumption rooms, medically supervised injecting centres, or safe injecting rooms — provide a legally sanctioned space where people can consume pre-obtained drugs under medical supervision. They do not supply drugs. Staff provide clean injecting equipment, overdose monitoring and reversal, basic wound care, and referrals to health and social services.

The first SIF opened in Berne, Switzerland in 1986. By 2023, more than 130 facilities operated across Europe, Canada, and Australia. In their 40-year history, there has been **no recorded overdose death inside any SIF anywhere in the world** — a safety record unmatched by any other clinical intervention for opioid overdose prevention.

SIFs are positioned differently from naloxone distribution (which intervenes after the overdose begins) and opioid agonist treatment (OAT, which reduces injecting frequency). A SIF intervenes at the moment of drug use — typically before or at the onset of respiratory depression — allowing skilled observation and immediate reversal. For the small proportion of clients who become unconscious, clinical staff are present from the moment of exposure.

### Australia's Two Facilities

**The Uniting Medically Supervised Injecting Centre (MSIC), Kings Cross, NSW** is Australia's first and oldest SIF. After a prolonged political battle and two years of trial operation, it was formally established in May 1999 in Kings Cross — then Australia's most concentrated heroin market and a suburb recording multiple overdose deaths per week. It operates under a specific exemption in the NSW Drug Misuse and Trafficking Act and is run by Uniting, a faith-based social service organisation, under a contract with NSW Health.

**The North Richmond Community Health Medically Supervised Injecting Room (MSIR), Victoria** opened as a trial on 6 July 2018 in North Richmond, an inner-Melbourne suburb with high rates of public injecting and associated overdose deaths. After a positive independent evaluation, the Victorian Government legislated the facility as ongoing in 2023 through the Drugs, Poisons and Controlled Substances Amendment (Medically Supervised Injecting Centre) Act. It operates through a partnership between North Richmond Community Health and St Vincent's Hospital Melbourne.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Evidence Base: 22,000+ Overdoses, Zero Deaths

The combined evidence from 25 years of operation is unambiguous on the core question.

**Sydney MSIC** has, since opening in May 2001 (through to its most recently reported figures):
- Supervised more than **1.28 million injections**
- Managed more than **11,205 overdoses without a single on-site fatality**
- Registered approximately **18,265 clients**
- Provided more than **20,420 referrals** to health, treatment and social services
- Reached a population of whom approximately **70% had never previously accessed any local health service**

**Melbourne MSIR** has, since opening in July 2018:
- Recorded more than **600,000 visits**
- Managed more than **11,000 overdoses** with zero on-site deaths
- Been estimated to have **saved up to 63 lives** (approximately 10 lives per year; equivalent to the service preventing one overdose death every 5–6 weeks)
- Provided more than **127,000 onsite health and social support services**

The ambulance data from Kings Cross, NSW is striking: when the Sydney MSIC opened, ambulance call-outs to Kings Cross for overdose fell by approximately **80%**. This is a direct measurement of the facility's core function — that people who would otherwise collapse in public, in stairwells, in toilets, and wait for emergency response are instead supervised in a clinical setting where reversal takes minutes rather than the critical 4–8 minutes that determines irreversible brain injury.

A 2022 MJA retrospective of the 21-year Sydney experience (*Twenty-one years at the Uniting Medically Supervised Injecting Centre, Sydney Australia: addressing the remaining questions*) confirmed that between 2010 and 2020 alone, only 1% of the more than 7,000 overdoses managed by the MSIC required ambulance transfer — approximately 76 over a decade — demonstrating that the vast majority of overdoses are manageable on-site without hospital admission.

### 2. Cost-Effectiveness: A Positive Return, Even in Small Jurisdictions

The economic case for supervised injecting facilities has strengthened steadily over 25 years.

**Vancouver's Insite** — the most comprehensively evaluated SIF in the world — generated a widely cited return: approximately **US$2.33 saved per US$1 spent**, driven primarily by HIV and hepatitis C infection prevention, reduced hospitalisation for injection-related infections, and averted overdose deaths. A 2010 analysis in *CMAJ* estimated that Insite prevented 35 HIV infections and 3.5 deaths per year at an annual cost of approximately CAD$1.5 million — a highly favourable cost-effectiveness ratio.

**In the Australian context**, the most recent and directly relevant economic modelling was published in January 2026 by Bowring et al. in *Addiction* (121(5):1272–1289), commissioned by ACT Health in partnership with the Australian National University and Burnet Institute. This analysis modelled the cost-effectiveness of current and potential harm reduction interventions in the ACT — a jurisdiction of approximately 470,000 people.

Key findings from the Bowring et al. (2026) analysis:
- The existing ACT harm reduction package (needle and syringe programs, take-home naloxone, opioid agonist treatment, and drug checking) was estimated to cost **$24.6 million over 2026–2030** and avert **454 opioid overdoses**, **20 non-opioid overdoses**, and **70 overdose-related deaths**
- **Supervised injecting spaces** were assessed as a *new* intervention to add to the existing ACT package, and were found to be **cost-effective even in a smaller jurisdiction like the ACT**
- This represents the first formal Australian economic modelling specifically of adding a SIF to an existing jurisdiction-level harm reduction system

The ACT cost-benefit analysis is also notable because the ACT comparison represents the most unfavourable scale conditions for a SIF — small population, relatively low injecting drug use prevalence — and still finds a positive economic case.

**The PBO's national costing** provides a different data point. The Australian Greens' 2025 federal election commitment to establish **11 new supervised injecting facilities** across Australia (modelled on the North Richmond MSIR), to commence from 1 July 2026, was costed by the Parliamentary Budget Office at **$261.2 million over the forward estimates period** (approximately 4 years). This implies:
- Average cost per facility per year: approximately **$5.9 million**
- Total 11-facility system: approximately **$23.7 million per year**

For context, $23.7 million per year for 11 facilities — covering every major Australian city — is roughly equal to **five days of the Commonwealth's Take-Home Naloxone Program annual budget** scaled up to cover a national SIF system.

### 3. The Payment Architecture: State-Only Block Grants, No MBS

This is the most distinctive feature of Australia's SIF model from a healthcare payments perspective: **both facilities are funded entirely through state health block grants, with no Commonwealth contribution of any kind**.

There is no MBS item for supervised consumption. There is no PHN commissioning. There is no Drug and Alcohol Services Australia equivalent funding line that includes SIFs. Commonwealth harm reduction funding (the $19.6 million THN Program, the NSP funding under the Blood Borne Virus and Sexually Transmitted Infections program) explicitly excludes SIFs, which are treated as entirely a state-level public health function.

This creates several structural effects:

**Invisibility in Commonwealth health data.** Neither the MSIC nor the MSIR generates any Medicare claims. Their activity cannot be tracked in AIHW national health system statistics in the same way that GP attendances or PBS prescriptions are tracked. The services are essentially invisible to federal health data systems, making it impossible to construct a national evidence base from routine administrative data.

**Funding instability.** Because SIFs depend entirely on state block grants negotiated in annual or multi-year budget processes, their continuation is directly subject to state electoral cycles and ministerial priorities. The MSIR in Victoria was running as a "trial" for five years before legislation made it permanent — five years in which its continuation was one state election away from termination.

**Legal dependency on state legislation.** Each SIF requires specific state legislation to create an exemption from criminal liability for clients and staff under state drug laws. Unlike other harm reduction services (NSPs, OAT), which can operate under general pharmaceutical/medical exemptions, a SIF cannot legally operate without targeted primary legislation. This is not a funding decision — it is a law-change decision, requiring parliamentary majorities. The ACT is the only jurisdiction with enabling legislation already passed that does not yet have an operating SIF.

**No path to Commonwealth MBS integration.** Unlike GP mental health plans (MBS-funded), pharmacist consultations (moving toward MBS funding via the scope-of-practice reforms discussed in Report 040), or nurse practitioner services (MBS-funded), SIFs cannot plausibly be brought into the MBS framework without federal decriminalisation of drug use — a step no Australian government has contemplated. The structural exclusion from MBS is permanent under current policy settings.

The contrast with opioid agonist treatment is illustrative: OAT (methadone and buprenorphine) is funded through a combination of PBS dispensing fees and state clinical governance programs. GPs and pharmacists receive MBS and PBS remuneration for OAT services. A SIF, which provides a more intensive and immediate harm reduction service for the same population, receives no equivalent federal support.

### 4. The Shifting Drug Profile: From Heroin Era to Stimulant Era

Both facilities were designed in the late 1990s — and specifically sited in Kings Cross and North Richmond — in direct response to the heroin overdose epidemic of that decade. The drug use patterns now presenting at both facilities have shifted dramatically.

A 2026 paper by Bartlett et al. in *Drug and Alcohol Review* examining changing drug consumption patterns at the Sydney MSIC documented that **amphetamine now accounts for 50.1% of injections at the MSIC** — compared to only 2–7% between 2002 and 2011. Heroin, which once dominated, now accounts for a declining proportion.

This has significant clinical implications:

- **Methamphetamine overdose is clinically different from opioid overdose.** It is not reversed by naloxone. The primary acute risks are cardiac arrhythmia, hyperthermia, psychosis, and seizure — not respiratory depression. The clinical skill set and equipment needed differs substantially.
- **The Kings Cross location may be less well-matched to the current drug-using population**, which has dispersed from the inner-city heroin markets of the 1990s.
- **The risk of stimulant-related acute harms at SIFs remains manageable**, but requires adaptation of protocols and staffing.

The Melbourne MSIR review suggested a similar although less extreme shift. As documented in Reports 058 and 061, the overall drug supply is increasingly polysubstance — combinations of stimulants, opioids, synthetic opioids (nitazenes), and adulterants (xylazine) that produce complex clinical presentations. SIFs, which were designed for the predictable pharmacology of heroin overdose, are now managing a substantially more complex picture.

### 5. The Expansion Failure: Three Blocked Proposals

Despite the evidence base and the growing severity of the overdose crisis (2,272 deaths in 2023, an estimated 2,596 in 2024), expansion has been systematically blocked:

**New South Wales:** The 2024 NSW Drug Summit — convened by the Minns Government, co-chaired by John Brogden AM and the Hon. Carmel Tebbutt — delivered 56 recommendations including a recommendation for additional supervised injecting sites beyond Kings Cross. The NSW Government's October 2025 response listed the recommendation as **"Noted"** — the Australian bureaucratic shorthand for "we have read this and will not act on it." The Government committed more than $50 million per year across multiple Drug Summit recommendations but explicitly declined to expand supervised injecting services.

The Greens' NSW MP Cate Faehrmann responded that "the Minns Government's refusal to support new sites is a slap in the face to the health experts and frontline workers who have made the Kings Cross Medically Supervised Injecting Centre a world-leading example of best practice in harm reduction."

**Victoria:** In April 2024, the Allan Labor Government rejected a second Melbourne MSIR despite receiving expert advice — held for nearly a year — that recommended establishing one. The proposed site was cohealth Central Melbourne on Victoria Street. Premier Allan cited difficulty "finding a location that strikes that balance between supporting people who use drugs with the needs of the broader community." The Victorian Greens called it a "spineless captain's call that will cost countless lives."

As of December 2025, the political situation around the existing North Richmond MSIR worsened when Yarra City Council — which had long supported the facility — reversed its support without warning. The Greens MP Sophie Wade published an analysis warning that the Victorian Government's "drug harm reduction strategy is not enough" and that the existing MSIR was facing renewed community opposition. No second Victorian facility has been pursued since the April 2024 rejection.

**ACT:** The ACT has the most supportive legislative environment of any Australian jurisdiction. The ACT Parliament has already passed enabling legislation for a supervised injecting facility. The ACT Government decriminalised personal drug possession in October 2023. CanTEST — the nation's only fixed-site drug checking service — has operated in Canberra since July 2022.

An ACT Health Directorate review of supervised injecting feasibility was commissioned in July 2024, with findings to be delivered to the newly elected ACT Legislative Assembly in December 2024. As of mid-2026, no facility has been announced. The ACT remains the most obvious candidate for Australia's third SIF — it has the legislation, the public health infrastructure, and a newly published positive economic analysis — but no government decision to proceed.

### 6. Community Opposition and the Location Problem

The principal obstacle to expansion is not evidence, not funding, and not federal legal barriers. It is the **NIMBY (Not In My Backyard) problem** — the intense, sustained opposition of residents and businesses in any neighbourhood proposed as a SIF location.

North Richmond has experienced years of community friction around the MSIR, including disputes about public injecting in surrounding streets, discarded needles, and increased foot traffic from people seeking services. The Yarra City Council reversal in December 2025 reflects this pressure reaching elected representatives. The Victorian Government's failed search for a CBD Melbourne site was essentially a failure to find any community willing to host the facility.

This is not a uniquely Australian problem. In Vancouver, Insite faced years of opposition from Downtown Eastside residents and businesses before opening. In New York, multiple proposed SIF locations failed due to community opposition before two facilities opened in 2021.

The structural answer — in jurisdictions that have successfully expanded SIF networks — has typically been:
1. **Hospital or health precinct co-location**, where the facility is embedded in existing clinical infrastructure rather than standalone in a residential area
2. **Government-owned sites** that are not dependent on commercial landlord or local council approval
3. **Legislated site protections** that override local planning objections once a location is designated

Australia has not adopted any of these solutions at scale. The Victorian MSIR, co-located with North Richmond Community Health and within walking distance of St Vincent's Hospital, is the closest to a hospital-adjacent model — and it has faced the most sustained community opposition of any Australian health facility in recent years.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The evidence-to-policy gap.** The evidence base for SIFs is as strong as for almost any harm reduction intervention and stronger than many funded clinical services. The failure to expand despite 25 years of evidence reflects political dynamics — community opposition, electoral calculation, stigma toward people who inject drugs — rather than evidential uncertainty. The question is not whether SIFs work; it is whether governments can manage the political cost of siting them.

**2. The stimulant transition.** With amphetamines now representing 50.1% of injections at the Sydney MSIC, the original opioid-era model requires clinical adaptation. SIFs in Europe are increasingly described as "drug consumption rooms" and have adapted protocols to include stimulant overdose management. Australia has not formally evaluated whether the existing model is optimally configured for its 2026 client base.

**3. The Commonwealth funding vacuum.** Both operating SIFs are entirely exposed to state budget cycles with no Commonwealth contribution. The ACT, which has the most supportive policy environment, has no state-equivalent funding mechanism and would depend on ACT Territory government budget capacity. Without any Commonwealth funding pathway, expansion depends entirely on eight separate state and territory governments each passing enabling legislation and providing ongoing funding — a near-impossible political coordination problem.

**4. The Greens' national proposal.** The Parliamentary Budget Office costed the Greens' 11-facility proposal at $261.2 million over the forward estimates. The 2025 federal election returned Labor to government without the Greens holding the balance of power in sufficient numbers to advance this policy. The proposal has not been adopted by the Albanese Government.

**5. The ACT stalemate.** The ACT has enabling legislation, positive economic modelling, a mature harm reduction ecosystem (CanTEST, NSPs, OAT), and a decriminalised possession framework. The only missing element is a Government decision to proceed. The delay from enabling legislation (already passed) to facility operation has now exceeded several years. Each year of delay in a jurisdiction with 470,000 people and growing synthetic opioid exposure represents preventable deaths.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Methamphetamine-specific harm reduction** — as the drug profile of injecting services has shifted to amphetamine-dominant, what does harm reduction look like for stimulant users? There are no pharmacotherapy equivalents to methadone or buprenorphine for methamphetamine dependence; the treatment and harm reduction toolkits are fundamentally different. The meth-psychosis pipeline, crystal methamphetamine MBS-funded psychology trials, and the funding gap for stimulant-specific AOD services.

2. **The National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — consultation is underway for the replacement of the National Drug Strategy (2017–2026). Whether the new strategy includes binding targets for SIF access, national coordination of harm reduction services, and synthetic opioid preparedness will determine the trajectory of Australian drug policy for the next decade.

3. **First Nations communities and the harm reduction gap** — remote and very remote communities with high rates of polydrug use have effectively zero access to supervised injecting, drug checking, or overdose prevention facilities; the combination of legal barriers (state Poisons legislation), infrastructure gaps, and community governance complexity creates a harm reduction dead zone for the populations at highest risk.

4. **The ACT as a test case for national expansion** — the ACT has enabling legislation, economic modelling, a supportive policy environment, and a newly operational drug checking service. Whether the ACT moves from "feasibility" to "implementation" in the 2026–27 budget cycle will signal the trajectory for national expansion under the current federal government.

5. **Drug consumption rooms beyond injecting** — European harm reduction models increasingly include inhalation rooms (for smoking methamphetamine, heroin, crack cocaine) alongside injection facilities. As Australia's drug supply becomes more polysubstance and as the stimulant proportion grows, the question of whether the "injecting room" model is the right architecture — or whether a broader "drug consumption room" model is needed — is becoming practically relevant.
