# Lynch Syndrome in Australia: The Hereditary Cancer Testing and Funding Gap

**Report 097 — 2026-09-14**

**Summary:** Lynch syndrome, the most common hereditary colorectal cancer predisposition, affects approximately 1 in 279 Australians — around 90,000 people — yet only a small fraction has been diagnosed. The pathway from tumour immunohistochemistry to germline genetic testing to preventive surveillance is fragmented by MBS funding gaps (no rebated MLH1 methylation testing, no MSI testing item), genetics services overwhelmed with wait times over 12 months, and clinician knowledge deficits. A 2022 Australian multicentre study found that nearly half of patients whose tumour testing indicated high Lynch syndrome risk had no record of referral to genetics services. The January 2026 DNA Screen pilot — which found 74.5% of genomic screening participants with pathogenic variants were ineligible for current government-funded testing criteria — crystallises the structural mismatch between the population's hereditary cancer burden and the public funding architecture that is supposed to address it.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 096 documented that dMMR testing for dostarlimab eligibility in endometrial cancer serves a second critical function — identifying Lynch syndrome carriers. But it also revealed that access to dMMR testing is neither adequate nor equitable, with geographic disparities as stark as NT (8.7% testing rate) versus NSW (23.8%). This report zooms out to examine the full Lynch syndrome diagnostic and surveillance ecosystem in Australia, and why that ecosystem consistently fails to convert tumour testing signals into diagnosis, counselling, and prevention.

---

## Background: What Is Lynch Syndrome and Why Does It Matter?

Lynch syndrome (formerly hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, or HNPCC) is caused by germline pathogenic variants in one of five DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes: **MLH1**, **MSH2**, **MSH6**, **PMS2**, and **EPCAM**. When MMR genes fail, DNA replication errors accumulate in tumour cells — producing the mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) and microsatellite-instability-high (MSI-H) tumour phenotype that opens access to PBS-subsidised checkpoint inhibitors, as documented in Reports 095 and 096.

Lynch syndrome accounts for approximately:
- **3–5% of all colorectal cancers** in Australia
- **3% of all endometrial cancers**
- Smaller proportions of ovarian, gastric, urinary tract, biliary tract, and CNS cancers

Lifetime cancer risks for Lynch syndrome carriers vary substantially by gene:

| Gene | Colorectal cancer (lifetime) | Endometrial cancer (lifetime) |
|---|---|---|
| MLH1 | 40–80% | 40–60% |
| MSH2 | 35–75% | 40–60% |
| MSH6 | 10–22% | 17–71% |
| PMS2 | 11–20% | 12–15% |

Without diagnosis and surveillance, a Lynch syndrome carrier faces decades of unmonitored risk from multiple cancer types. With diagnosis, annual surveillance colonoscopy meaningfully reduces colorectal cancer mortality, aspirin chemoprevention cuts incident colorectal cancer risk, and enhanced endometrial surveillance enables earlier detection.

### Prevalence: A Mass Under-Diagnosis Problem

The population prevalence of Lynch syndrome — combining carriers of pathogenic variants in all four main MMR genes — is estimated at approximately **1 in 279** (with gene-specific rates: 1 in 1,946 for MLH1; 1 in 2,841 for MSH2; 1 in 758 for MSH6; 1 in 714 for PMS2). Applied to Australia's population of approximately 27 million, this implies roughly **90,000 Australians** carry a Lynch syndrome gene fault.

The number actually diagnosed is a fraction of this. Cancer Australia describes Lynch syndrome as "one of the most common and underdiagnosed inherited cancer conditions in Australia." While no authoritative national registry count of diagnosed cases is publicly available, the structural evidence — tumour testing gaps, referral gaps, genetic counselling wait times — indicates the diagnosed population is well below 10% of those estimated to carry a pathogenic variant.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Testing Pathway Has Three Steps — Each Can Fail

The recommended diagnostic pathway for Lynch syndrome involves sequential steps, and each step has a distinct MBS funding status and a documented failure point.

**Step 1: Tumour Immunohistochemistry (IHC) — Partially Funded**

IHC testing for MMR protein loss is the recommended first step at cancer diagnosis (colorectal or endometrial). All four MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6) should be tested — loss of any one indicates dMMR and triggers further investigation.

MBS status: Claimable under item 72847 (general IHC with 4–6 antibodies) — the same non-specific item identified in Report 096 as inadequate for MSAC Application 1674. Importantly, the item does not specify the antibodies used or the clinical purpose, meaning there is no administrative signal that Lynch syndrome screening was the intent. This is precisely the funding gap that MSAC Application 1674 is seeking to resolve for endometrial cancer.

For colorectal cancer, a dedicated MBS item structure is also absent. The HGSA/COSA joint position statement recommends universal dMMR testing of all incident colorectal and endometrial cancers, but this recommendation lacks a dedicated, purpose-built MBS item to operationalise it.

**Step 2: Secondary Tumour Testing — Not Funded**

When MLH1 protein loss is detected, somatic MLH1 promoter hypermethylation (a sporadic event, not inherited) must be excluded before proceeding to germline testing. This requires:
- **MLH1 promoter methylation testing**, and/or
- **Microsatellite instability (MSI) testing** by PCR

**Neither of these secondary tests has an MBS item.** This gap — explicitly acknowledged in the HGSA/COSA position statement — means that the critical "step between tumour signal and genetic referral" is funded inconsistently: by some public pathology laboratories as part of standard practice, or by the patient out-of-pocket if testing is done privately. The practical effect is that the reflex testing cascade — which should automatically progress from tumour IHC → methylation testing → germline referral — breaks down at Step 2 in many settings.

**Step 3: Germline Genetic Testing — Funded Under Specific Criteria**

Germline testing for Lynch syndrome variants (via gene panel testing of MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM) is **MBS-rebated for eligible individuals** through Medicare Group P7 (Genetics) items. Family members at risk are eligible for rebated **predictive (cascade) testing** once a family variant is confirmed.

However, the eligibility criteria for MBS-rebated germline testing have historically been narrow, and:
- Pre- and post-test genetic counselling is not universally MBS-funded (varies by service model)
- In many jurisdictions, germline testing must be arranged through a Family Cancer Clinic or clinical genetics service — creating a bottleneck at the genetics services gateway
- The 2024 statewide mainstream model evaluation in South Australia (Jennings et al., 2025, *Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology*) found that while BRCA1/2 mainstream testing increased significantly after pathway reform, **MMR gene testing via mainstream pathways showed no change** — suggesting that Lynch syndrome specifically remains trapped in the genetics referral bottleneck

### 2. The Referral Gap: Half of High-Risk Patients Not Referred

A 2022 multicentre retrospective study of Lynch syndrome testing in **seven Australian hospitals** across three states (*Hereditary Cancer in Clinical Practice*) found:

- Of 1,624 colorectal cancer patients reviewed, 0–19% per hospital had missing MMR deficiency test results
- Of **74 patients** whose tumour testing was completed appropriately and indicated high risk of Lynch syndrome, **49% (36 patients) had no record of referral to genetic services** for diagnostic germline testing
- Referral missingness was higher for older patients — a known bias where oncologists may perceive Lynch syndrome diagnosis as less actionable in patients over 70
- Hospital practices varied substantially, from systematic reflex testing protocols to ad hoc referral depending on clinical team awareness

This is a system operating at roughly half capacity at the referral step alone. Combined with Step 2 funding gaps, the cumulative attrition from tumour testing signal to actual Lynch syndrome diagnosis is substantial.

### 3. Genetics Services Capacity: Already Overwhelmed Before Population Demand Increases

A 2024 workforce survey of genetic counsellors in Australia and New Zealand (published in *Genetics in Health*) found:

- In 2022, approximately **432 genetic counsellors** were working clinically in all of Australasia (including New Zealand)
- **Two-thirds** of respondents perceived a **critical shortage** of genetic counsellors to meet current or future demand
- Almost all respondents noted demand had increased significantly over the preceding five years and would continue to increase
- **Wait times of over 12 months** for appointments at many public genetic services

Against this backdrop:
- The Lynch syndrome cascade creates a multiplier: one confirmed Lynch syndrome case triggers genetic counselling and testing for multiple first-degree relatives (each of whom has a 50% chance of carrying the family variant)
- The expanding use of tumour dMMR testing as a companion diagnostic for checkpoint inhibitors (dostarlimab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab+ipilimumab) is generating new Lynch syndrome referrals from oncology pathways
- Population genomic screening for Lynch syndrome, if implemented, would create demand that would multiply current clinical genetics capacity requirements many times over

### 4. The DNA Screen Pilot: 74.5% Ineligible Under Current Criteria

The **DNA Screen nationwide pilot**, led by Professor Paul Lacaze at Monash University and published in *Nature Health* in January 2026, represents the most significant recent evidence on the gap between Lynch syndrome's population burden and Australia's current funded testing architecture.

The pilot:
- Offered genomic screening to **30,017 registrants** aged 18–40 years, of whom 18,573 were invited and **10,263 completed screening**
- Tested for ten genes linked to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (BRCA1/2), Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2), and familial hypercholesterolaemia
- Detected pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in **202 participants (2.0%)**
- Of the 189 referred for clinical follow-up, 97.9% accepted and 87.3% attended appointments

The critical finding for this report: **74.5% of those referred for clinical follow-up were ineligible for government-funded criteria-based genomic testing** under current MBS criteria. These individuals found their pathogenic variants through a research pilot — and would not have been diagnosed under the existing system.

The researchers from Monash University, publishing in January 2026, called for urgent government funding to progress a **national preventive genomic testing program**. A companion **eClinicalMedicine** modelling study (Lancet, 2023) found combined population genomic screening for HBOC + Lynch syndrome + familial hypercholesterolaemia at AU$200 per sample to be cost-effective and health-saving across a simulated 18–40 year-old cohort of 8.3 million Australians.

### 5. Chemoprevention: CAPP2 and CAPP3 — Evidence Available, Prescribing Lagging

Lynch syndrome presents a rare opportunity in cancer medicine: a chemoprevention intervention with proven efficacy in a molecularly defined population. The CAPP2 and CAPP3 trials have established aspirin as the standard preventive pharmacotherapy for Lynch syndrome carriers.

**CAPP2 (Lancet, 2020):** The 10-year follow-up of 861 Lynch syndrome patients from 43 international centres (including 112 from Australasia) found:
- **600 mg/day aspirin** for 2 years reduced colorectal cancer incidence (HR 0.65 vs placebo at 10-year follow-up)
- Seven endometrial cancers in aspirin arm vs 17 in placebo arm (suggestive protective effect)
- 20-year registry data confirmed durable benefit

**CAPP3 (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 2026):** The critical dose-finding follow-up trial:
- 1,879 Lynch syndrome patients across UK, Europe, and Australia randomised to **100 mg**, 300 mg, or 600 mg daily aspirin
- **100 mg/day was non-inferior to higher doses** at 5-year follow-up — a major finding enabling clinicians to recommend low-dose aspirin with fewer GI side effects and greater long-term tolerability
- Published in 2026; original findings presented June 2025

Despite this evidence, a prior Australian national GP survey found **most GPs demonstrated suboptimal knowledge of Lynch syndrome**, and many did not actively refer patients or prescribe aspirin to known Lynch syndrome carriers — a translation gap that costs cancer prevention opportunities in the diagnosed Lynch population, let alone the undiagnosed.

### 6. Annual Surveillance Colonoscopy — MBS-Funded but Dependent on Diagnosis

For confirmed Lynch syndrome carriers, MBS does fund annual surveillance colonoscopy (items 32225, 32226 for high-risk patients). Clinical practice guidelines recommend:
- Colonoscopy every 1–2 years starting at age 20–25 (or 10 years before earliest CRC in the family)
- Annual colonoscopy after age 40

Research evidence confirms that surveillance colonoscopy reduces CRC mortality in Lynch syndrome, though it does not completely prevent CRC incidence (particularly for MSH6/PMS2 carriers where the risk trajectory is different). The MBS-funding architecture for surveillance exists — but it is downstream of the cascade of failures that means most Lynch syndrome carriers never receive a diagnosis.

### 7. Cost-Effectiveness: The Investment Case Is Clear

Universal Lynch syndrome screening via tumour dMMR testing of all incident colorectal and endometrial cancers in Australia has been modelled as **cost-effective**. A published Australian cost-effectiveness analysis (Kench et al.) found universal testing strategies dominate selective testing strategies, and a cascade testing framework based on confirmed diagnoses generates health gains at acceptable cost per QALY.

The broader population genomic screening model (Lancet eClinicalMedicine, 2023) found that at test costs of AU$200–500 per sample, combined genomic screening covering Lynch syndrome alongside BRCA1/2 and familial hypercholesterolaemia **saves healthcare system costs in the long run** through averted cancers, avoided surgeries, and reduced treatment burden.

The funding gap, therefore, is not a question of cost-effectiveness. It is a question of upfront investment, system readiness, and political priority.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Universal tumour testing policy vs. operational reality.** The HGSA/COSA position statement recommends universal dMMR tumour testing of all incident colorectal and endometrial cancers. Most major hospitals describe this as routine practice. But "describing it as routine" and "systematically executing the full cascade to germline diagnosis" are different things — the 49% referral gap in the 2022 Australian multicentre study shows the distance between policy aspiration and operational delivery.

**2. Who funds the gap between tumour testing and germline referral?** MLH1 methylation testing and MSI testing have no MBS items. In public hospitals, the cost is absorbed into pathology budgets; in private practice or regional labs, it may not be available at all. This invisible gap in the pathway means Lynch syndrome can be missed not by clinical negligence but by funding architecture.

**3. Genetics services capacity and the population screening question.** Population genomic screening for Lynch syndrome is modelled as cost-effective — but implementing it would require at minimum tripling current genetic counsellor capacity. Calling for a national program without addressing the workforce constraint is policy posturing. The real bottleneck is not the test; it is the downstream clinical genetics infrastructure.

**4. The cascade testing equity problem.** Even when a Lynch syndrome proband is diagnosed, cascade testing of at-risk relatives depends on family communication, willingness to test, and access to genetic services. Studies globally show cascade testing uptake of only 30–50% among eligible first-degree relatives within 3 years of index diagnosis. In Australia, Indigenous families, CALD communities, and rural populations face additional barriers. Cascade testing is theoretically free under MBS for confirmed relatives — but practical access is not uniform.

**5. Aspirin prescribing: the treatment gap within the diagnosed population.** Among Australians who are diagnosed with Lynch syndrome — the minority who have been identified — the evidence that low-dose aspirin prevents cancer is now robust (CAPP3, 2026). Yet prescribing rates by GPs and the proportion of confirmed Lynch syndrome carriers on aspirin is unknown. The preventive benefit already available to diagnosed patients is likely not being captured at scale.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **BRCA1/2 and hereditary breast-ovarian cancer (HBOC): the BRCA testing and olaparib/niraparib PBS access picture** — a parallel hereditary cancer system that has seen more funding investment than Lynch syndrome, offering a comparative model for what Lynch syndrome policy could look like.

2. **Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) — Australia's parallel under-diagnosis hereditary disease crisis**: FH affects ~1 in 250 Australians (similar scale to Lynch syndrome), has a clear cascade testing framework (FREMTLE registers, lipid clinics), and demonstrates how a different hereditary condition has been addressed differently within the same health system.

3. **The Australian genomic medicine roadmap — National Genomics Strategy 2025 and what it means for hereditary cancer funding**: The national framework for genomics in health care, its implementation timeline, and whether it will translate into new MBS items for Lynch syndrome testing.

4. **Gastrointestinal cancer genetics beyond Lynch: FAP, MAP, serrated polyposis, SMAD4, BMPR1A** — the broader hereditary CRC ecosystem and the patchwork of MBS funding that covers some conditions but not others.

5. **The MLH1 epimutation: why some Lynch syndrome diagnoses are clinically Lynch but germline-test-negative** — constitutive MLH1 epimutations are not detected by standard germline testing, representing a distinct diagnostic and MBS challenge.
