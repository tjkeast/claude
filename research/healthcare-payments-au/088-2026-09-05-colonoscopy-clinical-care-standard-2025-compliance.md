# The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard 2025: Australia's Quality Architecture and the Enforcement Gap

**Report 088 — 2026-09-05**

**Summary:** The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) released an updated Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard in September 2025, the first revision since 2018. The nine-quality-statement framework introduces photodocumentation as a mandatory minimum, strengthens communication requirements for NBCSP follow-up, adds cultural safety provisions, and embeds the standard in mandatory NSQHS accreditation. But the simultaneous release of an Atlas Focus Report on colonoscopy revealed a worsening access gap: overall colonoscopy rates are four times higher in major cities than in remote areas, and geographic variation in repeat colonoscopy has grown from 11-fold to 18-fold over the past decade — a finding that exposes the limits of a quality standard that governs procedure conduct without addressing procedure supply.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 087 documented that First Nations Australians participate in the NBCSP at roughly half the national rate, and that even those who screen positive face lower rates of follow-up diagnostic colonoscopy. That follow-up failure occurs within a broader colonoscopy access crisis. Today's report examines the regulatory architecture designed to govern colonoscopy quality in Australia — what the 2025 Standard requires, how compliance is enforced, and where the architecture cannot reach.

---

## Background: The Clinical Care Standard Framework

Australia's Clinical Care Standards, published by the ACSQHC, define what constitutes high-quality care for a specific condition or procedure. They do not operate as legislation, but they carry significant force through their embedding in the National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards — the eight-standard framework against which every Australian hospital, day procedure service, and most public mental health services must be accredited.

The Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard has a distinctive history: the 2018 version was the first in Australia to link clinical quality standards to mandatory credentialling. Under the NSQHS Standards (second edition), certification and recertification of colonoscopists via the Conjoint Committee for the Recognition of Training in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (CCRTGE) is mandatory for all practitioners working in accredited facilities. Colonoscopy is the only surgical procedure in Australia subject to this recertification requirement — a reflection of both the high volume of the procedure (approximately 1 million performed annually) and the documented variation in quality outcomes, particularly adenoma detection rates (ADR).

The 2025 update, released on 21 September 2025 at the World Congress of Gastroenterology / Australian Gastroenterology Week conference in Melbourne, was the first revision in seven years. It was released simultaneously with an Atlas Focus Report on colonoscopy access — a pairing that, deliberately or not, placed the Standard's quality aspirations directly alongside data showing the access failures it cannot address.

## Key Findings

### 1. What the 2025 Standard Requires: Nine Quality Statements

The 2025 Colonoscopy Clinical Care Standard outlines nine quality statements:

1. **Initial assessment and referral** — clarified requirements including, for the first time, explicit considerations for open access colonoscopy services; improved communication at referral to reduce unnecessary procedures and duplication
2. **Appropriate and timely colonoscopy** — adherence to surveillance guidelines, with emphasis on avoiding early repeat colonoscopy outside evidence-based indications
3. **Informed decision making and consent** — the patient receives sufficient information to make a genuinely informed decision, including alternatives to colonoscopy
4. **Bowel preparation** — adequate preparation using a validated assessment tool; culturally appropriate written and visual instructions; time to yarn with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients about preparation, purpose, and what to expect
5. **Sedation** — updated guidance on sedation options and discharge supervision, reflecting practice evolution since 2018
6. **Clinicians** — practitioners must be certified (initial training) or recertified (ongoing competency) through the CCRTGE
7. **Procedure** — photodocumentation now a minimum requirement; caecal intubation documented; equipment must enable safe visualisation and photodocumentation
8. **Reporting and follow-up** — results shared with patients and GPs; for NBCSP participants, outcomes and adverse events reported to the National Cancer Screening Register; reports uploaded to My Health Record to prevent lost results and unnecessary repeat procedures
9. **Monitoring and evaluation** — health service organisations must collect and periodically review ADR, sessile serrated lesion detection rate (SSLDR), caecal intubation rate, and bowel preparation adequacy

### 2. The Credentialling Architecture: CCRTGE and Mandatory Recertification

The CCRTGE, constituted by representatives from the Gastroenterological Society of Australia (GESA), the Royal Australasian College of Physicians (RACP), and the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons (RACS), administers Australia's colonoscopy credentialling system. Under the 2025 Standard, all colonoscopists practising in NSQHS-accredited facilities must hold CCRTGE certification.

Recertification performance thresholds require:
- At least **150 consecutive colonoscopies** over 3 years, with at least 50 in the past 3 years
- A **caecal intubation rate ≥95%** in intact colons
- An **adenoma detection rate ≥25%** in eligible patients
- A **sessile serrated lesion detection rate ≥4%** in eligible patients

These thresholds are well-established in the international evidence base as proxies for colonoscopy quality and the likelihood that cancer precursors are being found. The ADR threshold in particular is predictive of interval cancer rates: colonoscopists with ADR below 20% have significantly higher rates of interval cancer diagnosis compared with those above 25%.

Photodocumentation, now a minimum requirement under Quality Statement 7, creates a verifiable record of caecal intubation and key findings. Its absence had been a documented gap in the 2018 Standard — some operators reported caecal intubation without a supporting image, making audit and quality feedback impossible.

### 3. The Atlas Data: A Worsening Access Landscape

The Atlas Focus Report on Colonoscopy, released simultaneously with the updated Standard in September 2025, examined MBS-subsidised colonoscopy rates across Australia using 2023–24 data. Its findings are striking:

**Geographic access gap:**
- Overall colonoscopy rates were **2,550 per 100,000** in major cities, compared to **633 per 100,000** in remote areas — a **four-fold difference**
- This gap had widened: colonoscopy rates fell by **26%** in remote areas over the past decade, while rates in major cities increased slightly

**Socioeconomic access gap:**
- Rates in the most affluent areas: **3,067 per 100,000**
- Rates in the most disadvantaged areas: **1,675 per 100,000** — nearly half
- The gap between the least and most disadvantaged areas is growing

**Surveillance overuse and underuse:**
- The national rate of repeat colonoscopy within 3 years (generally before guidelines recommend it) decreased by **8%** overall — consistent with the Standard's push to reduce non-evidence-based early surveillance
- However, the *fall* in early repeat colonoscopy was largest in remote areas (down 26%) and disadvantaged areas (down 17%) — raising the question of whether the reduction reflects guideline adherence or simply that fewer people in these areas are receiving colonoscopy at all
- Geographic variation in repeat colonoscopy rates grew from **11-fold to 18-fold** over the decade, meaning the gap between the highest and lowest-rate areas for early repeat procedure is now extreme

The ACSQHC's media release accompanying the Atlas Report noted that "a lack of access to colonoscopy services in some areas may be contributing to the low rates in remote areas." Where follow-up is clinically indicated, a missed repeat colonoscopy may result in delayed diagnosis and treatment — including for NBCSP-positive individuals who should be receiving diagnostic assessment.

### 4. Cultural Safety Provisions: Meaningful or Marginal?

The 2025 Standard includes, for the first time, a dedicated cultural safety section. Key provisions include:
- Information should be provided in a way the patient understands and is culturally safe
- Culturally appropriate written and visual instructions on bowel preparation
- Clinicians should "allow time to yarn" about why colonoscopy is needed and what to expect
- Consideration of complicating cultural and logistical factors for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients

These are meaningful inclusions — the 2018 Standard had no First Nations-specific provisions. But they are process provisions, not access provisions. The cultural safety obligations cannot be fulfilled if there is no colonoscopist within a reasonable distance of a First Nations patient's community in the first place. The rate data reviewed above — colonoscopy rates four times higher in cities than remote areas — represents an access failure that cultural sensitivity guidelines cannot resolve.

The inclusion of bowel preparation cultural safety guidance (the "time to yarn" instruction) is directly relevant to the NBCSP follow-up gap discussed in Report 087: First Nations people who return a positive NBCSP result already face lower colonoscopy follow-up rates, and inadequate bowel preparation — one factor contributing to incomplete or repeated procedures — may be more prevalent where preparation instructions are not culturally accessible.

### 5. Compliance Architecture: Real but Limited Teeth

The 2025 Standard operates through three enforcement levers:

**NSQHS accreditation:** All accredited hospital and day procedure services are assessed against the NSQHS Standards every three years. Clinical Care Standards are embedded in Standard 1 (Clinical Governance). A "not met" finding requires a written corrective-action plan within 30–90 days. Serious or systemic findings can lead to conditional accreditation or, in rare cases, accreditation withdrawal — which would prevent the facility from operating in its current classification. In practice, accreditation withdrawal is rare; corrective-action plans are the normal response.

**CCRTGE mandatory certification:** This is the most operationally significant enforcement mechanism. A colonoscopist without CCRTGE certification cannot perform colonoscopy in an NSQHS-accredited facility. Facilities are responsible for ensuring all practitioners hold valid certification. This creates a direct link between individual clinical performance (ADR, caecal intubation rate) and facility compliance obligations.

**NCSR reporting for NBCSP:** For practitioners performing colonoscopy on NBCSP-referred patients, Quality Statement 8 requires that outcomes and adverse events be reported to the National Cancer Screening Register. This reporting obligation connects the Standard to the governance gap discussed in Reports 083 and 087 — the NCSR's incomplete data on ADR and follow-up outcomes.

The compliance architecture has a fundamental limitation: it governs the *quality* of colonoscopy where it is performed, but has no mechanism to address *access* to colonoscopy where services do not exist. A remote health service with no on-site endoscopy suite is not non-compliant with the Standard — it simply cannot offer the service at all. The Standard correctly notes that "appropriate and timely colonoscopy" includes timeliness, but it cannot create capacity.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Quality standards without supply
The 2025 Standard is a quality document, not a capacity document. It can improve colonoscopy outcomes for the fraction of Australians who access the procedure — but it cannot close the four-fold city-to-remote access gap. The ACSQHC acknowledged this in the Atlas report, but the policy response remains incomplete.

### Tension 2: The recertification system excludes the private sector partially
CCRTGE certification is mandatory in NSQHS-accredited facilities. However, open access colonoscopy — where a patient is referred directly to a service rather than a named colonoscopist — creates credentialling complexities that the 2025 Standard explicitly acknowledges in Quality Statement 1, without fully resolving them. Private practitioners performing colonoscopy outside accredited facilities may not be subject to the same certification requirements.

### Tension 3: Photodocumentation and surveillance guidelines as gatekeeping
The addition of photodocumentation and the emphasis on evidence-based surveillance intervals could reduce unnecessary early repeat colonoscopy — which would free capacity. But if the capacity freed in cities is not redirected to under-served areas, the net equity effect could be neutral or negative. Safer Care Victoria's 2025 recommendations explicitly called for this kind of active redirection, but there is no national mechanism to achieve it.

### Tension 4: NCSR reporting completeness remains voluntary in key respects
The Standard requires NBCSP colonoscopy outcomes to be reported to the NCSR — but the NCSR's ADR and histopathology data completeness problems (documented in Report 083) mean the monitoring infrastructure supporting Quality Statement 9 remains incomplete. A Standard that requires monitoring cannot deliver its quality improvement function if the data systems underpinning that monitoring are structurally deficient.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **PHI and the colonoscopy out-of-pocket gap for NBCSP follow-up.** NBCSP-positive individuals in the private system face gap fees on anaesthesia, specialist consultation, and day hospital charges. What is the real cost to an uninsured Australian, how does this deter follow-up, and does it interact with the geographic access disparities documented here?

2. **The NBCSP's 20-year performance record: what can be attributed to screening?** Australia's colorectal cancer mortality has fallen from 44 to 23 per 100,000 between 2006 and 2025. How much of that improvement is attributable to the NBCSP, how much to treatment advances, and does the First Nations non-participation mean a disproportionate share of the improvement bypassed that population?

3. **CADx and optical biopsy: the resect-and-discard strategy.** Computer-aided characterisation (CADx) can optically diagnose polyp histology in real time. Relevant here because: (a) it could reduce histopathology costs cascading from NBCSP-positive colonoscopy, and (b) it is relevant to whether photodocumentation requirements create new quality floors or merely new audit trails.

4. **Open access colonoscopy: the referral model and the new Quality Statement 1 provisions.** Open access colonoscopy (direct referral to a service rather than a named specialist) is the dominant model in Australia's public sector. The 2025 Standard's new provisions for this pathway — on referral quality, consent, and informed decision-making — deserve examination in the context of public sector colonoscopy waiting lists.

5. **The ACCHOs as preventive health infrastructure: can the bowel screening model extend?** The National Indigenous Bowel Screening Pilot demonstrated that ACCHO-delivered kit distribution can reach 40% participation. Could the same infrastructure support ACCHO-partnered colonoscopy outreach — and would the Standard's cultural safety requirements give an ACCHO-delivered service regulatory recognition?
