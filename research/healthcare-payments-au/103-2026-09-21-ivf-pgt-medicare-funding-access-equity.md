# IVF Funding in Australia: Medicare Architecture, PGT-M Coverage, and the Downstream Access Gap for Carrier Couples

**Report 103 — 2026-09-21**

**Summary:** Australia provides partial Medicare funding for IVF with no cap on lifetime cycles — a generosity that masks structural inequity. Out-of-pocket costs average $6,700–$6,900 per initial cycle, rural patients pay substantially more, and market concentration among four private providers (accounting for ~85% of cycles) suppresses competition. The 2021 listing of PGT-M items on the MBS ($95.9M commitment) was a genuine advance for couples carrying identified serious genetic conditions — but eligibility criteria exclude couples who first discover their risk through population carrier screening, creating a gap at exactly the junction Report 102 identified. A September 2025 Rapid Review found the sector's self-regulatory accreditation model inadequate, and reform is now underway, but funding access rather than safety is the deeper unresolved problem.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 102 found that Mackenzie's Mission identified ~1 in 50 couples at increased reproductive risk, with 80% carrying conditions beyond the three Medicare-funded ones, and noted that the program's equity value depends entirely on whether identified couples can access funded IVF with PGT-M. This report examines that downstream question in detail.

---

## Background: How Australia Funds Fertility Treatment

Australia's approach to IVF funding is frequently cited internationally as relatively generous — Medicare provides rebates for the major components of an IVF cycle, there is no lifetime limit on the number of funded cycles, and fertility medications are PBS-subsidised. The reality is more complicated: Medicare provides *partial* funding, leaving out-of-pocket costs that are significant for most households and effectively prohibitive for many.

The Medicare framework for IVF sits inside the broader MBS structure. The key division is between *out-of-hospital* services (specialist consultations, blood tests, ultrasound monitoring) and *in-hospital* services (egg retrieval under anaesthetic, embryo transfer). Medicare and the Extended Medicare Safety Net (EMSN) apply to the out-of-hospital components. Private health insurance may cover in-hospital costs — but only for the appropriate tier of cover, and only after a 12-month waiting period.

This interaction between Medicare and PHI creates a system where accessing IVF at all requires navigating two separate funding architectures, each with its own eligibility rules, thresholds, and gap-payment structures.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Medicare IVF Rebate Architecture

Medicare rebates are available for most clinical components of an IVF cycle:

- **Specialist consultations** (MBS item 104 and higher for fertility specialists): rebated at 85% of schedule fee for initial, 75% for subsequent out-of-hospital consultations
- **Monitoring ultrasounds** during stimulation: MBS items for obstetric ultrasound apply with schedule-fee rebates
- **Egg retrieval (oocyte pick-up)**: surgical item under anaesthetic, requiring in-hospital or day-procedure facility; private health insurance relevant here
- **Embryo transfer**: MBS items for the transfer procedure
- **Frozen embryo transfer (FET)**: separate MBS items; generally cheaper per cycle than fresh IVF

For 2025–2026, the standard Medicare rebate across all rebatable components of a first IVF cycle totals approximately **$3,720.70**; for subsequent cycles **$3,486.25**. These figures represent Medicare's contribution to the MBS schedule fees — they do not include the EMSN component, which can add further coverage once an annual out-of-pocket threshold is exceeded.

**There is no cap on the number of IVF cycles** for which Medicare rebates can be claimed. Unlike the UK (which limits NHS funding to typically 3 cycles under age 40) or many European systems, Australia places no legislative ceiling on subsidised IVF cycles. In practice, clinical judgement by treating clinicians and cost to patients moderate how many cycles occur.

### 2. Out-of-Pocket Costs: What Patients Actually Pay

Despite Medicare rebates, out-of-pocket costs for IVF in Australia remain substantial:

- **Average first cycle OOP**: approximately $6,700–$6,900 after Medicare and EMSN, based on TreatCompare analysis of 29 RTAC-accredited clinics (June 2025)
- **Total cycle cost** before Medicare: typically $10,000–$15,000 per fresh cycle at major private clinics
- **Additional costs not captured in the base figure**: laboratory add-ons (embryo glue, assisted hatching, endometrial receptivity testing), embryo storage fees (~$500–$800/year), some medications not on the PBS, and — critically — PGT testing

The EMSN provides some relief for couples doing multiple cycles within a calendar year: once total out-of-pocket gap fees for out-of-hospital services exceed approximately $2,544 (2026 threshold), Medicare reimburses 80% of further gap fees for the rest of that year. For couples undergoing a second or third cycle in the same year, EMSN can reduce OOP costs by $1,000–$2,000 per subsequent cycle.

**PBS-subsidised fertility medications** represent a further offset. FSH, GnRH agonists and antagonists, and progesterone support are Authority-required PBS medications; general patients pay approximately $30–$40 per script, concession card holders $7.70. Total medication costs per cycle are typically $600–$1,200 before subsidy, significantly less for concession holders.

### 3. The PGT-M Medicare Items: A 2021 Advance

Before 1 November 2021, preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic conditions (PGT-M) attracted no Medicare rebate at all. The $95.9 million investment announced by the federal government in May 2021, implemented from November 2021, created five new MBS items:

| Item | Description | Purpose |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **73384** | Genetic analysis of reproductive couple | Pre-cycle testing to characterise the pathogenic variants in both partners |
| **13207** | Embryo biopsy (surgical) | Extraction of cells from the embryo for testing |
| **73385** | Embryonic genetic testing — 1 embryo | Testing biopsied tissue from 1 embryo |
| **73386** | Embryonic genetic testing — 2 embryos | Testing biopsied tissue from 2 embryos |
| **73387** | Embryonic genetic testing — 3+ embryos | Testing biopsied tissue from 3 or more embryos |

**Eligibility criteria** (MBS conditions of access):
- The patient or their reproductive partner has an identified pathogenic gene variant; AND
- That variant places the couple at risk of a pregnancy affected by a **Mendelian or mitochondrial disorder**, or an **autosomal dominant disorder**, or a **chromosomal rearrangement or copy number variant**; AND
- There is **no curative treatment** for the disorder; AND
- The disorder causes **severe limitation of quality of life** despite contemporary management

Conditions covered under PGT-M include cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, fragile X syndrome, muscular dystrophy, neurofibromatosis, and Huntington disease, among others.

The 85% Medicare rebate applies to these items (75% for subsequent episodes). In practice, this substantially reduces — but does not eliminate — the per-cycle cost of PGT. Typical out-of-pocket costs for the PGT component (laboratory set-up, biopsy, embryo testing) remain $1,500–$3,000 above the Medicare rebate, depending on the number of embryos tested.

**Only one PGT testing item** (73385, 73386, or 73387) may be claimed per ART cycle.

### 4. The Gap: Carrier Screening Couples and PGT Eligibility

The PGT-M MBS items require that an identified pathogenic variant already be known. This creates an access tension that sits directly at the junction with Report 102's findings:

- A couple who **already knows** they carry a condition (because of family history, or because they paid privately for expanded carrier screening) and then undergo PGT-M cycles can **claim Medicare rebates** for the genetic testing component.
- A couple who **discovers their risk for the first time** through the new Medicare-funded three-gene carrier screening (CF/SMA/fragile X, items 73451/73452) is in exactly the right position to use PGT-M — and **can claim the rebate** for those three conditions.
- A couple who discovers risk for conditions **outside the three-gene screen** — whether through private expanded carrier testing or (if a national program were funded) through an expanded program — would need to meet the "no curative treatment / severe quality of life limitation" eligibility criteria for their specific condition to access PGT-M rebates.

In practice, most conditions identified by expanded carrier screening panels would meet PGT-M criteria — but couples must first have a specific pathogenic variant identified through a positive carrier test before the MBS items become claimable. The cost of expanded carrier testing itself ($300–$600 privately, without Medicare rebate for most conditions) thus becomes a prerequisite cost for accessing subsidised PGT-M.

This is the downstream access gap Mackenzie's Mission identified: the screening program creates information that triggers a funded clinical pathway (PGT-M), but only for couples who can either afford the expanded screening test or who happen to carry one of the three Medicare-funded conditions.

### 5. The 2025 Medicare Eligibility Reform: Removing the Heterosexual-Couple Restriction

For decades, Medicare rebates for IVF were implicitly restricted to heterosexual couples satisfying a medical definition of infertility requiring prior periods of unprotected intercourse. This excluded single women, lesbian couples, and — in many practical interpretations — gay male couples pursuing surrogacy.

**April 2025 reform:** The federal government formally expanded Medicare eligibility to remove restrictions based on relationship status, sexual orientation, or gender identity. The outdated infertility definition was replaced, and single individuals and same-sex couples are now fully eligible for Medicare rebates for IVF treatment (excluding surrogacy, which remains outside the Medicare framework).

This reform was partly prompted by the updated American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) infertility definition (2024) and by advocacy from fertility societies and patient groups who argued that treating social infertility differently from medical infertility was discriminatory.

The practical effect: same-sex female couples and single women no longer need to pay for multiple cycles of donor insemination before qualifying for Medicare-rebatable IVF.

### 6. Rural and Regional Access: A Geography-Driven Equity Gap

Approximately 30% of Australians live in regional, rural, or remote areas. IVF access for these populations involves:

- **Travel costs**: Multiple trips to capital cities for baseline consultations, hormone monitoring (blood tests and ultrasounds during stimulation phase), egg retrieval under anaesthetic, and transfer; plus accommodation and childcare for existing children
- **Geographic price disparities**: Research and media reporting documents significant price variation by location. One frequently cited example: IVF treatment in Townsville was approximately 75% more expensive than equivalent treatment in Brisbane — counter-intuitively, due to lower economies of scale and higher operating costs in regional clinics
- **Workforce gaps**: Regional clinics report difficulty attracting and retaining embryologists, fertility specialists, and support staff; smaller professional networks, fewer training opportunities, and lifestyle considerations all contribute
- **Telehealth partial mitigation**: Monitoring blood tests can be processed locally; telehealth consultations have reduced travel burden for some components since 2020; but egg retrieval under anaesthetic and (usually) embryo transfer still require travel to clinic

A 2026 PMC study ("'The Right to Have a Family Should Not Be Determined by Your Postcode'") found that rural women consistently reported travel costs, time away from work, and geographic isolation as major barriers, and that the additional financial burden of regional IVF was often equivalent to an additional full IVF cycle in cost.

### 7. Market Concentration and the Private Equity Problem

Australia's IVF sector is dominated by four major private providers:
- **Virtus Health**: ~37% market share; acquired by BGH Capital (private equity) in 2022
- **Monash IVF Group**: ~31% market share; ASX-listed (MVF), itself subject to ongoing consolidation discussion
- **Genea**: ~12% market share; privately held
- **City Fertility Centre**: ~5% market share

These four providers collectively account for approximately 85% of cycles. The remainder is split across smaller independent and public-sector providers.

Private equity concentration in the IVF sector raises policy concerns that echo those in aged care and other health sectors:
- **Fee competition reduction**: Where local monopoly or duopoly exists (particularly in regional areas), patients have limited ability to compare or switch providers
- **Incentive alignment**: Vertically integrated private providers may have financial incentives to recommend ancillary services (add-ons) that are not evidence-based and attract gap payments
- **Transparency**: The 2025 Rapid Review found that existing accreditation structures lacked transparency in reporting of clinical outcomes, adverse events, and pricing

### 8. The September 2025 Rapid Review: Safety Failures and Reform

A series of high-profile incidents — most prominently two mistaken embryo transfers at separate Australian clinics — prompted federal, state, and territory health ministers to commission a Rapid Review of ART regulation and accreditation in June 2025. The review reported in September 2025.

**Key findings:**
- The existing **Reproductive Technology Accreditation Committee (RTAC)** model — an industry-led self-regulatory body run by the Fertility Society of Australia and New Zealand — was found to be "not adequate" and lacking "the transparency and rigour that governments and the community expect"
- Accreditation standards were not sufficiently rigorous; audit processes were not effective in identifying breaches
- There was no national mechanism for reporting or learning from serious adverse events
- The fragmented state-based legislative framework (each state has separate ART legislation) creates regulatory gaps

**Recommended reform:**
From 2027, the **Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC)** will independently accredit ART services against updated national standards. This transfers accreditation from the professional body (FSANZ/RTAC) to an independent government-backed entity — the same model that operates in hospitals more broadly.

Health Ministers agreed to the reform on 12 September 2025. Implementation is phased to 2027.

The Rapid Review focused primarily on safety and accreditation rather than on affordability and access — the latter remain outside its scope.

### 9. State Government Programs: Victoria and NSW

**Victoria** operates Australia's most developed public fertility program, delivered through the Royal Women's Hospital and satellite public hospital sites. Victorians who meet income and clinical eligibility criteria can access publicly funded IVF cycles at significantly reduced cost. The program has delivered approximately 350 babies and provides an alternative pathway for lower-income couples. Victoria has also introduced stronger consumer protections for IVF patients in 2025, requiring clearer fee disclosure and stronger informed consent requirements.

**NSW** has a lower-cost IVF program available at selected public hospital sites.

**South Australia** introduced means-tested IVF rebates from 1 July 2026 for residents receiving Family Tax Benefit, holding a Health Care Card, or with household income below $180,000.

There is no nationally coordinated public IVF access scheme equivalent to the cancer screening programs. Access to publicly supported IVF remains patchy, state-dependent, and overwhelmingly in metropolitan locations.

### 10. Cost-Effectiveness and the "No Cap" Tension

Australia's absence of any cycle limit is internationally unusual. The evidence on cost-effectiveness by cycle number and age is relevant:

- Research suggests at least five publicly funded cycles are cost-beneficial for women **under 42**, given cumulative live birth rates (roughly 70.4% across three cycles for women under 30 vs. 22.5% for women aged 40–44)
- For women **aged 42 and over**, the live-birth rate per cycle is sufficiently low that the cost per live birth becomes very high — IVF is unlikely to be cost-beneficial from a payer perspective, though individual patient autonomy complicates any restriction
- The unlimited funding model effectively cross-subsidises low-probability cycles in older patients with the rebate budget that could alternatively fund additional equity programs for younger patients without access

A 2025 Frontiers in Reproductive Health expert forum on fertility treatment access policy recommended age-stratified cost-effectiveness assessments be incorporated into future Medicare funding policy — not to impose hard cutoffs, but to ensure the funding architecture is transparent about its trade-offs.

A separate JHEOR analysis (2026) attempted to quantify the *fiscal value* to government of IVF-conceived children (tax revenue, social contributions) and found a positive fiscal return on Medicare IVF investment across the relevant age cohorts — a frame rarely used in Australian health economic discourse.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. The PGT-M catch-22 for carrier-screening-identified couples.** The carrier screening program creates risk information; the PGT-M items fund acting on it. But expanded carrier screening is not yet Medicare-funded for most conditions, meaning the couple who most needs PGT-M access must first pay privately for the expanded carrier test that would establish their eligibility. This is a circular funding problem.

**2. Cycle cap or not?** Australia's no-limit model is equitable in that it doesn't restrict access by number, but inequitable in that unlimited access is only meaningfully accessible to couples who can afford the large per-cycle OOP costs. A cycle-capped public program that fully funded a defined number of cycles (as in the UK NHS model) might serve low-income couples better than unlimited partial subsidy.

**3. Market concentration and fee inflation.** The rapid consolidation of the IVF sector under private equity creates a setting where prices are unlikely to fall and ancillary services may be oversold. The Rapid Review's focus on safety accreditation does not address the commercial structure of the sector.

**4. Rural equity.** The geographic access gap is well-documented and structural — rural patients pay more in travel costs and face higher clinical fees in regional clinics. No federal program addresses this specifically for IVF, unlike the Modified Monash incentives in primary care.

**5. The downstream gap for expanded carrier screening.** If Australia implements a national expanded carrier screening program (as Report 102 advocates), the PGT-M funding framework needs to be reviewed to ensure that couples identified through that program — potentially tens of thousands of new at-risk couples per year — can access PGT-M without facing a prior user-pays carrier test as a gatekeeping cost.

**6. Add-ons and evidence-based practice.** The Rapid Review flagged concerns about clinics offering and charging for IVF add-ons (e.g., endometrial receptivity testing, sperm DNA fragmentation analysis, embryoscope time-lapse monitoring) at significant gap costs, many of which have limited or contested evidence of benefit. Regulation of add-on evidence standards is a known gap.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Newborn bloodspot screening — the NBS expansion pipeline** — The National Bloodspot Screening (NBS) panel currently covers ~25 conditions. MSAC and the Department of Health have active work on expanding to more conditions, including SMA (the same condition at the heart of Mackenzie's Mission). This is the postnatal complement to reproductive carrier screening and raises parallel questions about which conditions are included, how testing infrastructure scales, and what happens when a condition is identified but treatment is expensive or not yet PBS-listed.

2. **Surrogacy in Australia — the one frontier Medicare doesn't fund** — Commercial surrogacy is prohibited in all Australian jurisdictions; altruistic surrogacy is permitted in most states but is largely unregulated and unsubsidised. Gay male couples and women without a uterus face significant additional costs and complexity. The Medicare exclusion of surrogacy is increasingly anomalous against the April 2025 equity reforms.

3. **The IVF add-ons industry and evidence standards** — Multiple systematic reviews have found most IVF "add-ons" (time-lapse imaging, ERA testing, IMSI, assisted hatching) have no proven benefit in increasing live birth rates in unselected patients. Patients pay significant gap costs for these services. How does Australia regulate add-on evidence claims, and what does the Rapid Review's accreditation reform do (or not do) about this?

4. **Fertility preservation and the access gap** — Medicare does not fund oocyte or embryo freezing for social fertility preservation (egg freezing). For people with cancer requiring gonadotoxic chemotherapy, Medicare items exist for fertility preservation — but coverage is narrow and out-of-pocket costs remain high. This is a growing equity and workforce issue as egg-freezing uptake rises.

5. **RTAC to ACSQHC transition: what the accreditation reform means for clinics** — The Rapid Review's shift of ART accreditation from RTAC to the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care is a significant structural change. What standards will the ACSQHC apply? What happens to the approximately 80 RTAC-accredited clinics currently operating? And what enforcement powers does the new regime carry?
