# The Broad PBS Listing Model: Australia's Tumour-Agnostic Approach to Funding Checkpoint Inhibitors

**Report 095 — 2026-09-12**

**Summary:** In 2026, Australia became the first country to fund checkpoint inhibitors under a genuinely tumour-agnostic, clinician-discretion model — without mandatory biomarker gating. Nivolumab plus ipilimumab (Opdivo/Yervoy) received such a listing from 1 March 2026, and pembrolizumab (Keytruda) followed from 1 September 2026, just eleven days ago. Both listings use a novel "broad multi-indication" payment structure: a single weighted-average PBS price across all cancer indications, with confidential utilisation-based rebate caps. The model solves a long-standing access problem — rare cancers that can't support individual PBAC submissions — but raises significant questions about prescribing governance, diagnostic testing funding, and PBS sustainability when clinical judgment replaces biomarker-gated access.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 094 documented Australia's cancer genomics testing gap — the MBS covers only a narrow set of biomarker tests, and many PBS-listed precision oncology drugs require testing that is not routinely funded. The checkpoint inhibitor broad listing takes the opposite approach: rather than tying PBS access to a specific genomic marker, it shifts to clinical judgment. This report examines what that shift means for access, governance, and the diagnostic testing landscape.

---

## Background: The Indication-by-Indication Bottleneck

Pembrolizumab is the world's most commercially successful cancer medicine. As of mid-2025, MSD had submitted 40 reimbursement applications to the PBAC across the drug's TGA-registered indications. Of those, only 15 indications were partially or fully PBS-funded — despite pembrolizumab having clinical evidence across dozens of cancer types. The average time from TGA registration to PBS listing for oncology indications is approximately **447 days** — and for cancers with small patient populations, companies often don't submit at all, judging the commercial case too weak for the PBAC assessment process.

This is the structural problem the broad listing model is designed to solve: it enables PBS access to immunotherapies for cancers that would never attract their own PBAC submission — rare tumour types with limited commercial incentive and small trial populations.

## Key Findings

### 1. Australia's World-First Move: Nivolumab + Ipilimumab Without Biomarkers (March 2026)

The combination of nivolumab (Opdivo) and ipilimumab (Yervoy) received a broad, multi-indication PBS listing from **1 March 2026** — described in peer-reviewed commentary (Annals of Oncology, 2026) as the "world's first tumour-agnostic reimbursement of dual immunotherapy."

The defining feature: **no biomarker requirement**. Globally, all existing tumour-agnostic drug approvals — including the FDA's groundbreaking 2017 approval of pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR solid tumours — depend on a molecular marker. Australia's nivolumab/ipilimumab listing abandoned this model. Instead, clinicians can prescribe the combination for any advanced or metastatic cancer where, in their clinical judgment, the patient is likely to benefit, "based on the best available evidence at the time."

The PBAC recommended the broad listing in September 2025; it took effect March 1. It also:
- Removed the previous "once in a lifetime" restriction that had applied to nivolumab and ipilimumab across cancer types
- Expanded access to rare cancers for which dedicated PBS submissions had never been lodged

### 2. Pembrolizumab Joins the Broad Listing Model (September 2026)

From **1 September 2026**, pembrolizumab (Keytruda) received a similar broad multi-indication PBS listing. The new listing applies to advanced or metastatic cancers where there is evidence the disease is "immunotherapy-sensitive" — clinical judgment-based access, not biomarker-gated.

Before this listing, pembrolizumab had **17 PBS-listed indications**, each requiring its own authority prescription with specific criteria. The new broad listing replaces this with a single category: clinician assessment of immunotherapy responsiveness.

Immediately affected cancers newly accessible on PBS include:
- Advanced **cholangiocarcinoma** (bile duct cancer)
- Advanced **ovarian cancer**
- **Merkel cell carcinoma** (a rare and aggressive skin cancer)

New cancer types — previously inaccessible without $14,800 per script out-of-pocket costs — can now be treated under the PBS co-payment (general: $25 per script; concessional: $7.70).

**Patient impact:** Approximately **700 Australians per year** are expected to benefit immediately. MSD estimates more than **10,000 Australians** will become eligible over the next four years under the broader arrangement.

### 3. The Road to Approval: PBAC's July 2025 Rejection and December 2025 Reversal

The path to the pembrolizumab broad listing was not straightforward. At its **July 2025 meeting**, PBAC rejected MSD's initial multi-cancer funding submission. The rejection was described by patient advocates as "bewildering," with Rare Cancers Australia arguing it was "a missed opportunity to ensure Australian cancer patients [were] not left behind."

PBAC's stated concerns centred on two points:
1. The proposed model was limited to indications for which pembrolizumab already held TGA registration — which PBAC considered would not provide access to some patient groups, including rare cancers where formal regulatory submissions were unlikely
2. The clinical governance and prescribing conditions required further specification to prevent inappropriate use

MSD resubmitted at the **December 2025 Intracycle PBAC meeting**, addressing these concerns. PBAC noted that MSD had "largely addressed its concerns from the July 2025 submission" and recommended the broad listing — on the condition that pembrolizumab join the same Risk Sharing Arrangement (RSA) as the nivolumab/ipilimumab broad listing, with consistent restriction wording across both medicines.

### 4. The Payment Model: Weighted Average Price and Confidential Rebate Caps

Both broad listings use a novel funding architecture that is a significant departure from standard PBS pricing:

**Single weighted-average price:** Rather than separate schedule prices for each cancer indication, a single price is calculated as a weighted average across all existing PBS-funded uses, weighted by observed historical utilisation volumes.

**Utilisation-based rebate caps:** Utilisation up to a first cap reflects use in already-listed indications and is priced at the weighted-average rate. Utilisation beyond this cap is assumed to reflect new indications (the "additional" access the broad listing enables) and is subject to a **confidential rebate** — meaning the government pays a lower, undisclosed effective price for the expanded use.

This architecture serves two functions: it controls PBS expenditure growth (the rebate provides a government backstop against unexpected utilisation) and it avoids requiring a cost-effectiveness demonstration for each new indication individually. However, the confidential nature of the rebates makes independent fiscal analysis impossible.

**PBAC's consistency condition:** The PBAC explicitly required that restriction wording for pembrolizumab and nivolumab/ipilimumab broad listings be made consistent — preventing clinical arbitrage where physicians choose between the two based on slightly different eligibility criteria.

### 5. The MSI-H Biomarker Context: How Australia Got Here

The FDA's 2017 approval of pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR solid tumours — the first tumour-agnostic drug approval in history — established biomarker-gated access as the global model. MSI-H status identifies patients whose tumours have high mutation rates due to mismatch repair failure, making them more responsive to checkpoint inhibition.

In Australia, MSI/dMMR testing has historically been claimed via **MBS item 72847** (general immunohistochemistry with 4–6 antibodies), which is not specific to MSI-H assessment. There is no dedicated MBS item for MSI testing across all cancer types: for colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer, reflex dMMR testing via IHC is well established; for rarer tumour types, testing access is inconsistent and sometimes out-of-pocket.

MSAC has an active application (**Application 1674**) for a new MBS item specifically to fund dMMR IHC testing for eligibility to access dostarlimab (another anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor) in endometrial cancer — a small but significant step toward dedicated diagnostic item coverage.

The broad PBS listing model sidesteps this diagnostic funding gap: because pembrolizumab's new listing is not MSI-H-gated, the question of whether MSI testing is MBS-funded becomes less critical for PBS access — though it remains relevant for clinical decision-making.

### 6. Prescribing Governance: The Open Question

The move to clinical discretion creates a prescribing governance gap that is openly acknowledged by clinicians and the PBAC. With biomarker-gated access, the test result serves as the gating mechanism: if the marker is present, the patient qualifies; if not, they don't. Without a biomarker requirement, access depends on the clinician's judgment of "immunotherapy sensitivity" — a category with no agreed-upon definition, no auditable test result, and no uniform clinical criteria.

The PBAC's December 2025 recommendation noted that restriction wording needed to be made consistent and carefully specified. But the restriction, as implemented, still relies heavily on clinical discretion. This raises:

- **Monitoring risk**: How will the Department of Health assess whether prescribing is consistent with the intent of the broad listing?
- **Equity risk**: Will clinicians at major metropolitan oncology centres be more likely to identify "immunotherapy-sensitive" cancers than clinicians in regional and rural settings?
- **Fiscal risk**: If prescribing expands more rapidly than the utilisation cap predicts, the rebate mechanism must work as designed.

Australia's approach is genuinely novel and arguably more access-positive than the biomarker model for rare cancers — but it trades one form of gatekeeping (molecular testing) for another (clinical judgment), and the governance infrastructure to oversee the latter is not yet in place.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Is the "immunotherapy-sensitive" criterion auditable?** The restriction does not specify what evidence a clinician must document to support their judgment. Without a test result, how does the Department of Health audit compliance with the broad listing's intent?

**2. Does the broad model undermine precision testing incentives?** If clinicians can prescribe pembrolizumab without a biomarker test, the incentive to test — and to advocate for MBS-funded testing — may weaken, even where testing would help identify patients most likely to respond.

**3. What happens to MBS funding for dMMR/MSI testing?** MSAC Application 1674 for dostarlimab/endometrial cancer is still narrower than the broad listing's scope. The broad listing removes biomarker requirements; the diagnostic pathway hasn't been reformed to match.

**4. Can the RSA model contain costs?** The confidential rebate cap structure relies on accurate utilisation projections. If the broad listing enables substantially more use than anticipated, and if the rebate caps are poorly calibrated, PBS expenditure growth in checkpoint inhibitors could accelerate significantly.

**5. Does the model export?** PharmacoEconomics research (2026) is examining the implications of Australia's PD-1/PD-L1 broad listing model globally. If the RSA-with-weighted-average-price model is seen as sustainable, other countries may adopt it — and pharmaceutical companies may seek it proactively.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Dostarlimab and the dMMR/MSI-H testing landscape**: MSAC Application 1674 for dostarlimab in endometrial cancer is a window into how Australia is building out the diagnostic infrastructure for checkpoint inhibitor access tumour-by-tumour. What is dostarlimab's funding status, and what does the MSAC assessment reveal about Australia's approach to diagnostic co-investment?

2. **Rare cancers and the PBS access gap before the broad listing**: The broad listing exists because rare cancer patients could not access immunotherapies that might help them. How bad was the access gap pre-2026? Which cancer types waited longest between TGA registration and PBS access, and which are now included for the first time?

3. **The PBAC's cost-effectiveness framework under stress**: The broad listing explicitly bypasses per-indication cost-effectiveness assessment. The RSA replaces it with a price-and-volume arrangement. Does this represent a sustainable PBS evolution, or a precedent that undermines the PBAC's evidence gating function?

4. **Immune checkpoint inhibitor safety monitoring**: The shift to clinical discretion and broader use increases the population exposed to immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Who monitors irAE rates under the broad listing, and what pharmacovigilance infrastructure exists?

5. **The TGA-PBS-MSAC pipeline for tumour-agnostic indications**: As FDA and EMA accelerate tumour-agnostic approvals (e.g., for NTRK fusions, RET fusions, BRAF V600E across tumour types), how will Australia's pipeline — TGA registration, then PBAC or MSAC assessment — manage an increasing volume of pan-tumour submissions?
