# The Colonoscopy Funding Gap: How MBS Schedule Fees, Wait Times, and Workforce Shortages Undermine NBCSP Follow-Up

**Report 080 — 2026-08-25**

**Summary:** Australia's National Bowel Cancer Screening Program generates roughly 100,000 positive iFOBT results each year, but the pathway from a positive result to a diagnostic colonoscopy is fractured by three compounding failures: a Medicare Benefits Schedule that substantially underprices colonoscopy relative to what providers actually charge; a public hospital system so capacity-constrained that fewer than 15% of positive screeners receive a colonoscopy within the recommended 30-day timeframe; and a colonoscopy workforce so unevenly distributed that rural and regional Australians face waits measured in months, not weeks, for a procedure that determines whether a curable early-stage cancer becomes an incurable late-stage one.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 079 established that the NBCSP's entire economic logic rests on a single mechanism — detecting bowel cancer at stage I instead of stage IV — and that this mechanism is undermined by low participation. This report examines the second structural failure: even among people who do return a positive iFOBT, the colonoscopy follow-up system itself creates barriers that delay or prevent the diagnosis that screening was designed to enable.

---

## Background: The Critical Link Between Positive Screen and Colonoscopy

The iFOBT kit that forms the core of the NBCSP is not a diagnostic test — it is a risk stratification tool. A positive iFOBT result means that blood has been detected in the stool, triggering the requirement for a diagnostic colonoscopy to determine whether that blood indicates cancer, precancerous polyps, or benign causes. Approximately 8–10% of kits returned test positive. At the NBCSP's current participation scale (approximately 44% of the 50–74 eligible population), this generates roughly 100,000 positive results nationally per year.

The clinical urgency of prompt follow-up is well-established. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care's colonoscopy quality standards recommend that positive-screen patients receive diagnostic colonoscopy within 30 days. At stage I CRC, five-year survival exceeds 90%. At stage IV — which develops, on average, over 12–24 months from a small adenoma — survival drops to 12–16%. Every month of delay from a positive result to colonoscopy is a month in which an undetected cancer may advance.

The screening program cannot deliver its survival benefit if the colonoscopy step fails.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Follow-Up Rate: 86% — and What That Hides

The headline figure from the AIHW National Bowel Cancer Screening Program monitoring report (2025) is that in 2023, **86% of people with a positive iFOBT result had a follow-up diagnostic assessment** recorded. This appears reassuringly high — but three problems undercut it.

**First, the 14% non-follow-up is not random.** Colonoscopy follow-up rates are consistently lower in rural and remote areas, among First Nations Australians, and in lower socioeconomic quintiles. The 14% without recorded follow-up is disproportionately drawn from populations at highest CRC risk and lowest baseline access — the same populations for whom an undetected stage IV diagnosis is most catastrophic.

**Second, the 30-day benchmark is being met by almost no one.** AIHW data shows that in 2024, only **14.6%** of positive-screen participants received a colonoscopy within 30 days (up marginally from 13.9% in 2023). The median time from positive iFOBT to diagnostic assessment was **62 days nationally** — but this national median conceals state and territory variation from 132 days to 202 days depending on location. A positive screener in the ACT, for example, faces a system where the public endoscopy waitlist has at times exceeded 6,000 patients against annual capacity of approximately 5,000 procedures.

**Third, recorded follow-up is not always the same as timely, high-quality colonoscopy.** Data completeness in the NBCSP's colonoscopy reporting remains imperfect; a colonoscopy performed at a private service may not be returned to the program registry in all cases.

### 2. The MBS Framework: From 32090/32093 to the 2020 Indication-Specific Reform

Until May 2020, most diagnostic colonoscopies were billed under MBS items **32090** (colonoscopy to the descending colon) and **32093** (colonoscopy with polypectomy). These items were criticised by the MBS Review's Gastroenterology Clinical Committee for being insufficiently stratified by clinical complexity, potentially incentivising low-value repeated colonoscopy, and not reflecting the full cost of providing a high-quality examination.

In November 2019 (effective May 2020), these items were replaced by a suite of **indication-specific items — 32222 to 32228** — that tie each colonoscopy claim to a defined clinical indication:

- **Item 32222**: Diagnostic colonoscopy for surveillance of personal history of adenomas, CRC, or inflammatory bowel disease
- **Item 32223**: Diagnostic colonoscopy for a confirmed positive screening test (iFOBT) — the primary NBCSP follow-up item
- **Item 32224**: Diagnostic colonoscopy for a significant family history of CRC
- **Item 32226**: Diagnostic colonoscopy for investigation of symptoms
- **Item 32228**: An "exception" item for clinically indicated colonoscopy where insufficient patient information exists to meet other item criteria — available once per patient per lifetime

The reform also addressed procedural items (32229 for polypectomy), endoscopic mucosal resection (item 32230, updated in November 2021), and introduced claiming restrictions to reduce same-day duplicate billing.

**The current MBS schedule fees for core colonoscopy items (updated 1 July 2025):**
- Item 32223 (NBCSP follow-up): approximately **$339.70** schedule fee; Medicare pays 75% = **~$255** for in-hospital private patients
- Item 32229 (colonoscopy with polypectomy): **$314.60** schedule fee; 75% benefit = **~$236**
- The schedule fee also attracts an anaesthetic item (approximately $300–400 in schedule fees) and a separate hospital item

### 3. The Cost Architecture and Who It Harms

The colonoscopy cost structure in Australia differs substantially by whether the patient accesses the public or private system:

**Public hospital pathway:**
A colonoscopy performed in a public hospital as a public patient is **free**. This is the correct pathway for most positive NBCSP participants — it removes the financial barrier entirely. The problem is not the price but the wait. With median wait times of 62 days nationally and well over 100 days in many jurisdictions, the free public option comes with a clinical cost measured in tumour progression time.

**Private hospital pathway — insured patient:**
For patients with appropriate private hospital cover, the private colonoscopy pathway is substantially faster (typically 1–4 weeks), and 74% have **no out-of-pocket cost**. The remaining 26% pay an average gap of approximately **$200**. The hospital excess (typically $250–$500 per admission for basic-tier cover) applies separately. This pathway is broadly accessible to the approximately 55% of Australians with private hospital insurance — but skews toward higher-income, urban populations.

**Private hospital pathway — uninsured patient:**
For an uninsured patient who opts for or is directed to private care, the all-in cost is approximately **$2,000**, comprising:
- Specialist (gastroenterologist/surgeon) fee: ~$1,300
- Hospital facility fee: ~$750
- Anaesthetist: ~$200–$400
- Pathology (biopsy processing): ~$50–$150

Medicare pays 75% of the schedule fee for each in-hospital item for private patients — but since the schedule fee is only $339.70 for the primary colonoscopy item (a fraction of the actual fee charged), the Medicare rebate covers perhaps $255 of a $1,300 specialist bill. The patient pays the remainder.

The gap between the MBS schedule fee and the actual charge is widest in metropolitan private practice, where gastroenterologists — specialists in high demand and short supply — can set fees substantially above the schedule. Medical Costs Finder data for 2023–24 showed the average specialist fee for colonoscopy was approximately $1,300, meaning the Medicare rebate covered roughly 20% of the specialist component.

### 4. Workforce Distribution: The Colonoscopy Desert

The financial barrier for uninsured patients is severe but in many parts of Australia the prior problem is simpler: there is no gastroenterologist to perform the colonoscopy at all.

A 2022 study on the distribution and composition of colonoscopy providers in Australia found **marked geographic concentration**, with colonoscopy services heavily clustered in major cities and inner regional areas. Outer regional, remote, and very remote Australians face colonoscopy access barriers that are structural — there is no private provider to pay even if the money were available, and public hospital endoscopy lists serve populations across vast catchment areas with staffing measured in sessions per week.

The NBCSP's screening workforce demand modelling (published in *BMC Health Services Research*, 2021) found that the expansion of the NBCSP to include 45–49 year olds — implemented in July 2024 — would require an additional 50,000–60,000 colonoscopies per year on top of existing demand from 50–74 year olds with positive results. This demand increase falls on a workforce that was already strained: significant proportions of current colonoscopists are approaching retirement, and barriers to recruitment to endoscopy include the physical burden of the procedure, a preference for non-endoscopic gastroenterology, and the relative unattractiveness of procedural practice in regional settings.

The ACT's situation — a waiting list of over 6,000 against annual capacity of approximately 5,000 — illustrates the systemic constraint. When the wait list substantially exceeds annual throughput, the system cannot clear it through normal operations alone.

### 5. NSW's Direct Access Colonoscopy Model: A Partial Solution

The most significant structural innovation to address the NBCSP follow-up bottleneck is New South Wales's **Direct Access Colonoscopy (DAC) program**, expanded across the state from 2020 and revised in 2024.

DAC is a **nurse-led, GP-referral pathway** that bypasses the requirement for a prior specialist consultation and enables direct colonoscopy booking for asymptomatic, low-risk positive-iFOBT patients aged 45–75. Over **80% of NSW residents** now live within reach of a DAC service. Key features:

- GPs directly refer eligible positive-screen patients to DAC units in public hospitals — no specialist referral required
- A standardised protocol (including the Refer4Scope tool, co-developed with the Daffodil Centre) guides clinical assessment and exclusion criteria
- Nurse-led pre-procedure assessment replaces the gastroenterologist consultation step
- The procedure is performed free (as a public patient) — the DAC model eliminates the cost barrier entirely for eligible participants

The limitation is that DAC is only available in public hospitals with endoscopy capacity, and its availability in rural and remote NSW is patchier than the "80% of residents" coverage figure suggests — geographic access to a hub service may require hours of travel.

Victoria introduced new companion statewide referral criteria in February 2024 to rationalise the public colonoscopy waitlist by clinical risk category. Neither NSW's DAC nor Victoria's new criteria have been systematically adopted nationally.

### 6. What the 14% Non-Follow-Up Actually Costs

At current NBCSP scales, the 14% of positive-screen participants who do not have a recorded colonoscopy follow-up represents approximately **14,000 people per year**. If a third of these have, in fact, received colonoscopy through pathways not captured in the registry, the effective non-follow-up is closer to 10,000 annually.

At the documented stage distribution differential between screen-detected and non-screen-detected CRC — with screen-detected cancers presenting at stage IV 3–5.5% of the time versus 15–20% for symptom-detected cancers — an undetected positive-screen patient who eventually presents symptomatically is approximately three to four times more likely to be stage IV at diagnosis. For a cohort of 10,000 patients, even a small proportion with actual CRC (around 7% of positive-screen participants are ultimately found to have CRC or advanced adenomas) translates to hundreds of preventable late-stage diagnoses annually.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Free in Public, But Months Away — Private, But Only If Insured

The system design creates a two-tier outcome: insured patients access colonoscopy quickly and cheaply; uninsured patients choose between a free service with a wait measured in months, or an expensive private option that strips most of the financial benefit from screening. The latter group — lower-income, uninsured, often with greatest CRC risk — faces the worst of both options.

### Tension 2: The MBS Reform Improved Clinical Targeting But Not Funding Adequacy

The 2020 restructuring of colonoscopy MBS items from generic to indication-specific was a sound clinical reform — it targeted high-value indications and reduced low-value repeat colonoscopy. But it did not address the fundamental disconnect between MBS schedule fees and the actual cost of providing a colonoscopy in private settings. Schedule fees of ~$340 for a procedure costing $1,300 in specialist fees alone produce a gap that the market cannot close without either the patient or private health insurance absorbing it.

### Tension 3: The 30-Day Benchmark Is Unachievable Without Capacity Investment

The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care's 30-day colonoscopy benchmark is meeting a 14.6% compliance rate. This is not a failure of individual hospitals — it is a system-level incapacity. Meeting the benchmark would require either a substantial expansion of public endoscopy capacity (endoscopy suites, trained endoscopists, recovery capacity) or a systematic shift of positive-screen patients to private providers with adequately subsidised access — i.e., a near-universal private health insurance solution or a substantially higher MBS rebate for NBCSP-indicated colonoscopy.

### Tension 4: DAC Is Effective but Not National

NSW's DAC program demonstrates that a redesigned, nurse-led pathway can reduce the specialist consultation barrier and accelerate colonoscopy access for positive screeners. But DAC is a state-funded model operating within NSW Health's public hospital network. It does not exist as a national program with dedicated federal funding, and its workforce model (trained endoscopist capacity for DAC-referred patients) still depends on the same constrained pool of endoscopists. Replicating DAC nationally would require both structural investment and a training pipeline for nurse endoscopists — a scope-of-practice reform that remains contested in gastroenterology.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Nurse endoscopists and extended scope: the contested solution to colonoscopy capacity.** Several countries, including the UK, have deployed nurse and non-medical endoscopists to substantially expand colonoscopy capacity. Australia's gastroenterology societies have historically opposed this model. The evidence on quality and safety, and the political economy of the scope-of-practice question in Australian endoscopy.

2. **The out-of-pocket cost trajectory for diagnostic imaging and procedures: colonoscopy as a case study in MBS indexation failure.** MBS schedule fees are indexed to the health MTAWE (male total average weekly earnings) — but their real value has eroded significantly against actual procedure costs since the 1990s. Colonoscopy is one of the most visible examples of this structural underpricing. A broader examination of MBS indexation and its consequences for out-of-pocket costs.

3. **Interval cancers in the NBCSP: what happens when colonoscopy misses a cancer?** A 2026 Medical Journal of Australia study examined interval cancer characteristics among NBCSP participants in Western Australia — cancers diagnosed between screens despite a prior negative test. Interval cancers present at more advanced stages and carry worse prognosis; their incidence is a quality metric for the colonoscopy workforce.

4. **The Victorian mental health levy model applied to cancer screening: could hypothecated funding solve NBCSP capacity constraints?** The Victoria-specific mental health levy (covered in Report 048) provides a precedent for hypothecated funding of health system capacity. Could a similar mechanism — a small levy or co-payment redirect — fund NBCSP endoscopy capacity expansion?

5. **First Nations bowel screening participation and the case for mobile endoscopy units.** First Nations Australians have lower NBCSP participation and lower colonoscopy follow-up rates than non-Indigenous Australians, despite higher CRC risk in some regions. Mobile colonoscopy units have been piloted in Queensland. The evidence on their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.
