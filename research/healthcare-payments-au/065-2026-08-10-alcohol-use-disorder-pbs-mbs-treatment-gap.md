# Alcohol Use Disorder: The PBS–MBS Funding Fault Line, and Why 97% of Australians with AUD Receive No Pharmacotherapy

**Report 065 — 2026-08-10**

**Summary:** Alcohol use disorder affects an estimated 800,000–1 million Australians, costs the economy $74.9 billion per year, and is responsible for 84,300 hospitalisations and 4.1% of total disease burden. Yet only 2.9% of people with AUD receive PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy — naltrexone or acamprosate — and no dedicated MBS item exists for brief alcohol intervention in primary care. The funding architecture is a mosaic of PBS medicines with streamlined authority, MBS items designed primarily for other conditions that AUD can access obliquely, Commonwealth Drug and Alcohol Program block grants to PHNs, and state-funded public treatment services facing a 115% surge in waitlists since 2020. The 18-year average delay from onset of AUD to treatment — cited in a landmark 2026 *Internal Medicine Journal* review — is not primarily a problem of patient motivation; it is a product of a payment system that provides almost no financial signal to GPs to identify, assess, or treat alcohol dependence, and that withholds three of the four licensed pharmacotherapies from public subsidy entirely.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 064 documented that semaglutide — a drug designed for diabetes — outperformed placebo for alcohol use disorder in a May 2026 *Lancet* RCT, generating enormous clinical interest in GLP-1 agonists as future AUD treatments. Before examining the policy pathway for novel addiction medicines, this report examines the existing therapeutic landscape: the two PBS-listed pharmacotherapies, two TGA-approved medicines without PBS listing, the MBS items GPs and specialists can use for AUD treatment, and the structural reasons the treatment gap remains so vast.

## Background

### Alcohol's Place in Australia's Drug Burden

Alcohol is not culturally framed as a drug in Australia, but epidemiologically it is the most damaging one. The AIHW's 2024 Australian Burden of Disease Study placed alcohol as the **sixth highest risk factor for total disease burden**, responsible for **4.1% of all DALYs** — comparable to high blood pressure and substantially above illicit drug use as an aggregate category. Among specific risk factors for males aged 15–44, alcohol is the single largest contributor to burden of disease.

Total disease burden from alcohol: **8.3 DALYs per 1,000 population** (age-standardised, 2024), down 13.5% from 9.6 in 2003 — a genuine improvement, but still enormous in scale. **Males bear 5.5%** of attributable burden; **females 2.6%** — a 2:1 ratio that reflects both consumption patterns and different biological dose-response curves.

The diseases in question span the full pathological landscape: **liver disease** (alcoholic hepatitis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma), **cancers** (oropharyngeal, oesophageal, colorectal, breast — no safe lower limit for cancer risk), **cardiovascular disease** (cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia), **neurological conditions** (Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, peripheral neuropathy), **pancreatitis**, and — most relevant to the payment system — **alcohol use disorder itself**, which carries a standalone age-standardised DALY rate of **2.9 per 1,000**, making it the **fourth leading cause of burden among all mental and substance use disorders** in Australia.

The 2024 *medrxiv* multistate life table analysis — now peer-reviewed — modelled a scenario in which the Australian adult population ceased all alcohol consumption. Over 25 years this would prevent **25.9 million disease and injury cases**, avert **211,697 deaths** (the majority from cancers: 114,948 deaths), and avoid substantial healthcare costs. This modelling provides the theoretical ceiling on alcohol's preventable burden; the practical ceiling is how effectively evidence-based treatment can be delivered to the 800,000+ Australians who already have AUD.

## Key Findings

### 1. AUD Prevalence and the Scale of the Treatment Gap

**Prevalence:** The AIHW's most recent systematic estimate places AUD at **4.4% of Australians aged 15 and over**. Applied to the current population aged 15+, this equates to approximately **800,000 to 1 million people** meeting diagnostic criteria for alcohol use disorder at any given time.

The National Drug Strategy Household Survey (NDSHS) 2022–23 provides a broader frame: **31% of Australians aged 14 and over** — 6.6 million people — consumed alcohol in ways that put their health at risk (more than 10 standard drinks per week, or more than 4 in a single day). Males (39%) drink riskily at substantially higher rates than females (23%). This 6.6 million figure captures hazardous drinkers who may not yet have AUD; the 800,000–1 million with AUD represents the subset with established physiological or psychological dependence.

**AODTS data (2024–25):** Australia's publicly funded Alcohol and Other Drug Treatment Services network delivered **244,400 treatment episodes to approximately 127,800 people** aged 10 and over in 2024–25. Alcohol was the most common principal drug of concern, accounting for **41% of treatment episodes (87,632 episodes)**. The treatment population has been falling: since 2019–20, the number of people accessing AOD treatment has declined **8.2% from 139,295 to 127,804**, with treatment rates falling from 623 to 527 per 100,000 people.

**The treatment gap:** Ritter and O'Reilly's 2025 landmark analysis in *Drug and Alcohol Review* estimated that between 752,812 and 1,291,119 Australians met criteria for a substance use disorder in 2023, and that only **30–48% of those requiring treatment** were receiving it. For alcohol specifically, the evidence is stark. A 2026 *Internal Medicine Journal* review by Palmer et al. — the most recent comprehensive Australian clinical synthesis — cited an average **18-year delay from onset of AUD to treatment**, and found that only **2.9% of Australians with AUD were receiving PBS-listed pharmacotherapy**.

This 97% untreated rate is not principally explained by patient reluctance or service unavailability. It reflects a payment system whose architecture contains almost no financial incentives to identify AUD in primary care, no dedicated MBS items for alcohol screening or brief intervention, and no PBS listing for two of the four TGA-approved pharmacotherapies.

### 2. PBS-Listed Pharmacotherapies for AUD

Australia has four TGA-approved pharmacotherapies for alcohol use disorder. Two are PBS-listed; two are not.

#### 2a. Naltrexone (oral) — PBS Item 8370M

**Drug:** Naltrexone hydrochloride 50 mg tablet. Multiple brands (Generic Health, Terry White, Alphapharm and others).

**Mechanism:** Mu-opioid receptor antagonist. Blocks the endogenous opioid release triggered by alcohol consumption, reducing the rewarding euphoric effect. Evidence: multiple RCTs; number needed to treat to prevent return to heavy drinking approximately 9–12.

**PBS listing:** Listed since 1999. Originally required written authority approval. In **May 2023**, the authority requirement was significantly reduced: naltrexone for alcohol dependence is now **Authority Required (STREAMLINED)** — prescribers endorse the prescription with the streamlined authority code rather than seeking prior approval. This was a meaningful access reform, reducing the administrative burden on GPs.

**PBS restriction:** *"Patient who is alcohol dependent as part of a comprehensive treatment program with a goal of abstinence."* The key restriction is the **abstinence goal** — naltrexone's PBS listing does not cover controlled-drinking approaches (see Section 4 on nalmefene).

**Prescribing authority:** Any medical practitioner or authorised nurse practitioner (following May 2023 reform). No specialist requirement.

**Co-payment:** Standard PBS co-payment — **$7.70 per prescription** (concessional, frozen to 2030); **$25.00 per prescription** (general, from 1 January 2026). A 28-day supply at 50 mg/day costs the patient the co-payment only. This makes naltrexone extremely affordable for patients who access it.

**Injectable naltrexone:** Extended-release injectable naltrexone (Vivitrol, Alkermes, 380 mg monthly injection) is **not TGA-approved in Australia** and is not PBS-listed. This is a meaningful gap relative to the United States, where Vivitrol is FDA-approved and widely used for patients with adherence issues or those preferring monthly dosing. Australian patients cannot access injectable naltrexone through the PBS or standard clinical pathways.

#### 2b. Acamprosate — PBS Item 8357W

**Drug:** Acamprosate calcium 333 mg enteric coated tablet (2 tablets, three times daily = 6 tablets/day, ~1,998 mg/day).

**Mechanism:** Modulates glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission during alcohol withdrawal and abstinence, reducing the hyperexcitability-driven cravings that precipitate relapse. Most effective after detoxification when started early in abstinence. Does not block alcohol's rewarding effect.

**PBS listing:** **Authority Required (STREAMLINED)** — same authority level as naltrexone following recent reform.

**PBS restriction:** *"Patient who is alcohol dependent as part of a comprehensive treatment program with a goal of abstinence."* Same abstinence-goal requirement as naltrexone.

**Prescribing authority:** Any medical practitioner or nurse practitioner. No specialist requirement.

**Co-payment:** Standard PBS rates — $7.70 concessional; $25.00 general per prescription. Given the 2-tablet, three-times-daily dosing, compliance is a practical challenge, and the dispensing volume (each prescription typically covers 28 days) is higher per patient than naltrexone.

**Dispensing trends:** Acamprosate dispensing has been **essentially flat from 2006 to 2023** in PBS data, in contrast to naltrexone, which more than doubled over the same period. This likely reflects the more complex dosing schedule, the shift toward naltrexone as the first-line agent in Australian clinical guidelines, and some evidence that naltrexone has a somewhat broader evidence base.

#### 2c. Nalmefene — TGA-Approved, NOT PBS-Listed

**Drug:** Nalmefene 18 mg tablet (Selincro, Lundbeck).

**TGA status:** TGA-approved for "reduction of alcohol consumption in adult patients with alcohol dependence who have a high drinking risk level without physical withdrawal symptoms and who do not require immediate detoxification." The distinctive feature is that it is approved for **as-needed dosing** — patients take it when they anticipate drinking, targeting reduction in heavy drinking days rather than abstinence.

**PBAC history:** Nalmefene was reviewed by the PBAC at the **November 2015 meeting**. The PBAC did not recommend listing. The key issues identified were: (a) questions about the applicability of the controlled-drinking target population to Australian clinical practice and PBS restriction frameworks which default to abstinence; (b) uncertainty about comparative clinical benefit versus naltrexone for the overall AUD population; (c) the clinical model of "as-needed" dosing being inconsistent with the PBS restriction framework for both existing AUD pharmacotherapies. Nalmefene has not been resubmitted to the PBAC as at mid-2026.

**PBS status:** **Not listed.** Patients who access nalmefene in Australia pay full private cost. As nalmefene is not marketed in Australia at commercial scale (the clinical model never transitioned to PBS subsidy after PBAC rejection), access is effectively not available through routine prescribing pathways.

**Clinical significance of the gap:** The controlled-drinking model that nalmefene supports reflects both a change in the evidence base (controlled drinking can be a legitimate treatment goal for many patients with AUD) and the clinical reality that abstinence-only treatment goals are a significant barrier to treatment engagement. Many people with AUD will not engage with treatment that requires them to commit to permanent abstinence before starting. The absence of a PBS-subsidised controlled-drinking pharmacotherapy option means GPs cannot offer a PBS-subsidised medicine for patients whose treatment goal is reduction rather than abstinence — a population that international guidelines now recognise as substantial.

#### 2d. Disulfiram — TGA-Registered, NOT PBS-Listed

**Drug:** Disulfiram 200 mg tablet (Antabuse, ARTG 70468, Arrotex Pharmaceuticals). Available as prescription-only.

**Mechanism:** Inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, causing highly unpleasant acetaldehyde accumulation (flushing, nausea, vomiting, hypotension) if alcohol is consumed while taking the drug. Functions as an aversive deterrent rather than reducing craving.

**PBS status:** **Not listed.** Disulfiram has never been listed on the PBS. A 2011 *Medical Journal of Australia* commentary examined why: the fundamental issue is that **no pharmaceutical company sponsor has a commercial incentive to fund a PBAC application** for disulfiram. The drug is old, cheap, off-patent, and generic. The cost of preparing a quality-use submission to PBAC — clinical data dossier, health economic model, supporting documentation — is substantial. The potential commercial return from a PBS listing that drives volume at the co-payment level (essentially no margin) is negative relative to the application cost. Disulfiram sits in a market failure trap common to repurposed and off-patent medicines: it is TGA-registered, clinically used, and guideline-recommended in specific circumstances (supervised administration), but not PBS-subsidised.

**Private cost:** Approximately **$80–90 per month** out-of-pocket for patients who access disulfiram privately — not enormous, but a recurring cost with no safety net coverage.

**Clinical role:** Australian guidelines no longer position disulfiram as first-line (due to adherence challenges, toxicity risk, and the emergence of better-evidenced alternatives), but it remains useful in specific circumstances — particularly when supervised administration (by a partner, family member, or clinician) can be arranged, which addresses the principal adherence problem. The absence of PBS listing means that even this second-line option carries an out-of-pocket cost for patients, creating a perverse incentive structure where the cheapest medicine is the one without subsidy.

### 3. MBS Items for AUD Assessment and Treatment

The MBS contains no dedicated item for alcohol use disorder screening, brief intervention, or AUD-specific management. AUD is managed in primary care through a patchwork of items designed primarily for other purposes.

#### 3a. Standard Attendance Items (Time-Based Consultation)

The most common MBS pathway for AUD in general practice is the standard time-based attendance:

- **Item 3** (Level A, ≤5 min): $19.65 rebate. Rarely sufficient for AUD assessment.
- **Item 23** (Level B, 6–20 min): $43.90 rebate (from 1 July 2025). This is the standard GP consultation item used most frequently for AUD-related GP visits. A 20-minute consultation is marginally sufficient for screening and brief advice; inadequate for comprehensive assessment or pharmacotherapy initiation.
- **Item 36** (Level C, 20–40 min): $75.10 rebate. More appropriate for initial AUD assessment, treatment planning, or pharmacotherapy initiation. Under-utilised relative to clinical need because bulk-billing economics favour Item 23.
- **Item 44** (Level D, >40 min): $110.70 rebate. Appropriate for complex AUD cases with comorbidities. Rarely claimed for AUD in bulk-billing practice.

**Critical structural gap:** There is **no dedicated MBS item for alcohol screening (AUDIT or AUDIT-C)** or brief alcohol intervention. In many comparable health systems — the UK NHS, for example — brief intervention services attract separate payment. In Australia, if a GP spends 10 minutes screening a patient for hazardous drinking, providing brief advice, and arranging follow-up, this is subsumed into the Level B item at $43.90. There is no financial signal to GPs to routinely screen for AUD beyond what is captured in general consultations.

The **AUDIT-10 and AUDIT-C tools** are clinically recommended as standard screening instruments for AUD in primary care, with strong sensitivity and specificity and strong evidence supporting GP-administered brief intervention. Neither generates its own MBS billing; both must be embedded in existing consultation time.

#### 3b. Better Access Mental Health Pathway

Alcohol use disorder qualifies for the **Better Access to Mental Health Care initiative** under the relevant MBS items. The relevant ICD-10 primary care diagnostic categories include **alcohol use disorders explicitly** — they do not require a co-existing psychiatric diagnosis.

This pathway opens two MBS streams for AUD patients:

**GP Mental Health Treatment Plan (MHTP):**
- Preparation of MHTP (≥20 min, relevant to AUD): Part of the general professional attendance framework from November 2025 — GPs can now use time-tiered professional attendance items to prepare, review, and provide ongoing mental health consultation for patients including those with AUD.
- Item 2713 (GP Mental Health Treatment Consultation, ≥20 min): rebate $73.95 (100% of schedule fee).
- Item 2715 (for GPs with Mental Health Skills Training, 20–40 min): $93.90 rebate.

The MHTP enables referral for **up to 10 individual psychology sessions per calendar year** under Better Access — sessions that can be used for AUD-focused cognitive-behavioural therapy, motivational enhancement therapy, or other evidence-based psychological treatments. Psychology rebates: $145.25 (clinical psychologist) to $98.95 (general psychologist) per session. Gap fees apply ($172 and $219 respectively, using APS recommended fees).

**Significant caveat:** The Better Access pathway was designed for mental health conditions and carries the same structural limitations for AUD as for other conditions (see Report 011 for the session cap analysis). The 10-session cap represents inadequate coverage for AUD, where treatment durations of 3–6 months or longer are standard in guidelines, and where the disease is characterised by relapse and requires ongoing support.

#### 3c. GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (GPCCMP)

From 1 July 2025, the GPCCMP system (replacing GPMP Item 721 and TCA Item 723) introduced:
- **Item 965** (preparation of GPCCMP): rebate $156.55
- **Item 967** (review of GPCCMP, every 3 months): rebate $156.55

AUD is **eligible for a GPCCMP** as a chronic condition. There is no exclusion list — eligibility is determined by the GP's clinical judgment that the condition has been present (or is likely to be present) for at least 6 months. AUD clearly meets this criterion for patients with established dependence.

The GPCCMP enables access to **up to 5 individual allied health services per calendar year** (expandable for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients to 10 under Item 715). For AUD patients, this could include:
- **Dietitian** (for nutritional rehabilitation — Wernicke's, nutritional deficiency)
- **Psychologist** (CBT, motivational interviewing — though this overlaps with Better Access)
- **Social worker** (complex social circumstances driving drinking)
- **Occupational therapist** (cognitive rehabilitation)

In practice, the GPCCMP pathway is used inconsistently for AUD, partly because GPs are unaware of AUD's eligibility, partly because the allied health referral network for AUD-specific services is thin relative to more common chronic disease conditions (diabetes, heart disease) where GPCCMP use is well-established.

#### 3d. Mental Health Treatment Plan Under MyMedicare

From November 2025, the MHTP framework was linked to **MyMedicare** registration. For AUD patients, the MHTP must now be prepared by the patient's usual medical practitioner or a GP at their MyMedicare-registered practice. Telehealth MHTP access is restricted to the patient's MyMedicare-registered GP.

Given that only **~10% of Australians** are MyMedicare-enrolled (see Report 010), the majority of AUD patients accessing Better Access psychology services are not affected by this linkage in 2026. But as enrolment grows, disruption to existing clinical relationships — patients with established AUD treatment plans who change practices — becomes a practical concern.

#### 3e. Specialist Items: Psychiatry and Addiction Medicine

**Psychiatry (MBS Item 291/92435):** A GP can refer a patient with AUD to a psychiatrist for an assessment and development of a management plan. Item 291 (outpatient assessment, ≥45 min): rebate **$365.75**. Item 293: psychiatrist ongoing management. Psychiatry gap fees are among the highest of any specialty: private psychiatry first appointments commonly cost $500–$1,000 out-of-pocket, with a rebate covering only $365.75 of that cost.

**Critical point:** Psychiatry is not the primary clinical pathway for AUD management in Australia — most AUD care is and should be delivered in primary care. Psychiatric referral is appropriate for complex co-occurring mental illness, AUD with severe psychopathology, or cases beyond GP management capacity. The psychiatry workforce shortage (meeting only 56% of national demand, see Report 028) means psychiatric referral pathways are difficult to access even when appropriate.

**Addiction Medicine:** Addiction medicine became a recognised specialty in Australia through the Chapter of Addiction Medicine (ChAM) within RACGP/RACP. **Fellow of the Australasian Chapter of Addiction Medicine (FAChAM)** physicians are the specialist cohort with specific AUD expertise. However, the MBS does not have a separate specialty classification for addiction medicine as distinct from general practice or internal medicine. Addiction medicine specialists bill using items appropriate to their primary qualification — those who are also GPs bill GP items; those with physician training may bill specialist attendances under internal medicine or psychiatry depending on their dual qualifications.

The practical consequence is that addiction medicine specialist services are **invisible in MBS claims data** as a distinct specialty, making it impossible to track the volume of specialist AUD treatment being delivered through the MBS. This is a significant accountability and planning gap.

#### 3f. MBS Item 715 — Aboriginal Health Assessment

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, **MBS Item 715** (Annual Health Assessment) is a significant AUD-relevant item. The AUDIT-C is a **national key performance indicator** for the Item 715 assessment. This item:
- Can be claimed every 9 months (no restriction by presenting complaint)
- Is billable at no cost to the patient
- Triggers up to **10 allied health sessions per year** (double the GPCCMP baseline)
- Generates data feeding into Closing the Gap tracking

The paradox is that First Nations Australians — who bear disproportionately high rates of AUD-related harm, particularly in remote and rural communities — have the weakest access to the pharmacotherapy that follows a positive AUDIT-C screen. Research published in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Purcell-Khodr et al., 2023) found that across **22 Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services**, only **0.2% of patients** received alcohol pharmacotherapy prescriptions. Of those identified as "likely dependent" (AUDIT-C ≥ 9), only **3.4%** received a prescription for naltrexone or acamprosate. This is a treatment rate approximately 12 times lower than the already-low national average.

### 4. PBS Dispensing Volumes: The 2.9% Problem

The most current comprehensive dispensing analysis — Morley et al. (2024), published in *Alcohol and Alcoholism* — examined 17 years of PBS claims data from 2006 to 2023.

**Scale:**
- **22,745 individuals** received at least one PBS prescription for alcohol pharmacotherapy over the entire 2006–2023 period
- **117,548 total prescriptions** dispensed: 45.3% naltrexone, 43.0% acamprosate, 11.7% concurrent dispensing of both
- **Dispensing rate: 1,354 per 100,000** individuals — a modest absolute figure in the context of 800,000+ people with AUD
- The effective treatment rate: **2.9% of Australians with AUD** receiving PBS pharmacotherapy

**Temporal trends:**
- Naltrexone dispensing **more than doubled** between 2006 and 2023 — the streamlined authority reform in May 2023 will have contributed to this trajectory
- Acamprosate dispensing was **essentially flat** over the same period
- The demographic pattern: 60% male, 56% aged 35–54

**Treatment duration — a critical gap:**
- Only **5.1% of individuals** dispensed alcohol pharmacotherapy received treatment for the minimum recommended duration of **3 months**
- Current Australian guidelines recommend a minimum 3-month pharmacotherapy course; most clinical evidence supports 6–12 months for naltrexone
- The median treatment duration is far below this threshold
- This suggests that even within the small proportion accessing pharmacotherapy, treatment discontinuation is the norm rather than the exception

**Geographic variation:** Access to pharmacotherapy is significantly lower in areas that are socioeconomically disadvantaged or geographically remote. Urban, higher-income, middle-aged males are the patients most likely to receive naltrexone or acamprosate — the inverse of many demographic patterns in alcohol harm burden, where younger people, First Nations communities, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups bear disproportionate disease burden.

### 5. The Treatment Gap: Structural Causes

The 97% untreated rate and 18-year average delay are not explained by a single failure. They reflect multiple reinforcing structural problems:

#### 5a. Stigma and the Medical Framing of AUD

Alcohol use disorder is under-recognised as a medical condition by both GPs and patients. In a 2025 survey context, many GPs reported limited confidence in initiating pharmacotherapy for AUD — a pattern replicated in multiple international studies. Barriers include:
- **Time constraints**: A Level B consultation ($43.90, 6–20 min) is inadequate for the psychosocial complexity of AUD assessment. GPs operating in bulk-billing practices under BBPIP have limited ability to extend consultation time.
- **Therapeutic pessimism**: Evidence consistently shows that GPs systematically underestimate the effectiveness of naltrexone and acamprosate relative to what clinical trial evidence demonstrates.
- **Prescribing complexity**: Until May 2023, naltrexone required written authority approval rather than streamlined code endorsement. This administrative barrier materially suppressed prescribing, particularly in general practice where authority prescribing imposes meaningful time and compliance costs.
- **Abstinence-only framing**: The PBS restriction for both listed pharmacotherapies — "part of a comprehensive treatment plan with a goal of abstinence" — encodes an abstinence-only clinical model that many patients cannot commit to. Patients who want harm reduction or controlled drinking as a treatment goal have no PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy option (nalmefene not listed; naltrexone technically restricted to abstinence-goal patients).

#### 5b. No MBS Incentive for Screening

Brief alcohol intervention in general practice is one of the most cost-effective health interventions known: a Cochrane review estimated that brief intervention (5–15 minutes) reduces weekly alcohol consumption by approximately 38 grams in hazardous drinkers. The NNT to reduce one person from harmful to moderate consumption is approximately 8 — comparable to statins for cardiovascular prevention.

Yet unlike cardiovascular risk screening (which has dedicated MBS items for lipid testing, blood pressure measurement, and risk assessment), there is **no MBS item for alcohol screening or brief intervention** in Australia. Every minute a GP spends administering the AUDIT-C and providing brief advice is extracted from within an existing consultation rebate — typically $43.90 for a Level B attendance.

The contrast with chronic disease screening is stark. A GP who identifies a patient's elevated cholesterol can bill Item 23 for the consultation plus a separate pathology item for the lipid test, plus a Health Assessment item if indicated — an integrated billing pathway with multiple supporting items. A GP who identifies harmful drinking can bill Item 23 and nothing else. There is no parallel pathway; no screening item; no brief intervention item; no recall item for follow-up.

#### 5c. Gaps in the Specialist Pathway

**Addiction medicine as an invisible specialty:** As noted, MBS data cannot distinguish addiction medicine consultations from other specialist attendances. The practical consequence is that clinical capacity in this subspecialty is invisible to workforce planning, and advocacy for dedicated addiction medicine MBS items is harder to mount without claims-based evidence of service volumes.

**Psychiatry cost barrier:** For complex AUD with psychiatric comorbidities, the psychiatry referral pathway carries gap fees of $134–$634+ (difference between MBS rebate $365.75 and private fee of $500–$1,000). These fees are among the highest in any medical specialty (see Report 016) and represent a material access barrier for AUD patients, who are disproportionately represented in lower socioeconomic groups.

#### 5d. Non-PBS Pharmacotherapies: The Three Unlistings

Three TGA-approved or TGA-registered AUD pharmacotherapies are not PBS-listed:
1. **Disulfiram** — market failure: off-patent, no sponsor incentive to apply to PBAC
2. **Nalmefene** — PBAC rejected 2015; controlled-drinking model inconsistent with PBS abstinence-only framework; not resubmitted
3. **Injectable naltrexone (Vivitrol)** — not TGA-approved in Australia at all

Each unlisting or non-approval reflects a different failure mode. Disulfiram is a market incentive problem. Nalmefene is a policy frameworks problem (abstinence vs harm reduction). Injectable naltrexone is a regulatory gap. Together they mean that the AUD pharmacotherapy toolkit available to Australian patients through the PBS is narrower than in comparable health systems.

### 6. Funding Architecture: Commonwealth vs State Responsibilities

AUD treatment in Australia is funded through at least four separate mechanisms, with no single accountable funder for the treatment pathway.

#### 6a. Commonwealth: Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP)

The Commonwealth's primary AOD treatment funding vehicle is the **Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP)**. Through the **2025–26 MYEFO**, the government consolidated DAP terminating and ongoing funding into a single ongoing program. From **2026–27: $727 million over three years**, followed by **$244.2 million annually from 2029–30**. Current DAP grants (due to expire June 2026) were extended one year to **June 2027** to allow a consultation process on next round design; new three-year grant agreements will commence **1 July 2027**.

DAP funding flows primarily through **Primary Health Networks (PHNs)**, which commission local AOD services based on assessed need. PHNs exercise discretion over which services to fund — counselling, withdrawal management, residential rehabilitation, pharmacotherapy support — creating geographic variation in what is available in different PHN regions. Approximately **30% of PHN AOD funding** is earmarked for First Nations-specific treatment services.

The DAP consultation process (currently underway) is examining several structural issues including: the appropriate split between PHN block grants and fee-for-service MBS pathways; how to integrate DAP-funded services with primary care; and how to ensure continuity of service during grant transitions (a persistent problem given the three-year grant cycle and high provider vulnerability).

#### 6b. States and Territories: The Bulk of Public Treatment Capacity

States and territories fund the majority of **publicly accessible AUD treatment infrastructure**: public detoxification services, withdrawal management units, public residential rehabilitation programs, community-based drug and alcohol services (CDAS), and hospital alcohol liaison teams. These services are free at the point of care for residents.

**Public treatment access is the dominant bottleneck.** Victoria's AOD sector demand survey (VAADA 8th Annual Survey, data from May 2026) found **5,122 people on the daily AOD treatment waitlist** — a **115% increase** from 2,385 in September 2020. Approximately **40% of people seeking treatment were waiting to be assessed** — not waiting for a treatment bed, but waiting for the initial clinical assessment. VAADA is calling for an additional **$70 million per year** from the Victorian Government to reduce wait times.

The state–Commonwealth split creates a structural funding gap: the Commonwealth controls the PBS (pharmacotherapy subsidy) and MBS (GP/specialist consultations), while states control the public treatment infrastructure (detox, rehab). A patient who presents to a GP for AUD treatment may be able to access PBS-subsidised naltrexone immediately, but if they need supervised detoxification first — which is clinically required for dependent patients before starting naltrexone (to avoid precipitating withdrawal) — they enter a state-funded queue.

**Private residential rehabilitation** provides an alternative for those who can afford it. Costs for 28-day residential AUD rehabilitation range from **$12,000–$60,000** depending on facility tier and location. Private health insurance hospital cover may contribute if the patient is admitted under a psychiatrist in a psychiatric facility — but PHI does not cover most private rehabilitation programs that are not structured as hospital admissions.

#### 6c. PBS: Pharmacotherapy Subsidy

The PBS provides the pharmacotherapy subsidy for naltrexone (8370M) and acamprosate (8357W). The total PBS expenditure on alcohol pharmacotherapies is not separately published in standard PBS reporting, but given the dispensing volumes identified in the 2006–2023 analysis (117,548 prescriptions over 17 years = approximately 6,900 prescriptions per year on average, rising to higher levels in recent years), the PBS alcohol pharmacotherapy spend is relatively modest compared with the overall $19.1 billion PBS budget — representing perhaps $3–5 million per year at co-payment equivalents, a negligible fraction of the total.

This modest PBS expenditure on AUD pharmacotherapy reflects the treatment gap rather than treatment effectiveness. If 2.9% are being treated, the PBS spend on AUD is 2.9% of what it would be if pharmacotherapy were prescribed at the rate clinical need would suggest.

#### 6d. MBS: Fee-for-Service Consultations

The MBS provides rebates for GP consultations, psychiatric consultations, and allied health sessions for AUD patients — but without dedicated AUD-specific items, claims for AUD-related care are indistinguishable from claims for other conditions in standard MBS reporting. There is no MBS-based mechanism to track the volume or quality of AUD assessment and treatment in primary care. This accountability gap makes it impossible to measure progress against the treatment gap through MBS data.

### 7. Economic Burden: Costs That Dwarf the Treatment Investment

The contrast between the scale of alcohol-related economic harm and the investment in AUD treatment is among the starkest in Australian health policy.

**Total social and economic cost of alcohol:** The George Institute's updated cost analysis estimated:
- **$72.9 billion** in 2020–21
- Projected **$74.9 billion** in 2022–23

This estimate encompasses tangible costs (healthcare, crime, workplace, road crashes) and intangible costs (premature mortality, lost quality of life for those with dependence). The tangible healthcare cost component alone was **$2.8 billion** in the 2017–18 data year — the most recent year for which a detailed breakdown has been published.

**Hospitalisations:** In 2023–24, alcohol was responsible for **84,300 hospitalisations** — accounting for **58% of all drug-related hospitalisations** in Australia. Key features:
- 59% male; 60% aged 45 and over
- Over half (52%) required at least one overnight stay
- Alcohol-related hospitalisations span: mental and behavioural disorders due to alcohol use, liver disease, pancreatitis, trauma, cardiac arrhythmia, and cancer
- An estimated $2–3 billion in annual public hospital costs attributable to alcohol

**AIHW Burden of Disease Study 2024:**
- Alcohol: **6th highest risk factor** for disease burden in Australia
- **4.1% of total DALY burden** — significant but down from prior years, suggesting some effectiveness of population-level interventions (drink-driving enforcement, responsible service frameworks)
- Age-standardised burden: **8.3 DALY per 1,000** (down 13.5% from 9.6 in 2003)

**Avoidable healthcare costs:** The medrxiv 2024 multistate life table modelling estimated that eliminating all alcohol consumption from the Australian adult population over 25 years would:
- Prevent 25.9 million disease and injury cases
- Avert 211,697 deaths (majority cancer-related: 114,948 deaths)
- Substantially reduce healthcare expenditure across cancer, liver disease, cardiovascular disease, and injury

Placed against this background, the current AUD treatment investment — roughly $244 million/year in DAP funding, plus PBS pharmacotherapy costs (a few million), plus MBS consultations (uncaptured) — is materially insufficient relative to the economic harm being generated.

### 8. Policy Context

#### 8a. NHMRC 2020 Alcohol Guidelines

In February 2020, the National Health and Medical Research Council released the **Australian Guidelines to Reduce Health Risks from Drinking Alcohol** (first revision since 2009). The revised guidelines tightened the recommended limits:
- **No more than 10 standard drinks per week** (down from 14 in 2009 guidelines)
- **No more than 4 standard drinks on any one day**
- For under-18s and pregnant women: no safe level of drinking

By these guidelines, **31% of Australian adults** (6.6 million people) currently drink in excess of the low-risk threshold. The guidelines were developed by an independent committee over four years, drawing on evidence that even moderate drinking increases cancer risk (particularly breast cancer and colorectal cancer), and that the previously cited cardioprotective effects of moderate alcohol were largely artefacts of methodological confounders ("sick quitter" bias).

The guidelines have clinical payment implications: the revised thresholds mean that a larger proportion of the population drinking at formerly "socially acceptable" levels now technically meets the criteria for hazardous drinking that triggers brief intervention recommendations. With no dedicated MBS brief intervention item, this expanded clinical target population generates no additional payment signal to GPs.

#### 8b. National Alcohol Strategy 2019–2028

The National Alcohol Strategy 2019–2028, agreed by all Health Ministers, set a **10% reduction in harmful alcohol consumption by 2025** as its headline target. This target was not achieved. The Strategy noted in its framework that "there has been no demonstration of progress being made" — reflecting the absence of a national monitoring mechanism for strategy implementation.

The Strategy's implementation failures reflect its structure: it assigns responsibility for funding and program implementation to individual jurisdictions, with no binding Commonwealth accountability mechanism and no dedicated funding stream attached to the strategy itself. The strategy's priority areas (preventing and reducing harms in the community, health system responses, treatment and recovery services) have not translated into funded programs matched to their stated objectives.

#### 8c. National Drug Strategy 2017–2026

The National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 provides the overarching framework covering alcohol, tobacco, and other drugs under three pillars (supply reduction, demand reduction, harm reduction). The strategy expires **end 2026**, with a final report due in 2027. No formal successor strategy has been announced as at mid-2026.

This creates a strategic uncertainty for the AOD sector. The DAP consultation process (currently underway for the post-July 2027 grant round) is happening without a clear overarching policy framework to anchor it. The AADC (Australian Alcohol and other Drugs Council, representing 550+ AOD services) has called for a "funding overhaul" ahead of the new grant agreements, citing the mismatch between growing treatment demand and static or declining service capacity.

## What the Data Shows

The structural failures in AUD treatment funding in Australia can be summarised as:

1. **The PBS pharmacotherapy gap:** Only 2 of 4 licensed AUD pharmacotherapies are PBS-listed. Of the two listed medicines, the co-payments are genuinely low, but the streamlined authority requirement still poses some prescribing complexity. The abstinence-only PBS restriction excludes the substantial population of patients for whom controlled-drinking is a treatment goal. Only 2.9% of those with AUD access PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy — a treatment rate that would be considered a systemic failure in any other chronic disease context.

2. **No MBS brief intervention item:** The most cost-effective primary care intervention for hazardous drinking — brief advice by a GP — generates no dedicated Medicare rebate. GPs have no financial incentive to screen routinely; screening displaces consultation time for other billable services without any additional compensation.

3. **The 18-year delay:** Average time from AUD onset to treatment, cited by Palmer et al. in the 2026 *Internal Medicine Journal* review, reflects decades of insufficient primary care investment in AUD identification and management. A health system that provides no specific payment incentive for AUD screening will, predictably, produce late and low rates of treatment initiation.

4. **Waitlists doubling:** Victoria's AOD treatment waitlist has grown 115% since September 2020, reaching 5,122 people in May 2026. The state-funded public treatment system is the primary gateway to detoxification and residential rehabilitation — and it is under severe capacity strain across Australia.

5. **First Nations pharmacotherapy gap:** Despite AUDIT-C being embedded in MBS Item 715 and generating a documented population of patients with likely alcohol dependence, only 3.4% of likely-dependent First Nations patients in community-controlled settings received pharmacotherapy — a treatment failure occurring inside the very infrastructure designed to detect AUD.

6. **Absent addiction medicine visibility:** The absence of a distinct MBS specialty classification for addiction medicine means clinical capacity in this subspecialty is invisible in claims data, hampering service planning and limiting advocacy for dedicated MBS items.

## Threads Opened by This Report

1. **GLP-1 agonists for AUD and the PBS pathway** — Report 064 documented the May 2026 *Lancet* RCT showing semaglutide's efficacy for AUD. What would a TGA application and PBAC submission pathway for a GLP-1 AUD indication look like? What is the clinical model (abstinence or reduction)? What are the access equity implications given PBS listing only for type 2 diabetes?

2. **First Nations AOD and ACCHO pharmacotherapy access** — Report 013 documented ACCHOs' 3.6 million annual episodes of care; this report documents the 3.4% pharmacotherapy rate in likely-dependent ACCHO patients. The gap between detection (AUDIT-C as Item 715 KPI) and treatment (naltrexone/acamprosate prescribing) deserves dedicated examination.

3. **National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — The NDS expires end 2026; DAP consultation is underway for post-July 2027 grants. What should a successor strategy commit to on AUD treatment? What would a funded, accountable national plan for reducing the treatment gap look like?

4. **Brief intervention MBS item case** — Several countries (UK, Canada, some US states under Medicaid) have created dedicated payment mechanisms for alcohol brief intervention in primary care. What would an Australian equivalent look like, what is the cost-effectiveness evidence, and what is the policy pathway?

5. **Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) and prevention funding** — FASD is the leading preventable cause of neurodevelopmental disability in Australia, with a prevalence estimated at 2–5% in some populations; it is substantially under-detected and under-resourced. Its relationship to AUD in the parent generation and the treatment funding system warrants examination.
