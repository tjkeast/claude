# PHI Reform for Private Maternity and Newborns: The 2026 Consultation Paper, What the Sector Is Asking For, and What Might Actually Change

**Summary:** The federal government's first Private Health Sector Reform Consultation Paper (2 July 2026) puts maternity care front and centre among the acute viability pressures requiring intervention. Four reform levers are on the table — shorter pregnancy waiting periods, expanding maternity tier coverage below Gold, prospective risk equalisation incorporating maternity costs, and bundled payment models — but 100 submissions reveal deep disagreement about which levers will work and which will simply redistribute costs without fixing the structural underpayment driving the closure cascade. As of October 2026, the government has yet to publish its response; the 2027 premium round applications close in November 2026, making the next six weeks decisive.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 134 documented the paediatric and neonatal workforce gap as the third structural pillar of private maternity's collapse (alongside obstetric and anaesthesia shortages), and identified the July–August 2026 PHI reform consultation as the government's primary policy vehicle for addressing the whole crisis — this report examines what that consultation actually contains, what stakeholders are demanding, and whether any of it reaches the structural causes documented in the preceding ten reports in this series.

---

## Background: The Crisis That Forced a Consultation

The 2026 consultation did not emerge from a calm policy review. It was forced by an accelerating structural crisis whose key metrics have been documented across this series:

- **18 private maternity unit closures since 2018**, with 13 in the three years to October 2026 alone (Catholic Health Australia, September 2025)
- **22% fall in private births** from 2012 to 2023 (MJA, 2024)
- **~360,000 Gold-tier PHI policy downgrades** since 2020, driven by Gold premiums rising approximately 71% over six years (CHOICE)
- **$756 million sector loss** for the private hospital sector in 2024–25, against insurer profits of **$2.7 billion** and insurer management expenses of **$3.4 billion** in the same period (Catholic Health Australia pre-budget submission, May 2026)

Against this backdrop, Minister for Health and Aged Care Mark Butler tasked the Private Health CEO Forum — a government-industry body that had been meeting since late 2025 — with advising on five areas: (1) making maternity care more accessible and affordable; (2) improving access to mental health care; (3) expanding Hospital in the Home; (4) making contract negotiations fairer; and (5) reducing regulatory red tape. The Forum's advice fed directly into the consultation paper released on 2 July 2026.

A legislative companion — the **Health Legislation Amendment (Improving Choice and Transparency for Private Health Consumers) Bill 2026** — passed both houses and received Royal Assent on **18 September 2026**, covering improved informed financial consent and split billing arrangements, and modernising specialist referral pathways. The more contested structural reforms, however, depend on regulatory changes still under consultation.

*(Sources: DoHDA Consultation Paper 1, 2 July 2026; CHA Pre-Budget Submission May 2026; CEO Forum Terms of Reference September 2026)*

## What the Consultation Paper Proposes for Maternity

Consultation Paper 1 was structured around four maternity-specific reform initiatives, each framed as a question rather than a concluded policy, with the department explicitly noting that "these proposals do not represent government policy."

### Initiative 1: Shorter Pregnancy and Birth Waiting Periods

The current statutory maximum waiting period for pregnancy and birth services is **12 months** — the same as for pre-existing conditions. The paper asks whether this maximum should be reduced, and if so, to what level.

The 12-month rule has two practical effects: it prevents people from joining cover solely when pregnant and immediately claiming, but it also locks out the approximately 40% of Australian women who have an unplanned pregnancy without adequate PHI cover at the time of conception. The AMA submission to the consultation (September 2026) specifically called for "greater flexibility for women who unexpectedly become pregnant to access appropriate private health insurance cover" — a direct consequence of the 12-month rule.

The industry response was sharply divided. **Private Healthcare Australia (PHA)**, the insurer peak body, argued that reducing waiting periods would incentivise gaming — consumers joining cover when pregnant and downgrading or exiting immediately after birth — thereby increasing costs across the broader insured population and raising premiums for long-term, particularly older, policyholders. Members Health Fund Alliance took a similar position, raising "moral hazard" concerns about consumers treating PHI as a short-term transactional product rather than a long-term pooled insurance arrangement.

The **Australian Private Hospitals Association (APHA)**, the hospital peak body, backed a reduction to six to nine months, arguing that 18 maternity unit closures demonstrated that the 12-month rule was not solving the access problem it was designed to address.

*(Sources: AMA submission September 2026; PHA submission 13 August 2026; APHA statement on consultation)*

### Initiative 2: Expanding Maternity Coverage Below Gold Tier

Currently, pregnancy and birth hospital cover sits primarily in Gold products, with some Silver Plus policies optionally including it. The paper asks whether maternity cover should be mandated at a lower tier level.

This question goes to the heart of the PHI tier architecture that the Morrison government introduced in 2018. The four-tier framework (Basic, Bronze, Silver, Gold) was designed to create clear expectations; Gold requires a mandated minimum set of clinical categories including pregnancy and birth. Requiring maternity coverage in Silver would lower the entry price for maternity access but would spread costs across a significantly larger insured base — Silver is the most popular tier for younger adults.

PHA modelled the premium impact and shared the figures in its August 2026 submission: moving pregnancy and birth into Silver products would increase Silver premiums by approximately **3.5%** and Bronze premiums by approximately **13.3%** (under the scenario where Bronze is also included). PHA opposed both changes on affordability grounds.

The **Actuaries Institute** took a more nuanced position, supporting broader risk sharing for maternity costs in principle but calling for detailed modelling of participation effects before implementation — noting that premium increases in entry-level products could reduce PHI take-up among young, healthy adults, worsening risk pool dynamics over time.

*(Sources: PHA submission 13 August 2026; Actuaries Institute submission; DoHDA Consultation Paper 1)*

### Initiative 3: Prospective Risk Equalisation for Maternity and Mental Health

Risk equalisation is the mechanism by which all registered health funds pool the cost of insuring high-need populations. Currently, it operates **retrospectively** (APRA calculates and distributes quarterly adjustments after claims are incurred). This means funds that attract younger or healthier members benefit more than those with older or sicker memberships.

The consultation proposes moving to a **prospective** risk equalisation model that would explicitly incorporate maternity and mental health costs — meaning funds receive risk equalisation payments before providing coverage, and maternity-specific costs are redistributed across the whole fund sector regardless of which fund an individual is insured with.

This was the most widely supported proposal across submissions. The **Actuaries Institute** backed it as a way to "widen the effective risk pool" and reduce the concentration of maternity risk in a subset of funds. **Members Health Fund Alliance** supported the direction. **PHA** backed prospective risk equalisation including maternity and mental health as its first structural recommendation.

The mechanism's appeal is straightforward: if maternity costs are equalised across all funds, no individual fund has an incentive to deter or exclude pregnant members (as currently occurs through product design), and the cost of maternity coverage becomes more predictable and therefore more accurately priced. This is the reform most favoured by actuarial and economic analysis.

Its limitation, raised by APHA and CHA, is that it does not address the **underpayment of hospitals by insurers** — it redistributes costs across the insurance sector without increasing what flows through to the hospitals that deliver the care. A fund that receives more through risk equalisation but continues underpaying its contracted hospitals does not increase the supply of maternity services.

*(Sources: Actuaries Institute submission; PHA submission August 2026; CHA pre-budget submission)*

### Initiative 4: Bundled Payment Models for Maternity Care

The most commercially significant — and most contested — proposal is PHA's push for a **bundled payment model**, under which a lead clinician (obstetrician, GP, or midwife) would coordinate all care and agree a single up-front price with an insurer for a complete pregnancy and birth episode.

PHA's specific proposal involves:
- Insurer and Commonwealth government each contributing a **minimum of $3,000** to the lead clinician
- Total government investment estimated at **$246 million over four years**
- Projected reduction in private birth costs of **approximately 30%**

PHA argues that bundled payments would eliminate the current fragmented billing architecture — where an obstetrician, anaesthetist, paediatrician, and hospital each bill separately, creating unpredictable out-of-pocket costs. PHA's modelling claims this fragmentation is a significant driver of consumer deterrence from private maternity and of "surprise bills."

The **AMA** rejected bundled payment models comprehensively in its January 2026 Position Statement on Key Principles for Private Maternity Funding. Its objections:
- **Medico-legal risk**: A designated "lead clinician" becomes legally exposed for outcomes they cannot fully control (e.g., an obstetric complication managed by another specialist).
- **Clinical autonomy**: Contracts that fix a single price for a pregnancy episode constrain the lead clinician's freedom to recommend additional interventions or consult specialists.
- **Administrative unworkability**: The complexity of billing adjustments when a bundled episode goes outside parameters (prematurity, caesarean section, neonatal intensive care) makes the model practically difficult to implement.

The **APHA** published a detailed rebuttal arguing that "bundled payments fail maternity patients" — arguing that the model advantages large corporate hospital groups (which can absorb risk within their networks) over smaller independent private hospitals, and that the government-to-insurer flow of money may not translate into better prices paid to hospitals.

*(Sources: PHA proposal; AMA Position Statement January 2026; APHA rebuttal publication; DoHDA Consultation Paper 1)*

## Key Findings

### 1. The Consultation Has 100 Submissions But No Government Response Yet

At the 21 September 2026 CEO Forum, the department reported receiving **100 submissions** to Consultation Paper 1. No government response had been published as at 26 October 2026. The department indicated that any reforms would need to be communicated to insurers before the **2027 Premium Round** applications, which close on **11 November 2026**, with premium outcomes determined between December 2026 and January 2027. Approved premiums take effect on **1 April 2027**.

This creates a narrow window: any reform that requires legislative change (like amending waiting period maximums) is unlikely to be in place for April 2027. Reforms achievable through regulation or administrative instruction (like risk equalisation changes) have a somewhat faster pathway, but even these require APRA interaction and insurer product recertification.

*(Source: CEO Forum 21 September 2026; PHI circular 78/26)*

### 2. The Deepest Fracture Is About Whose Problem the Reforms Are Solving

The consultation reveals a fundamental disagreement about the diagnosis of the crisis. The insurer peak body (PHA) and the government's consultation paper frame the problem as **consumer affordability** — premiums are too high, waiting periods are too long, and the Gold tier is inaccessible for younger families. Reform in this frame involves making cover cheaper or expanding what lower-tier products include.

The hospital sector (APHA and CHA) frames the problem as **structural underpayment** — insurer contracts pay hospitals less than it costs to deliver care, and insurer profits of $2.7 billion against hospital losses of $756 million demonstrate that the current system extracts value from hospitals to fund insurer profitability. Reform in this frame requires minimum hospital payment standards, ACCC-overseen contracting codes, and pass-through ratios (APHA and CHA have jointly proposed a minimum 90% of premium revenue passing to hospitals).

These are not compatible diagnoses. Making cover cheaper does not address hospital underpayment. Forcing higher hospital payments may raise premiums unless insurer margins are compressed. The consultation paper addresses the former (consumer affordability) but largely sidesteps the latter (hospital contracting), even though the government's June CEO Forum minutes included "making contract negotiations fairer" as a named objective. APHA stated explicitly that the consultation's maternity proposals "do not address the viability crisis caused by contracting abuses."

*(Sources: CHA pre-budget submission; APHA responses to consultation; CEO Forum June 2026)*

### 3. The AMA Identifies the Invisible Gap: Inadequate MBS Rebates

The AMA's January 2026 Position Statement articulates a point that both the PHI reform consultation and the insurer proposals systematically avoid: the **MBS rebate** for obstetric, anaesthetic, and paediatric services in the private sector is structurally inadequate and, for paediatric attendance at birth, virtually non-existent.

As documented in Reports 133 and 134 of this series, MBS fees for anaesthesia and obstetrics have been chronically under-indexed relative to rising practice costs, and MBS rebates for paediatric attendances at delivery do not cover the actual cost of the service. The AMA called for these rebates to be "immediately addressed" and indexed annually to rising care costs. Bundled payment models, by fixing a price envelope for the episode, do not solve this gap — they simply move it inside a contract rather than acknowledging it through the fee schedule.

The private health reform consultation's scope is explicitly limited to PHI regulation; it does not include MBS reform. Yet the MBS gap is a primary driver of the specialist shortages and out-of-pocket costs that make private maternity unaffordable. A consultation that repairs PHI product design but leaves MBS rebates inadequate is unlikely to stabilise the specialist workforce.

*(Source: AMA Position Statement January 2026; AMA submission September 2026)*

### 4. Newborn and Neonatal Coverage Is a Structural Gap PHI Reform Could Address

PHA's August 2026 submission included a specific and under-reported recommendation: that PHI fund **specialist and nurse practitioner care for newborns in the first three months after birth**, regardless of whether care is delivered in hospital or in a community setting.

Currently, a baby's PHI claim is only triggered when the baby is admitted as a patient to a Special Care Nursery or NICU — routine newborn assessments, lactation support, and community neonatal follow-up are unfunded under PHI (and, as documented in Report 131, poorly covered under the MBS). PHA's proposal would extend PHI value for newborns beyond the narrow "admitted to SCN" threshold and support the post-discharge care gap identified across Reports 131 and 134.

This was the one maternity reform proposal that the hospital sector did not actively contest — because it would generate new revenue for hospitals and community providers rather than redistributing existing costs. It also aligns with the NSW Birth Trauma Inquiry's recommendations on improved postnatal care. Whether the government includes it in its response remains to be seen.

*(Source: PHA submission August 2026; Report 131)*

### 5. Rural and Remote Australia Has a Special Vulnerability

The **National Rural Health Alliance (NRHA)** submission to the consultation flagged that PHI reform for maternity must include specific protections for rural, regional, and remote populations. The concern is that any reform focused on making metropolitan private maternity more affordable and sustainable may further disadvantage regional areas where the closure dynamic is already most advanced.

In Northern Territory, there is now no private maternity service at all (following Darwin Private Hospital's June 2025 closure). In regional Western Australia and Tasmania, closures have reduced choice to a single public option in many areas. PHI reforms that lower waiting periods or broaden tier coverage are irrelevant in communities where no private maternity unit remains to cover.

The NRHA asked that the government's reform response specifically model the impact on rural and remote communities and include targeted measures — potentially including block-grant or direct Commonwealth subsidies for private maternity facilities in under-serviced markets — rather than relying solely on PHI product design to maintain viability in areas where market incentives have already failed.

*(Source: NRHA submission to Consultation Paper 1)*

## Tensions and Open Questions

**Does PHI reform fix anything if hospital underpayment continues?** The actuarial logic of risk equalisation is sound — broader risk pooling reduces incentives to design policies that exclude costly sub-populations. But if the money flowing through risk equalisation still passes through insurer contracting processes that pay hospitals at below-cost rates, equitable redistribution among insurers does not improve hospital viability. The consultation paper's framing treats PHI product design and hospital contracting as separable issues; the hospital sector says they cannot be.

**Can a bundled payment model survive medico-legal complexity?** PHA's 30% cost reduction projection rests on a model where a lead clinician accepts responsibility for coordinating a complex clinical episode. The AMA's objection — that this creates unacceptable medico-legal exposure — applies with particular force in private obstetrics, where the long-tail liability documented in Report 128 (claims arising up to 21 years post-birth under paused limitation periods) would sit with the "lead clinician" under the bundled model.

**Who is reforming the MBS in parallel?** The consultation paper explicitly limits its scope to PHI regulation. But PHI costs are partially determined by the gap between what the MBS pays and what specialists charge — a gap that grows every year without MBS indexation. No concurrent MBS reform process for maternity-related items was announced as at October 2026.

**Is April 2027 achievable?** The premium round timeline is extremely compressed for structural reform. Risk equalisation changes require APRA engagement, actuarial modelling across all funds, and product recertification. Bundled payment models require contract frameworks that do not currently exist. Waiting period changes require legislative amendment. The most credible scenario is that the April 2027 round includes transitional risk equalisation adjustments, with more substantive structural reform deferred to 2028.

**What happens if the government does nothing?** The September 2026 CEO Forum noted that the second phase of the consultation would focus on hospital cost transparency and possible codes of conduct for contracting — the issues the hospital sector believes are the real problem. If Consultation Paper 1 produces only modest risk equalisation tweaks, and Consultation Paper 2 (on contracting) is unresolved by the 2027 premium round, the closure cascade documented since 2018 is likely to continue. Catholic Health Australia's projection — that private maternity could be extinct by the end of the decade without structural reform — would remain credible.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The hospital contracting crisis: secret pricing, underpayment, and the case for an ACCC code of conduct.** The second phase of the PHI reform consultation focuses specifically on hospital-insurer contracting — the issue APHA and CHA argue is the primary driver of hospital losses. An examination of how private hospital contracts work, why they are secret, and what a minimum pass-through or code of conduct might look like.

2. **APRA's role in private health insurance stability: prudential regulation vs. affordability.** APRA oversees insurer prudential requirements and advises on premium approvals. An examination of how APRA's capital adequacy requirements interact with the premium-setting process and whether the prudential framework is fit for purpose in a market experiencing structural contraction.

3. **The 2027 Premium Round and what it means for private health affordability.** Applications close 11 November 2026. An analysis of what premium increase requests have looked like in recent years, how the approval process works, and what consumers should expect in April 2027.

4. **Birthing on Country and First Nations perinatal health.** The Closing the Gap Target 2 (halving the rate of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander babies born with low birth weight by 2031) and the federal Birthing on Country program's scope and funding — a gap that the PHI reform consultation completely omits.

5. **International models for PHI maternity reform: Germany, Netherlands, Canada.** Other mixed public-private health systems have navigated similar maternity viability problems through different instruments — Germany's statutory insurer mandated coverage, the Netherlands' community-rated premium regulation, and Canada's provincial variation. What could Australia learn?
