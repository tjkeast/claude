# The $160 Billion Reckoning: Tobacco-Related Disease Burden in Australia — COPD, Lung Cancer, Cardiovascular Disease, and What It Costs the Health System

**Report 074 — 2026-08-19**

**Summary:** Despite daily smoking rates falling to a historic low of 5.8% in 2025, tobacco use remains Australia's second leading risk factor for disease burden, responsible for approximately 21,000 preventable deaths per year, 7.6% of all disability-adjusted life years lost, and a combined social cost of $160 billion in 2022–23. This report frames the economic stakes of the tobacco and vaping policy debate through the three disease categories that drive the majority of tobacco-attributable harm: COPD, lung cancer, and cardiovascular disease — and asks what the health payments system spends, saves, and could save further.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 073 established that 95.7% of all vapes sold in Australia are illicit and that the pharmacy-only cessation model struggles to compete with a $1.6 billion black market. That regulatory failure matters most when set against what tobacco-related disease actually costs the health system and society. This report provides that framing: the disease burden is the economic stake in every policy decision about tobacco control, cessation funding, and illicit market enforcement.

---

## Background: Where Australia Stands on Tobacco, 2026

The trajectory of Australian tobacco control is, by global standards, a genuine success story. Australia introduced the world's first plain packaging legislation in 2012, has run successive large-format graphic health warnings, and has raised tobacco excise systematically for over a decade (see Report 070). The National Drug Strategy Household Survey 2025 recorded daily smoking prevalence at **5.8% of adults aged 18+** — the lowest in recorded history, and well below the National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030's 2025 interim target of 10%.

But prevalence is a lagging indicator of burden. The cohort of Australians who smoked for 20–40 years, and who have now quit or are in the oldest age cohorts, is still presenting to hospitals, requiring PBS-funded medications, and dying of tobacco-caused disease. The disease burden attributable to tobacco reflects decades of accumulated exposure, not just the current smoking rate. That is why, even as smoking prevalence has halved in a generation, the absolute cost of tobacco to the Australian health system — measured in hospitalisations, MBS services, PBS expenditure, and productivity losses — remains enormous.

The **National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030** sets a goal of reducing smoking prevalence to **below 5% by 2030** across the population, with equity-specific goals for First Nations people, people in socioeconomic disadvantage, and people with mental illness. The government invested $93 million in the strategy's launch (including $63 million for a quit-smoking communication campaign and $30 million for cessation assistance strengthening). That investment is justified by the healthcare cost it aims to displace.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Scale of Tobacco's Disease Burden

The most recent Australian Burden of Disease Study data (AIHW, 2024) confirms tobacco as **the second leading risk factor for total disease burden** in Australia, responsible for **7.6% of all disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)** lost in 2024. While this is a substantial improvement on the 10.4% recorded in 2003 — reflecting decades of declining smoking rates flowing through to reduced disease incidence — tobacco remains, in absolute terms, a vast driver of preventable illness and death.

**Deaths attributable to tobacco:** Tobacco causes approximately **21,000 premature deaths per year** in Australia. The 2015-16 figure from AIHW was 20,032; more recent estimates based on updated mortality data and disease-attribution modelling are consistent with this range. Tobacco causes more preventable deaths than alcohol, illicit drugs, and road trauma combined.

**The disease distribution:** The highest proportions of any disease's burden attributable to tobacco are:
- **Lung cancer: 73.0%** of all lung cancer burden
- **COPD: 71.0%** of all COPD burden
- **Laryngeal cancer: 68.9%**
- Tobacco also contributes to oesophageal cancer, kidney cancer, bladder cancer, cervical cancer, and many other cancers — the carcinogenic reach of smoking extends well beyond the lung

**Standardised rate improvement:** The tobacco-attributable DALY rate has fallen from 21.6 per 1,000 Australians in 2003 to 12.7 per 1,000 in 2024 — a 41% improvement. This is the dividend of tobacco control policy. But at 12.7 per 1,000, the residual burden is still massive at a population scale.

### 2. COPD: The Slow-Moving Hospital Admission Crisis

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is the single largest component of Australia's respiratory disease burden, and tobacco is its dominant cause. COPD accounts for **51% of all respiratory disease burden (DALY)**, **72% of the respiratory fatal burden (years of life lost)**, and **38% of the non-fatal respiratory burden (years lived with disability)**.

**Hospitalisations:** In 2023–24, there were **67,700 hospital admissions** with a principal diagnosis of COPD for people aged 45 and over. COPD is consistently among the leading causes of **potentially preventable hospitalisations (PPHs)** — a metric the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care uses to identify conditions where adequate primary care should reduce acute hospital presentations. The COPD clinical care standard, updated in October 2024, was partly a response to the high PPH rate.

**Deaths:** COPD was the underlying cause of **7,437 deaths in 2023** (28 deaths per 100,000 population) — **4.1% of all deaths**. Among chronic diseases, COPD ranked fifth in the AIHW's top disease burden list for 2024.

**Comorbidities:** In 2022, **88% of people aged 45 and over with COPD** were living with one or more other chronic conditions. COPD rarely presents alone: it clusters with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, depression, and musculoskeletal conditions. This comorbidity profile substantially multiplies the MBS, PBS, and hospital cost per patient.

**Economic burden:** A 2024 analysis — *Change that cannot wait: reducing the human and economic burden of COPD in Australia* — estimated COPD's total economic burden at **$24.98 billion** annually, comprising:
- Annual healthcare costs: **$1.67 billion**
- Lost productivity: **$4.85 billion**
- Premature mortality costs and intangible burden: the remainder

Separately, the AIHW estimated **$1.8 billion in government health expenditure** on COPD in 2023–24 — making it responsible for **30% of all respiratory condition expenditure**. PBS costs for COPD include long-acting bronchodilators (LABAs, LAMAs), inhaled corticosteroids, and triple-therapy combinations such as budesonide/glycopyrronium/formoterol. Supplemental oxygen (Home Oxygen Program, state-funded) adds further cost outside the PBS.

**The treatment gap:** Despite the clinical care standard, COPD is substantially under-diagnosed. An estimated one million Australians are living with COPD without a formal diagnosis. Spirometry — the diagnostic test — is an MBS-rebated item, but uptake in general practice is low. Undiagnosed patients do not receive disease-modifying treatment, accumulate lung function loss, and present to hospitals in acute exacerbation. This under-diagnosis problem is partly a payment system problem: GPs are not well remunerated for the time required to conduct spirometry, interpret results, and initiate a complex management plan.

### 3. Lung Cancer: The Deadliest Cancer, Now Entering a New Treatment Era

Lung cancer is Australia's **deadliest cancer by mortality**, despite not being the most common. In 2025, approximately **15,108 new cases** were diagnosed — broadly stable in age-standardised terms (an estimated 55 cases per 100,000, down slightly from 57 in 2000).

The gender pattern has reversed significantly. Male lung cancer incidence has fallen sharply (from 85 per 100,000 in 2000 to ~60 per 100,000 in 2025) — tracking the steep fall in male smoking rates from the 1970s onward. Female incidence has *risen* (from 36 per 100,000 in 2000 to ~51 per 100,000 in 2025) — because female smoking rates peaked later and have been slower to decline, and because adenocarcinoma of the lung (the subtype more common in women and non-smokers) has distinct epidemiology.

**Survival has improved substantially:** Five-year survival rose from **9.7% (1992–1996)** to **27.3% (2017–2021)** overall; from 12% to 33% for women, and from 9.7% to 22.7% for men. This improvement is largely attributable to two treatment revolutions:

1. **Immunotherapy** (checkpoint inhibitors): pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab target the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and have transformed outcomes for a subset of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. PBS listings have expanded significantly: from 1 March 2026, the PBS listing for nivolumab and ipilimumab was extended to cover broader indications for advanced NSCLC.

2. **Targeted therapies**: for patients with *EGFR* mutations (~15% of Australian NSCLC), *ALK* rearrangements (~5%), *ROS1* rearrangements, and *KRAS* G12C mutations, oral targeted therapies (osimertinib, alectinib, crizotinib, sotorasib) have moved lung cancer from a rapidly fatal disease to a managed chronic condition for an increasing minority.

**Healthcare costs per patient are substantial:** Research from the 45 and Up Study estimated a **mean excess health service cost of $51,944 per lung cancer case** from one year prior to three years after diagnosis. Higher costs correlated with younger age, metastatic disease, and longer survival (which, paradoxically, generates more treatment utilisation). The PBS component alone is significant: monthly costs of PBS-funded immunotherapy or targeted therapy easily reach $5,000–$15,000 per patient before managed entry agreements (MEAs) — the commercial-in-confidence price negotiated between manufacturers and the Department of Health.

**The PBAC tension:** The Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) has consistently grappled with how to assess lung cancer medicines. MSD's proposal for a "multi-cancer" broad access model for pembrolizumab (KEYTRUDA) was rejected by PBAC — reflecting the Committee's concern about open-ended PBS expenditure without indication-specific evidence of cost-effectiveness. Individual pembrolizumab indications continue to progress through PBAC on their own merits.

**National Lung Cancer Screening Program:** Commencing **July 2025**, Australia's National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) uses low-dose CT (LDCT) in high-risk individuals (approximately 50–70 years old with significant smoking history). The program was assessed by MSAC in October 2022 and announced in May 2023. An updated health economic analysis (Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific, 2024) using NELSON trial data and modern immunotherapy-era treatment costs estimated an ICER of **AU$39,250 per QALY** (95% CI: $18,150–$108,300), within the range Australia typically considers cost-effective. Importantly, the cost-effectiveness improves further when the downstream Medicare and PBS savings from earlier-stage diagnosis (lower-cost surgery vs. high-cost immunotherapy for late-stage disease) are incorporated.

### 4. Cardiovascular Disease: The Largest Absolute Disease Burden

Cardiovascular disease is the **#1 cause of disease burden in Australia** — coronary heart disease alone tops the AIHW's burden-of-disease ranking. Tobacco's contribution to CVD is smaller in proportionate terms than its COPD and lung cancer contribution, but in absolute terms, the CVD burden attributable to tobacco is enormous because CVD is so common.

Key figures for tobacco's CVD contribution (AIHW Burden of Disease Study 2024 and Heart Foundation):
- Tobacco is responsible for **12% of the total cardiovascular disease burden** (Heart Foundation)
- Tobacco contributes **11% of the coronary heart disease burden**
- Tobacco contributes **9.4% of the stroke burden**
- Tobacco use is one of six major modifiable risk factors that together account for **65% of all CVD burden in Australia**; the others are high blood pressure (36%), dietary risks (32%), high cholesterol (30%), overweight/obesity (28%), and physical inactivity (12%)

The global burden of disease analysis (GBD 2021, published 2024) projected that tobacco-induced cardiovascular disease burden will continue to decline globally through 2045 in high-income countries with strong tobacco control — consistent with Australia's trajectory.

**Hospitalisation and cost context:** Cardiovascular disease generates the highest total hospital inpatient costs of any disease category in Australia. The fraction attributable to tobacco is difficult to isolate at the MBS/PBS level — coronary artery disease patients receive PBS-funded statins, antiplatelets, beta-blockers, and ACE inhibitors regardless of the cause of their atherosclerosis. But the tobacco-attributable fraction (12% of CVD burden) represents a substantial cost displacement: smokers who develop coronary artery disease approximately 10 years earlier than non-smokers generate earlier, longer utilisation of cardiologist MBS items, cardiac imaging, cardiac catheterisation, stenting, and CABG.

### 5. The $160 Billion Aggregate: Understanding the Social Cost Figure

The most comprehensive estimate of tobacco's cost to Australia comes from the George Institute for Global Health and the National Drug Research Institute (NDRI), with 2022–23 figures as follows:

| Cost Category | Estimated Amount (2022–23) |
|---|---|
| Premature mortality (intangible) | $107.5 billion |
| Ill-health and quality of life loss | $29.8 billion |
| Healthcare costs (direct) | $7.9 billion |
| Lost productivity | Included in broader estimate |
| **Total social cost** | **~$160 billion** |

The $7.9 billion in direct healthcare costs is the component directly relevant to the payments system — MBS services, PBS medications, hospital admissions, community health programs, and allied health. This is the floor: the figure that flows through Medicare, the hospital funding agreements (National Health Reform Agreement), and the PBS.

Earlier estimates from NDRI for 2015–16 found **1.7 million hospital admissions** related to smoking and $6.8 billion in direct healthcare costs — a figure that has grown in real terms in the intervening decade, despite falling smoking prevalence, because the treatment intensity per smoker-attributable disease episode has increased (particularly for lung cancer, where immunotherapy has dramatically extended treatment duration and cost).

The non-healthcare components — $107.5 billion for premature mortality and $29.8 billion for ill-health — use the value of a statistical life (VSL) framework and are not MBS/PBS expenditure. They represent the economic value of the years of life lost and quality of life lost to tobacco-attributable disease.

### 6. The Cessation-to-Savings Calculation

The case for investing in smoking cessation is powerful at every level of the payments system.

**PBS-funded cessation pharmacotherapy is highly cost-effective:**
- Bupropion (Zyban): **$7,900 per DALY averted** — well below the commonly used Australian willingness-to-pay threshold of ~$50,000 per QALY
- Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT patches): **$17,000 per DALY averted** — also cost-effective
- Varenicline (generic, following the Champix recall and relisting): ~**$103 for a full 12-week course** at the concessional PBS rate — among the most cost-effective interventions in the entire PBS

**Quitting's effect on life expectancy:** Modelling estimates quitting increases life expectancy by **1–7.6 years**, depending on age at cessation and sex — with earlier cessation generating far greater gains. A 40-year-old who quits smoking avoids the majority of their tobacco-attributable disease risk.

**The vaping-as-cessation gap (highlighted in Report 073):** A 2026 MJA study found vaping **three times more effective than NRT** for smoking cessation in disadvantaged populations (28.4% continuous abstinence vs 9.6% for NRT). Yet vaping is not PBS-listed, not MBS-rebated for the pharmacist consultation, and not covered by private health insurance. The pharmacist Schedule 3 vaping consultation — the access point the 2024 vaping reforms created — attracts no professional services payment. This creates a structural misalignment: the most effective cessation tool is the most poorly integrated into the funded cessation pathway.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Lagging Disease Burden vs. the Falling Prevalence Narrative

The government's public messaging (correctly) celebrates historic low smoking rates. But the disease burden in 2026 is dominated by cohorts who smoked heavily in the 1970s–2000s. The 21,000 annual tobacco deaths, and the 67,700 annual COPD hospitalisations, are the legacy of yesterday's smoking rates — not today's. This means the burden reduction from the current 5.8% prevalence will not be fully visible in disease statistics for another 20–40 years. The policy challenge is sustaining investment in cessation and control measures whose payoff is decades away.

### Tension 2: PBS Affordability of Lung Cancer Treatment in the Immunotherapy Era

The survival improvement in lung cancer is real and large — from a 10% to a 27% five-year survival rate in three decades. But it has been bought partly by PBS listing of immunotherapy drugs that cost tens of thousands of dollars per patient per year. As more patients survive longer on these treatments, the PBS expenditure for lung cancer rises even as incidence slowly declines. The PBAC faces increasing pressure on how to evaluate drugs where extended survival is itself a cost driver, and where managed access agreements are structurally opaque. The Commonwealth's HTA reform agenda (covered in Report 022) directly intersects with lung cancer medicine access.

### Tension 3: The COPD Under-Diagnosis and Prevention Gap

The estimate that one million Australians have undiagnosed COPD suggests a massive prevention opportunity: earlier diagnosis means earlier bronchodilator therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, and cessation support — interventions that slow disease progression and reduce hospitalisation. But general practice is not resourced or incentivised to conduct systematic spirometry screening in at-risk groups (current and former heavy smokers aged 45+). The GP practice economics crisis (Report 009) directly constrains the ability to deliver this preventive care. A spirometry MBS item exists (item 11506), but practice nurse delivery and standalone spirometry clinics are not well supported in the current payment architecture.

### Tension 4: The Equity Dimension

Tobacco use is heavily concentrated in socioeconomic disadvantage, First Nations communities, people with mental illness, and those with other substance use disorders — populations with higher smoking rates, more difficulty accessing cessation support, and less capacity to pay out-of-pocket for cessation aids. The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030 explicitly targets equity, but the cessation pathway — even with PBS-subsidised pharmacotherapy — requires GP visits, pharmacy access, and health literacy that disadvantaged groups face barriers to utilising. The illicit tobacco market (Report 072) further complicates this: price-responsive disadvantaged smokers who might have been deterred by high legal tobacco prices can now access cheaper illicit cigarettes, reducing the price signal's effectiveness in the most price-sensitive populations.

### Tension 5: The National Lung Cancer Screening Program's Equity Design

The NLCSP, commencing July 2025, was designed with equity explicitly in mind. An equitable program design requires reaching First Nations people, rural and remote populations, and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations — the groups with the highest tobacco-attributable lung cancer rates. But CT scanning infrastructure is concentrated in metropolitan areas, and primary care access in rural and remote settings is constrained (Report 012). An NLCSP that screens predominantly middle-class metropolitan Australians will miss the populations with greatest mortality reduction potential.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The National Lung Cancer Screening Program in detail: implementation, equity design, and cost.** The NLCSP commenced in July 2025 — its first year of operation is now underway. How is it being implemented in general practice? What MBS items are involved? What is the cost model? This is a specific, new, and directly relevant healthcare payments development.

2. **Heart failure and tobacco's cardiovascular legacy: the growing burden of HFpEF.** Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is increasingly prevalent among older Australians — many of whom are former smokers — and is one of the fastest-growing causes of hospitalisation. The payments system is ill-equipped for its management.

3. **Pulmonary rehabilitation and chronic disease management plans: the MBS gap in COPD care.** COPD is a prime example of a condition where chronic disease management payments (GP management plans, team care arrangements, the chronic disease dental program) intersect in complex ways — and where there is strong evidence that pulmonary rehabilitation reduces hospitalisation but access is severely limited.

4. **Cancer screening economics: comparing the NLCSP, BreastScreen, bowel screening, and cervical screening.** Australia now funds four national cancer screening programs. The NLCSP is the newest. How does the cost-per-QALY and equity design compare across these programs?

5. **The tobacco-mental health nexus: smoking rates among people with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and depression, and why the cessation system fails them.** Smoking prevalence among people with serious mental illness exceeds 40% — dramatically above the 5.8% general population rate — and these individuals face structural barriers to cessation support, pharmacotherapy access, and follow-up. This thread connects to the mental health funding series (Reports 011, 015, 017, 020).
