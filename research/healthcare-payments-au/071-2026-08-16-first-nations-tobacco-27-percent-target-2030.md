# First Nations Tobacco: The 27% by 2030 Target, the Remote Stall, and the Payment System That Wasn't Built for This Problem

**Report 071 — 2026-08-16**

**Summary:** Australia's National Tobacco Strategy sets a target of reducing daily smoking among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people to 27% or less by 2030. At the national headline level the trajectory looks surprisingly close — daily smoking fell from 37% in 2018–19 to 29% in 2022–23 — but the aggregate figure conceals a profound geographic fracture: non-remote First Nations communities are already at the target (27%), while remote and very remote communities have seen essentially no change for a decade (52–53%). The 27% target will not be met without tackling the remote stall, and tackling the remote stall requires addressing structural determinants — poverty, overcrowding, normalised smoking, illicit tobacco access, and chronically underfunded community-controlled health services — that the existing healthcare payment architecture is not designed to reach.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 070 examined Australia's tobacco and nicotine payment system — the excise collapse, vaping disruption, and PBS cessation architecture — and identified Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people as among the hardest to reach within the residual smoking population, with their own specific target embedded in the National Tobacco Strategy. This report examines that target in depth.

---

## Background: The Scale of the Problem and Why It Matters

Tobacco smoking is the largest single contributor to the health gap between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and non-Indigenous Australians. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare estimates that tobacco use accounts for:

- **23% of the total gap** in disease burden between First Nations people and other Australians
- **12% of the total disease burden** experienced by First Nations people (2018 data)
- **47%** of the burden of respiratory disease among First Nations people
- **37%** of the burden of cancer
- **34%** of the burden of cardiovascular disease

These are extraordinary figures. No other single modifiable risk factor comes close to tobacco's contribution to First Nations health inequity. Addressing tobacco smoking among First Nations Australians is not a niche public health objective — it is one of the highest-value interventions available in the entire healthcare system.

### The Historical Trajectory

Smoking prevalence among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults aged 15 and over has declined substantially over the past two decades:

| Year | Daily smoking rate (First Nations 15+) |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| 2004–05 | ~50% |
| 2008 | ~45% |
| 2014–15 | ~41% |
| 2018–19 | 37% |
| 2022–23 | **29%** |

The most recent figure — 29% daily smoking in 2022–23 — comes from the ABS National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Survey (NATSIHS), released in 2024. This represents a fall of 8 percentage points over just four years, a rate of decline faster than any prior period. First Nations people remain, however, **2.6 times as likely** as non-Indigenous Australians to smoke daily, after adjusting for age differences (the non-Indigenous daily rate is approximately 8.3%).

### The National Tobacco Strategy Target

The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030 sets a specific First Nations target:

> *Reduce daily smoking prevalence among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people aged 15 and over to **27% or less by 2030**.*

This is a Closing the Gap priority, and sits alongside two broader national targets (less than 10% adult daily smoking by 2025 — likely met — and 5% or less by 2030).

At 29% daily smoking in 2022–23, the First Nations target appears tantalizingly close — within 2 percentage points, with seven years remaining. That reading is dangerously misleading.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Geographic Fracture: Non-Remote at Target, Remote Stuck for a Decade

The most important finding in the 2022–23 NATSIHS data is not the headline figure but the split by remoteness:

| Geography | Daily smoking (2012–13) | Daily smoking (2022–23) | Change |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|--------|
| Non-remote | ~40% | **~27%** | −13 pp |
| Remote/very remote | ~52% | **~53%** | ≈ 0 (no change) |

Non-remote First Nations communities have effectively met the 2030 target already — a decade ahead of schedule, through the same population trends (tobacco denormalisation, excise price pressure, urban health service access) that have driven the national decline. This is a genuine and significant achievement.

But remote and very remote First Nations communities have seen essentially **no change in daily smoking rates for more than a decade**. The national headline improvement is almost entirely a non-remote phenomenon. The aggregate 29% figure flatters the real picture for those who need the most help.

Approximately 22% of First Nations Australians live in remote or very remote areas (about 175,000–180,000 people). Surveys of specific remote communities document daily smoking rates of up to **82%**, with community-level normalisation so profound that smoking is embedded in social rituals, kinship relationships, and daily routine in a way that standard tobacco control interventions have historically been unable to penetrate.

The implications for the target are stark: to reach 27% nationally by 2030, the remote cohort — which will barely move without specific structural intervention — must either be brought into significant decline, or the non-remote cohort must continue falling sharply to compensate.

### 2. The Pregnancy Crisis: 44% of First Nations Births Involve a Smoking Mother

Smoking during pregnancy is the most significant **modifiable** risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, perinatal death, and sudden infant death syndrome.

As of June 2025 data from the national key performance indicators (nKPI) for Indigenous primary healthcare:

- **44%** of female First Nations regular clients who gave birth within the previous 12 months smoked at some point during pregnancy
- This compares with approximately 7–9% for non-Indigenous Australian mothers
- The First Nations antenatal smoking rate, while declining, remains more than **five times the national average**
- Of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women who smoke at the time of pregnancy, approximately **only 1 in 10 successfully quits** during pregnancy

The SISTAQUIT program — a randomised controlled trial of training healthcare providers in intensive smoking cessation support for pregnant First Nations women — demonstrated that structured provider training with NRT provision significantly improves cessation rates. The program, led by Professor Gillian Gould, has produced a scaled-up implementation protocol (iSISTAQUIT). However, national rollout has been constrained by: limited primary care capacity in remote areas, the absence of routine recording of smoking status at every antenatal contact, and workforce gaps in rural maternity services (documented in Report 012 on the Modified Monash Model).

### 3. The Tackling Indigenous Smoking (TIS) Program: Structure, Funding, and Limits

The primary Commonwealth-funded program addressing First Nations tobacco is the Tackling Indigenous Smoking (TIS) program, operating continuously since 2010. Key features:

**Structure:**
- 26 organisations funded nationally through Regional Tobacco Control Grants (RTCGs)
- The vast majority of TIS teams are based out of Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs)
- Since July 2023, TIS geographical boundaries were realigned with Indigenous Regions, achieving **complete geographical coverage across Australia** for the first time — a significant operational milestone

**Funding:**
- Current program period: 2023–24 to 2026–27
- Base funding for TIS: approximately $55–60 million over the four-year period (aggregate, from 2023–24 Budget vaping and tobacco control package totalling ~$141 million across all tobacco-related programs)
- Additional $10 million per annum (indexed) for vaping prevention activities was added in the 2023–24 Budget to specifically address the youth vaping crisis in First Nations communities

**What TIS does — and does not do:**
TIS is explicitly described by the Department of Health and Aged Care as a **population health promotion** program, not a smoking cessation clinical program. TIS teams are funded to conduct:
- Community education and health promotion activities
- Brief intervention training for health workers
- Smoke-free environment advocacy
- Vaping prevention activities (from 2023)
- Social marketing and community events

TIS teams are explicitly **not** funded to provide clinical cessation support, prescribe NRT, or operate as therapy services. This is an important distinction: the program is designed to shift population-level norms and uptake, not to provide individualised cessation treatment. For that, First Nations smokers must access primary care — an ACCHO, a GP, a primary health network commissioned service — and work through the PBS pharmacotherapy pathway.

**The gap between TIS and PBS:**
The practical pathway for a First Nations person in a remote community who wants to quit smoking looks like this:
1. Attend a health service (ACCHO or equivalent)
2. Discuss cessation with a clinician who can prescribe or recommend PBS-listed NRT/varenicline/bupropion
3. Obtain and use pharmacotherapy (with a PBS co-payment of $7.70 for concession card holders)
4. Access follow-up support (phone, Quitline, TIS worker)

In major cities and regional areas, this pathway is achievable. In remote communities with limited ACCHO staff, transport barriers, low health literacy, and community smoking normalisation, each step presents significant friction. The TIS program can work on norm change but cannot substitute for the clinical pathway.

**The Wave 1 Evaluation (2025):**
A June 2025 evaluation report (Wave 1) of the current TIS program round (2023–24 to 2026–27) documented that TIS teams have achieved complete geographic coverage for the first time and are engaging communities on both smoking and vaping. However, the evaluation also notes the inherent challenge of measuring population-level impact within the timeframe of the grant round, and does not yet provide revised smoking prevalence figures by region. A full program evaluation is expected by 2027.

### 4. What the Evidence Says Works — and Doesn't

The evidence base for effective First Nations tobacco cessation has grown substantially in the past decade. Key findings:

**Culturally-led, community-controlled approaches work better:**
- Programs designed and delivered by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people consistently outperform external-provider models in engagement, retention, and cessation success
- The **Koori Quit Pack** (an ACCHO-led multi-component mailout NRT plus phone support program) demonstrated 39% smoke-free at 6 months among participants — significantly better than standard Quitline outcomes
- The **Which Way?** program (led by Wiradjuri researcher Associate Professor Michelle Kennedy) is now scaling nationally, incorporating mailout NRT, peer phone support from Aboriginal staff, and culturally tailored cessation resources

**Combination NRT + brief counselling works:**
- RCTs in remote Australian settings confirm combination NRT (patch + faster-acting formulation) with brief counselling is effective when delivered with cultural competency
- The BOABS study (a remote community RCT) demonstrated that intensive Aboriginal health worker-delivered support significantly increased quit attempts and sustained cessation compared to usual care

**Barriers to pharmacotherapy are structural, not motivational:**
Research consistently shows First Nations people who smoke have high motivation to quit — in the 2022–23 NATSIHS, 44% had attempted to quit in the past year, and 44% of current smokers expressed a desire to quit. The barrier is access to support, not intention. Key structural barriers include:
- **Distance from clinical services** (remote, very remote)
- **Cost** — even $7.70 PBS co-payments are a barrier for households in very low-income situations; Free NRT supply through programs like Koori Quit Pack dramatically increases uptake
- **Racist or culturally unsafe healthcare experiences** — documented in surveys of both patients and Aboriginal health workers as a significant deterrent to seeking cessation support in mainstream (non-ACCHO) settings
- **Smoking normalisation** in the community context — making quit attempts harder to sustain when smoking is ubiquitous in households and social networks
- **Clinical workforce gaps** — fewer doctors per capita in remote areas means fewer opportunities for brief intervention or prescribing

**What doesn't work:**
- Standard mainstream Quitline (limited cultural adaptation; call centre model poorly suited to remote community contexts)
- Mass media campaigns designed for non-Indigenous urban audiences
- Individual behaviour-change approaches that ignore social determinants and community context
- Programs that require face-to-face clinical contact as a prerequisite for NRT access in remote communities where clinical contacts are infrequent

### 5. Socioeconomic Determinants: Tobacco as a Poverty Disease

A 2025 study in the *International Journal of Social Welfare* (Nguyen et al.) used longitudinal data (2002–2022) to decompose the smoking prevalence gap between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Australians. Socioeconomic factors — income, education, employment — account for a substantial share of the observed disparity. Key determinants of non-smoking among First Nations adults in regional areas include:

- **High income** — strongly associated with non-smoking
- **Completing Year 10** — independently associated with non-smoking, even controlling for income
- **Employment** — associated with both cessation and prevention

The corollary is important: First Nations communities experiencing the highest poverty rates, lowest educational attainment, highest unemployment, and most severe housing overcrowding also carry the highest tobacco burden. These are the same communities in which smoking rates have not changed in a decade.

This finding does not mean tobacco control programs are pointless — it means that programs focused solely on information and motivation will have limited impact unless they operate alongside structural investments in economic participation, housing, and education. The Closing the Gap priority areas are not separate silos; the trajectory of the tobacco target depends partly on delivery against other Closing the Gap targets.

### 6. The Illicit Tobacco Threat in Remote Communities

Report 070 documented the collapse of Australia's tobacco excise base through the illicit market. In remote First Nations communities, illicit tobacco introduces a specific complication.

Research on the impact of excise increases on tobacco sales in remote Aboriginal community stores found a 5.8–8.2% immediate decline in sales following 12.5% price rises (2016, 2018). This suggests price sensitivity — a positive finding for tobacco control. However, the effect was concentrated on mainstream and premium factory-made cigarettes; cheaper roll-your-own or loose-leaf tobacco showed smaller responses.

As illicit tobacco enters remote community supply chains at $10–20 per pack (versus $40–55 legal), the price differential undermines one of the two main drivers of smoking reduction that have worked in non-remote areas (the other being cultural denormalisation, which is also weaker in high-prevalence remote communities). The specific penetration of illicit tobacco into remote First Nations communities is not well measured, but the conditions — geographic isolation from enforcement, high price sensitivity, entrenched supply relationships — are consistent with significant risk.

### 7. The ACCHO Funding Constraint

As documented in Report 013 (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Payments), the Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation sector operates with a structural funding deficit estimated at $4.4 billion nationally. ACCHOs are the most trusted and effective points of healthcare access for First Nations people — including for smoking cessation — but they are chronically under-resourced relative to the health need of their patient populations.

The practical consequence for tobacco: ACCHOs that might otherwise offer dedicated cessation clinics, train Aboriginal health workers in cessation support, stock and dispense NRT for free provision, or run group quit programs are typically operating at or beyond staffing capacity. Tobacco cessation — unless separately funded (as TIS partly is, though for health promotion not clinical cessation) — competes for clinical time with diabetes management, chronic disease care plans, mental health support, and antenatal care.

The separation of TIS (population health promotion, DOHAC-funded) from clinical cessation support (expected to flow through the MBS/PBS pathway via ACCHO primary care consultations) creates a funding gap: TIS workers can raise awareness and motivate quit attempts, but the clinical infrastructure to actually support cessation in a sustained, evidence-based way in remote communities is underfunded.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: The Target Is Met in Cities, Stuck in Country — But Policy Is National

The 2030 target of 27% daily smoking is a national figure. Non-remote First Nations communities are at 27% already. For the national figure to stay at or below 27%, remote areas must come down significantly — or the target will be met on paper due to the majority of First Nations Australians living in non-remote areas while nearly one-third of the First Nations population in remote areas continues smoking at extraordinary rates. The risk is that the headline target is met while a profound remote-area crisis is obscured.

### Tension 2: Health Promotion vs. Clinical Cessation — TIS Funds the Former, Leaves the Latter to a System Not Designed to Deliver It

TIS is a health promotion program. PBS is a clinical reimbursement system. Quitline is a telephone service. The integration of these three into a functioning cessation pathway for remote First Nations communities is assumed rather than funded. Evidence-based models like Koori Quit Pack and iSISTAQUIT exist but are not nationally funded; they operate through research grants and limited program dollars. Scaling these evidence-based models requires deliberate investment decisions that have not yet been made.

### Tension 3: Pregnancy Smoking — The Highest-Impact Target Is Not Being Resourced Proportionally

With 44% of First Nations births involving antenatal smoking and only 1 in 10 women successfully quitting, the pregnancy cohort represents the single highest-impact tobacco intervention opportunity in the system. Each successful antenatal quit has second-order benefits across generations — lower rates of low birth weight, perinatal death, and infant SIDS, and potentially reduced social transmission of smoking to children. Yet antenatal cessation receives no dedicated national funding stream; it is expected to be delivered within existing antenatal care frameworks by an already-stretched workforce.

### Tension 4: Social Determinants Cannot Be Fixed by a Health Program

Tobacco control programs can shift norms and improve cessation support access. They cannot fix poverty, housing overcrowding, or unemployment — the structural drivers of elevated smoking in remote communities. The 27% target, to be achievable for remote populations, arguably requires the government to acknowledge that tobacco control funds need to work in partnership with non-health investments, and that the target timeline may need to be recalibrated for remote populations even as national progress is celebrated.

### Tension 5: The Illicit Tobacco Market Risks Undermining Remote Price Sensitivity

The positive finding that excise price rises had measurable impact on tobacco sales in remote community stores is potentially being eroded as illicit tobacco becomes more accessible. This has not been studied in detail for remote First Nations communities, but it represents a monitoring gap in current tobacco control surveillance. If illicit tobacco is penetrating remote supply chains, the price mechanism — one of the few tools that has worked — loses traction precisely where it is most needed.
