# Opioid Treatment Program Reform: Australia's 57,754-Patient System That Needs to Double

**Report 058 — 2026-08-03**

**Summary:** Australia has 57,754 people receiving opioid pharmacotherapy — but the best available evidence suggests the system should be serving at least twice that number. A landmark July 2023 PBS reform moved methadone and buprenorphine onto the standard prescribing pathway for the first time, committing $377.3 million over four years to improve access. But prescriber numbers are declining, geographic inequities remain severe, prisons remain essentially untreated, and the imminent December 2026 withdrawal of one of two long-acting injectable options is creating clinical disruption. With overdose deaths at a record high and the National Drug Strategy due to expire this year, the opioid treatment program sits at an inflection point.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 057 documented that Australia spends only 1.6% of its drug budget on harm reduction and identified opioid agonist treatment (OAT) as a harm reduction modality whose geographic inequity, prison access gap, and funding architecture warrant dedicated examination.

## Background

### What Opioid Agonist Treatment Is

Opioid agonist treatment (OAT) — also called opioid pharmacotherapy or opioid dependence treatment (ODT) — uses long-acting opioid medicines to stabilise people with opioid use disorder. By occupying the same brain receptors as illicit opioids, these medicines eliminate withdrawal, reduce cravings, block the euphoric effects of additional opioid use, and enable people to function and engage with other health, social, and employment services.

OAT is one of the most evidence-supported interventions in medicine. A Cochrane systematic review estimated that methadone maintenance treatment reduces illicit opioid use by 33%, criminal activity by 45%, mortality by 40–80%, and HIV risk behaviours substantially. Buprenorphine achieves similar outcomes. Among people with opioid use disorder, OAT is associated with a four-fold reduction in all-cause mortality, primarily by preventing overdose deaths. The evidence base is decades deep and consistently positive.

In Australia, four medicines are approved for opioid dependence treatment:
- **Methadone** (liquid, daily supervised dosing) — the oldest and most used formulation; cleared very slowly, providing long duration of action
- **Buprenorphine-naloxone (Suboxone and generics)** — sublingual film or tablet; the naloxone component deters injection; taken daily or every second day
- **Buprenorphine alone (sublingual)** — used where naloxone co-formulation is clinically contraindicated
- **Long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB)** — subcutaneous depot injection; Buvidal (Camurus) available weekly and monthly; Sublocade (Indivior) available monthly (see Section 5 for upcoming withdrawal)

### Australia's OAT Architecture: State-Managed, PBS-Funded

Unlike most of Medicare's fee-for-service structure, opioid dependence treatment has historically been managed through state and territory opioid treatment programs (OTPs), with state governments controlling prescriber accreditation, approved dispensing points, and takeaway dose policy. This creates a fragmented system where the rules — who can prescribe, where medicines can be dispensed, how many takeaway doses are allowed — vary substantially across jurisdictions.

A pivotal change occurred on **1 July 2023**: methadone and the buprenorphine formulations were listed on the PBS under Section 100 (Highly Specialised Drugs, Community Access), bringing them into the standard PBS prescribing pathway for the first time. The 2023–24 Budget committed **$377.3 million over four years** plus **$98.4 million ongoing** to fund this reform.

Under the new model:
- Prescribers with appropriate authority prescribe using PBS streamlined authority
- Dispensing occurs at Section 90 approved community pharmacies, or Section 92 approved medical practitioners, or Section 94 hospital pharmacies
- Patients pay a PBS co-payment: **$7.30 per 28-day supply** for concession card holders (the frozen concessional rate); **$30 per 28 days** for general patients — with payments counting toward the PBS Safety Net
- Pharmacies cannot charge additional private dispensing or dosing fees on top of the co-payment

This reform was significant. Previously, patients often paid separate dispensing fees to pharmacies (commonly $4–$8 per supervised dose, amounting to $30–$60 per week), creating substantial out-of-pocket costs that were invisible to the PBS and excluded from safety nets. The July 2023 change standardised and reduced these costs substantially.

## Key Findings

### 1. System Scale: 57,754 Patients and a Probable Treatment Gap of 50–70%

On a snapshot day in **June 2025**, the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) National Opioid Pharmacotherapy Statistics Annual Data (NOPSAD) collection recorded **57,754 Australians receiving opioid pharmacotherapy**. This represents people in active treatment on the data collection day across all jurisdictions.

Of those:
- **60% (34,665 clients)** received a buprenorphine formulation (including LAIB)
- Approximately **40%** received methadone — which remains dominant for older patients (50+) while buprenorphine formulations are more common among clients aged under 40
- **70%** of OAT clients were male
- **52% (30,059)** dosed at a community pharmacy; the remainder at public clinics, hospitals, correctional facilities, or other settings
- **65% (29,009)** were classified as ongoing treatment (continuing from the prior year); 15% (6,417) were new to treatment

The 57,754 figure is the denominator the system can see. But the system does not know — and cannot easily count — the people who need OAT and are not receiving it.

The most recent systematic analysis of unmet treatment need, Ritter et al. (2025) published in *Drug and Alcohol Review*, estimated that **less than a third to half of those who should receive treatment access it** in Australia for alcohol and other drug conditions. For opioid use disorder specifically, international estimates suggest OAT coverage rates typically reach 40–60% of those who would benefit. Applying conservative coverage assumptions to the 57,754 receiving treatment implies **50,000–100,000+ additional Australians with opioid use disorder who are not in treatment** — consistent with the Penington Institute's characterisation of "substantial and potentially growing unmet demand."

This gap has real consequences. Opioid overdose is now the leading cause of unintentional drug-related death in Australia, with opioids involved in approximately 2 in 5 of the record 2,091 unintentional overdose deaths in 2022 (see Report 057). People outside OAT face dramatically higher overdose risk; OAT is, in a direct sense, a life-saving intervention being denied to tens of thousands of people.

### 2. The Prescriber Shortage: 3,357 Accredited Practitioners Serving a Nation

There were **3,357 authorised opioid pharmacotherapy prescribers** in Australia in 2025 — down from 3,384 in 2024. While the number appears stable, this headline conceals structural fragility:

- **82% (approximately 2,763)** work in the private sector — primarily private general practices
- Around two-thirds (64% or 2,176) are authorised to prescribe more than one OAT medicine
- The remainder are either restricted to buprenorphine-only or methadone-only prescribing authority

Victoria illustrates the structural problem most acutely. Despite having approximately 26% of Australia's population, Victoria recorded only **14,456 OAT patients** in June 2025 — significantly below NSW's **25,082** despite similar or higher rates of illicit opioid use. Victoria is experiencing a prescriber shortage driven by:
1. **Ageing prescriber base** — when high-patient-load prescribers retire or come under regulatory scrutiny, their patients must be urgently redistributed across a limited remaining network, creating "mini-crises"
2. **Medicare reimbursement inadequacy** — MBS item rates for pharmacotherapy management do not reflect the complexity and time required, particularly during induction (the highest-risk period), making OAT economically unviable for many GPs compared to standard consultations
3. **Training and stigma** — opioid dependence receives inadequate attention in medical and nursing education; accreditation requires a specific training course (one-day hybrid workshop, five times per year) plus supervised placement, creating a formal bottleneck

In rural and regional areas, the GP-as-prescriber model is particularly vulnerable. A Barriers to Accessing Opioid Treatment in Rural Queensland study (Wang et al., *Drug and Alcohol Review*, 2026) documented how geographic isolation combines with prescriber scarcity and limited community pharmacy dosing points to create severe access barriers for patients in smaller communities — barriers that daily or twice-daily dosing requirements for methadone and sublingual buprenorphine make almost insurmountable for anyone living more than 30–60 minutes from a dosing point.

### 3. Long-Acting Injectable Buprenorphine: The Reform That Is Working — and About to Lose Half Its Products

Long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB) represents the most significant clinical innovation in opioid treatment in decades. By replacing daily or twice-daily pharmacy visits with weekly or monthly injections, LAIB removes the most significant structural barrier to treatment access: the requirement to be at a pharmacy at a fixed time, every day, while managing withdrawal, employment, childcare, and the other pressures of dependency recovery.

LAIB was listed on the PBS in **September 2019** and was expanded to non-specialist prescribers in April 2020. By June 2025, LAIB had become the **dominant formulation in several jurisdictions**:
- Tasmania: 57% of OAT clients
- Northern Territory: 58% of OAT clients
- South Australia: 39% of OAT clients
- Queensland: 42% of OAT clients

The dominance of LAIB in the NT and Tasmania is not coincidental — these are the jurisdictions where daily pharmacy visits are most logistically difficult. LAIB has been transformative in enabling access for rural, remote, and peripatetic patients.

A critical 2025 clinical development was the regulatory approval of **direct induction onto low-dose weekly LAIB** — removing the prior requirement that patients first stabilise on sublingual buprenorphine before transitioning to injectable. This substantially simplifies the initiation pathway and removes a significant clinical barrier.

However, the market for LAIB in Australia is now facing disruption. **Sublocade** (Indivior), one of two available LAIB products, will be **withdrawn from the PBS on 31 December 2026**. Indivior's commercial decision — citing high local costs against relatively low patient numbers — will affect a substantial patient cohort: PBS data show **15,553 Sublocade 100mg prescriptions** and **26,827 Sublocade 300mg prescriptions** dispensed in the 12 months to April 2026.

The withdrawal is not caused by any safety or efficacy concern. But Sublocade and Buvidal (the remaining product, made by Camurus in weekly and monthly formulations) have different pharmacokinetic profiles — they are not directly clinically interchangeable for all patients. Addiction medicine specialists have characterised the withdrawal as "a disaster," with patients who have achieved exceptional stability on Sublocade facing potential disruption at a particularly vulnerable period in their recovery.

NSW Health has published guidance on transition pathways, and Services Australia has confirmed PBS claims for Sublocade will be accepted until 31 December 2026. But for patients and prescribers navigating an already fragile system, the withdrawal adds significant clinical and administrative burden.

### 4. OAT in Prisons: 30%+ Opioid Dependence, Near-Zero Treatment Coverage

Australian prisons present an acute case study in OAT access failure. The AIHW and other sources estimate:
- More than **40,000 people** are incarcerated in Australia on any given day
- Surveys of prison entrants show **more than 65% had used illicit drugs in the previous year**
- **More than 50%** of incarcerated people have substance dependence
- **More than 30%** have opioid dependence

Despite these numbers, OAT access in Australian prisons has historically been severely constrained — and in some jurisdictions, effectively unavailable — for a combination of security concerns, jurisdictional cost-shifting, and a clinical culture that has often viewed prison as an opportunity for "forced detoxification" rather than treatment.

The consequences are lethal. **Overdose is the leading cause of death among people released from prison in Australia** — a phenomenon documented globally and consistent across Australian states. The mechanism is well understood: enforced abstinence during incarceration causes rapid opioid tolerance loss; upon release, people who return to using their pre-incarceration dose face a dramatically elevated overdose risk because their tolerance has dropped.

In March 2025, the *Medical Journal of Australia* published a landmark **National Consensus Statement on Opioid Agonist Treatment in Custodial Settings** (Chan et al.), derived from a modified Delphi process among a national multidisciplinary expert panel. The statement contains **19 recommendations** across five domains:
1. **Induction or continuation of OAT** — OAT should be offered on entry to custody; existing OAT should continue without interruption
2. **OAT options and administration** — all approved OAT formulations, including LAIB, should be available in custodial settings; LAIB is particularly appropriate where daily dosing creates administration challenges
3. **Transition of care to community** — structured warm handover to community prescribers; ideally beginning LAIB before release to bridge the highest-risk transition period
4. **Special populations** — pregnant incarcerated women; people with concurrent mental health conditions; First Nations people
5. **Organisational support** — health authority responsibility for OAT provision in prisons regardless of jurisdiction

The consensus statement explicitly invokes alignment with the **UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (Mandela Rules)**, which require health care in prisons to be equivalent to that available in the community. On this standard, Australian prisons are failing — comprehensively.

A cost-effectiveness model published in the *Medical Journal of Australia* (Appendix to Report 057 context) estimated that prison LAIB programs could prevent post-release overdose deaths at highly favourable cost-effectiveness ratios. The clinical case for universal OAT in Australian prisons is now well-established; the gap is political and structural.

### 5. The Geographic Access Problem LAIB Has Partially Solved — and What Remains

The July 2023 PBS reform combined with LAIB expansion has measurably improved access. For rural and remote patients, LAIB means the treatment pathway no longer requires daily pharmacy visits — a weekly or monthly injection can be administered by a GP, nurse practitioner, or nurse, at any location with appropriate training and storage.

This is particularly important given Australia's modified Monash classification: for patients in MMM4–7 communities (outer regional to very remote), daily supervised dosing at a community pharmacy may require round trips of 2–4 hours or more. LAIB transforms this into a once-weekly or monthly engagement.

However, barriers remain:
- **Prescriber scarcity** in rural areas means even monthly injections require a willing and accredited local practitioner; the accreditation process (one-day training plus supervised placement) has limited rural uptake
- **Cold chain requirements** for LAIB injections (both Buvidal and Sublocade require refrigeration) create logistics challenges in communities with limited pharmacy infrastructure
- **First Nations communities** face compounding barriers: the high disease burden documented in Report 057 (23% of NSP attendees), cultural safety concerns about mainstream services, and geographic remoteness converging to make community-controlled health service delivery the most appropriate model — but funding for ACCHO-delivered OAT remains fragmented

A 2025 study (Conigrave et al., *Drug and Alcohol Review*) on OAT accessibility for priority populations in NSW identified particular gaps for people experiencing homelessness, people in rural and remote areas, First Nations people, and women — noting that these groups face compounding barriers that standardised program design does not adequately address.

### 6. The Research Gap: What the July 2023 PBS Reform Is Now Making Possible

The listing of OAT medicines on the PBS in July 2023 created, for the first time, a **national individual-level claims dataset** for opioid pharmacotherapy. Previously, NOPSAD data were collected through state and territory program records — giving patient counts but no linkage to Medicare or PBS claims, diagnoses, comorbidities, or health service use patterns.

This data infrastructure is the foundation of the NHMRC-funded Monash project: a **$1.5 million Targeted Call for Research grant** awarded to Professor Suzanne Nielsen (Monash Addiction Research Centre, Eastern Health Clinical School) to use co-design approaches with people lived experience of opioid dependence, working with national PBS data to:
- Identify who is and isn't accessing treatment (by demographics, geography, and clinical characteristics)
- Map the patterns of treatment initiation, continuation, and interruption
- Develop and test innovative care models to address gaps

The project represents the first systematic opportunity to use linked national data to understand OAT access at the individual level. Results are several years away, but the dataset itself is now being built with each PBS prescription claimed.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Can the prescriber base grow fast enough?** The 3,357 authorised prescribers have changed little over several years while the evidence for treatment need has grown. The Medicare reimbursement problem is structural: an OAT initiation consultation is complex and high-risk, but MBS item rates reward time and throughput, not complexity for low-status patient groups. Without a specific MBS item for OAT management that reflects the clinical burden, prescriber growth will remain sluggish.

**2. Is the July 2023 PBS reform actually reaching unserved populations?** The reform reduced cost barriers for people already in treatment. But the people who most need to enter treatment are not primarily deterred by the dispensing fee — they are deterred by stigma, difficulty navigating prescriber accreditation, incarceration, homelessness, and complex comorbidities. The reform improved the economics of treatment without necessarily expanding reach.

**3. Who should hold responsibility for prison OAT?** The national consensus statement recommends that health authorities — not correctional services — hold responsibility for OAT provision in prisons. This is a cost-shifting argument between state health and corrective services budgets, a jurisdictional negotiation between Commonwealth (who funds PBS medicines) and states (who fund correctional health), and a values question about whether prisoners are owed equivalent healthcare. None of these tensions have been resolved, and they will determine whether the 2025 consensus statement becomes a standard of care or a document without implementation.

**4. The Sublocade cliff and product concentration risk.** After December 2026, Australia's LAIB market will consist of a single product: Buvidal (Camurus). This creates a single-supplier situation for a critical medication category. While Camurus has given no indication of market exit, the Sublocade experience demonstrates that commercial decisions can withdraw medicines from the market without clinical justification. The government may need to consider procurement arrangements that reduce single-supplier dependency.

**5. The National Drug Strategy and OAT's place in it.** The 2017–2026 National Drug Strategy nominally includes OAT within its "demand reduction" pillar. But as Report 057 documented, the Strategy contains no binding funding commitments. A successor Strategy that includes explicit OAT access targets — coverage rates, prescriber-to-patient ratios, prison OAT availability — would give the reform program a policy anchor that currently does not exist.
