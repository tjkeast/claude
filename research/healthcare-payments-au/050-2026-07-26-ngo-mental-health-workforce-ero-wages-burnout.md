# The NGO Mental Health Workforce: Wages, Burnout, and the SCHADS Award Overhaul

**Report 050 — 2026-07-26**

**Summary:** Australia's community-managed mental health sector depends on a workforce of roughly 30,000+ people — predominantly women, many on fixed-term contracts — paid under the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services (SCHADS) Industry Award. A June 2026 Fair Work Commission decision found longstanding gender-based undervaluation of this work and restructured the entire award, mandating increases of up to 27% for some classifications from late 2026. But state governments are indexing NGO block grants at rates well below the new wage floor, creating a structural funding gap that could force service cuts precisely when the sector is being asked to do more.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 049 showed that Australia's community-managed mental health NGO sector receives only ~7% of total government mental health spending — and operates on short-term block grants that cause chronic workforce instability. The workforce that delivers those services is the thread this report follows: who they are, what they earn, and why a landmark Fair Work Commission decision on gender undervaluation is colliding with government indexation inadequacy.

---

## Background

The mental health NGO sector — the organisations that deliver psychosocial recovery coaching, peer support, housing assistance, drop-in services, and community outreach for Australians with serious mental illness — is fundamentally a workforce question. Capital is not the constraint. Unlike hospitals, which require large infrastructure investment, community-managed mental health services are almost entirely people: the workers who build relationships with the people they support, who conduct outreach, who help with tenancy or employment, who sit with someone in crisis.

The workforce that does this work is governed by the **Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award 2010 (SCHADS Award, MA000100)**, the minimum wage and conditions instrument that covers more than 250,000 workers across Australia's community services sector. Mental health support workers, psychosocial recovery coaches, peer workers, case managers, and crisis workers in community organisations are predominantly SCHADS employees.

That workforce has been structurally undervalued since Australia's welfare state was built. The reason is not subtle: it is a female-dominated sector, historically regarded as "care" rather than "skilled work," and compensated accordingly. The Fair Work Commission acknowledged this in 2012 and ordered corrections. It has now done so again — this time more comprehensively.

## Key Findings

### 1. The SCHADS Award and the Equal Remuneration Order: A History of Catch-Up

In 2012, the **Australian Services Union (ASU)** successfully argued before the Fair Work Commission that SCHADS-covered workers had been systematically underpaid because the majority of workers in the sector were female and their work had been undervalued relative to comparable male-dominated industries. The Commission agreed and issued an **Equal Remuneration Order (ERO)**: staged increases of **19–41%** above award rates, phased in from 1 December 2012 to 1 December 2020.

The ERO was landmark. It acknowledged that low pay in community services was not a market outcome but the product of structural gender discrimination — the same work, the same skill, simply undervalued because women did it. The staged implementation was designed to allow governments, as primary funders of the sector, to absorb the cost incrementally.

But the ERO had limits:
- It applied only to social and community services classifications — not to home care disability workers (Schedule E) who remained at a different rate
- It operated as a separate supplement *on top of* underlying award rates, creating administrative complexity
- It did not eliminate the pay gap with equivalent public sector workers — it narrowed it

By 2023–24, the ERO had been absorbed into SCHADS Award rates, but the underlying classification structure — five separate Schedules (B, C, E, F) with inconsistent pay points — had become unwieldy and was no longer fit for purpose.

### 2. The June 2026 FWC Decision: A Second Landmark

On **1 June 2026**, the Fair Work Commission handed down its final decision in the **Gender-based Undervaluation Priority Awards Review** — a two-year process examining whether SCHADS workers continued to experience undervaluation after the ERO's completion.

The Commission found: yes, they did.

The decision made fundamental structural changes:

| Change | Details |
|--------|---------|
| New classification structure | Five fragmented Schedules (B, C, E, F) replaced by a single **Final Classification Structure (FCS)** with 9 classification levels |
| ERO status | ERO removed; undervaluation correction embedded into base rates |
| Social and community services (SACS) workers | Wage adjustments **ranging from -1% to +17%** depending on classification level |
| Schedule E (disability home care) | Interim increase of approximately **15%** from 1 October 2026 |
| Full FCS implementation | 1 October 2027 for most workers; 1 October 2026 for Schedule E |
| Annual wage review (July 2026) | Separate **4.75% increase** to all SCHADS minimum rates from 1 July 2026 |
| Some disability support worker classifications | Increases of **up to 27%** |

The decision is significant for several reasons. It acknowledges that the 2012 ERO did not fully correct gender-based undervaluation — after a decade of phased increases, structural pay gaps remained. It also consolidates a fragmented award structure that had become a compliance burden for employers and an equity barrier for workers who could not easily move between roles.

The June 2026 decision effectively removes the ERO as a separate legal instrument and bakes equity corrections into the core award, which should be more durable — but the transition creates an immediate funding problem.

### 3. The Funding Gap: Government Indexation vs Mandatory Wage Obligations

The community mental health sector's funding flows primarily from government through block grants — PHN mental health flexible funding, state health department contracts, CPSP arrangements — that are indexed annually by a formula set by the relevant funder.

The problem is stark: the mandatory wage increase from 1 July 2026 (**4.75%**) is running ahead of government indexation rates:

- **Queensland** approved NGO indexation of **3.31%** for 2026-27 — a gap of **1.44 percentage points** against the wage floor alone, before accounting for the classification restructure increases flowing from October 2026 and 2027
- **NDIS providers** delivering disability support work received no equivalent increase in their support prices to fund the 4.75% SCHADS rise, despite their workers' legal obligation to receive it
- **Victoria** and **NSW** are similarly exposed, with their state-funded community sector contracts indexed below the wage outcome

The Queensland Alliance for Mental Health (QAMH), which represents 140+ NGO mental health organisations, was explicit: **"Providers must receive adequate funding to meet increased Award obligations, as community-based mental health services cannot absorb rising wage costs within existing funding arrangements."**

Peak COSS bodies across Australia (VCOSS, NCOSS, TasCOSS, QAMH) have all advocated for state and Commonwealth governments to fund the increases in full. NCOSS committed to "do all we can to ensure the NSW Government fully covers the cost of this vital increase to worker pay." But advocacy commitments are not funding guarantees, and the sector's characteristic position — legally required to pay workers more, contractually restricted to grant amounts set before the wage decision — leaves NGOs to absorb the gap from reserves or reduce service volumes.

### 4. Who Is the Workforce?

The community-managed mental health workforce is structurally distinct from the clinical workforce:

- **NSW alone** has approximately **5,000 paid workers** in community-managed mental health organisations, representing almost 25% of the state's total mental health workforce (MHCC, 2023)
- The workforce is **female dominated** — reflecting decades of care work being gendered and undervalued
- **Peer workers** — people with lived experience of mental illness employed to support others — are now the **second-largest occupational group** in NSW's community-managed sector, comprising **12.3% of all workers**
- The workforce is younger and more highly qualified than Australia's general workforce, but earns significantly less than equivalent public sector mental health workers
- **Allied health workers** in community-managed organisations almost doubled between 2021 and 2023 (MHCC), reflecting rapid growth in the role, but wage relativities with clinical counterparts remain unfavourable

The highest qualification available to peer workers is the **Certificate IV in Mental Health Peer Work** — below the level of any registered health profession — which restricts their position descriptions, pay grades, and career progression within the award framework.

For non-peer clinical staff (social workers, occupational therapists, psychologists), the gap between a community-sector position and a public health position can be significant in career terms — public sector enterprise agreements, in states like Victoria with the 2025-28 Mental Health Services EBA, can run well above the SCHADS floor even after the new increases.

### 5. Burnout and Turnover: The Real Cost of Underfunding

The structural underfunding of NGO wages compounds with working conditions to produce chronic workforce instability:

- Disability support worker turnover: **17–25% annually** — roughly one in five workers leaves each year
- In a 2025 survey of community service workers, **high workload and burnout** were the second-most common reason for leaving (behind only remuneration)
- Over **60% of disability support workers** reported experiencing burnout in 2024
- Short-term fixed-term contracts — the dominant model in block-grant funded NGOs — prevent career development, limit training investment, and make workers structurally less secure than their public sector counterparts

High turnover is not merely an administrative problem. In mental health support — where therapeutic relationships, trust, and continuity of care drive outcomes — turnover directly harms the people receiving support. When a recovery coach leaves mid-program, the person they support must rebuild trust with a replacement worker, often missing months of effective support in the gap.

### 6. The Government Funding Obligation: Who Pays?

The FWC's SCHADS decision creates legal obligations on employers — but the community sector cannot generate revenue independently. Its income is government grants. The question of who actually pays for the wage increases is therefore a policy question, not a market one.

The Commonwealth and states co-fund the community mental health sector through multiple streams. When the Fair Work Commission raises award wages, the cost flows through to the sector within weeks. But government funding decisions — budget allocations, indexation formulas — move on annual cycles, and indexation rates are often set well before the annual wage review decision is known.

This creates a systematic lag: **wages go up in July, funding was set in April based on earlier forecasts**. Each year, the sector absorbs the gap or reduces services. The 2026 gap is particularly severe because the June 2026 restructure decision added additional classification increases *on top of* the July annual wage review — a double movement with only one (inadequate) indexation adjustment.

The ACOSS network's October 2025 joint submission to the FWC called on the Commission to sequence its decisions to allow government funding cycles to respond — but this addresses timing only, not the structural underfunding problem. The 2026-27 Queensland indexation rate of 3.31% was set on earlier, lower CPI forecasts; QAMH formally wrote to the Treasurer requesting a CPI-aligned adjustment, but the outcome is not yet publicly confirmed.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Who absorbs the gap?** If governments do not fund the SCHADS increases in full and on schedule, NGOs will face an unresolvable squeeze: reduce staff, reduce hours, reduce services, or cross-subsidise from reserves they rarely have. The sector's financial margins are thin.

**2. Classification transition risk.** The 9-level Final Classification Structure replaces five different schedules — every current employee must be mapped to a new classification level. For small NGOs without dedicated HR capacity, this is a major compliance burden arriving simultaneously with a funding squeeze.

**3. The ERO removal and legal certainty.** The ERO was a discrete legal order. Its removal and replacement with embedded base rates is conceptually cleaner, but means future undervaluation claims must be pursued through general equal remuneration or award review processes — not a dedicated order mechanism. The durability of the correction depends on future award reviews maintaining the corrected rates.

**4. Peer worker pay equity.** The Certificate IV qualification ceiling for peer workers restricts their award classification regardless of experience. A peer worker with 15 years in the sector cannot access the higher classification levels available to diploma or degree-qualified workers. This structural ceiling drives experienced peer workers out of the sector — the opposite of what a quality, recovery-oriented system requires.

**5. The public-private wage gap.** Even after the June 2026 increases, community-managed mental health workers will earn significantly less than equivalent public sector workers covered by state-level enterprise agreements. Victoria's 2025-28 Mental Health Services EBA — covering public sector mental health nurses and allied health — set rates meaningfully above the SCHADS Award floor. This differential makes the community sector a training ground: workers build experience in NGOs, then move to public sector positions with better pay, stability, and career progression.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Peer support workers and the MBS exclusion** — peer workers cannot bill Medicare for their services; their work is funded entirely through block grants. The case for MBS inclusion, the barriers, and what the evidence says about peer work outcomes compared with clinical services.
2. **The Medicare Levy and Commonwealth health hypothecation** — is Australia's 2% income-based Medicare Levy actually funding healthcare, or is it consolidated revenue by another name? Why Australia chose a levy over an insurance model, and what this means for funding the community sector.
3. **Housing as a mental health payment question** — housing is the largest unmet need for Australians with serious mental illness; the intersection of mental health funding with NRAS, state public housing, NDIA, and the question of whether housing support belongs in health budgets.
4. **The Fifth National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan: accountability** — what the Plan committed to, what the National Mental Health Commission has measured, and whether there is any genuine accountability for non-delivery.
5. **NGO sector financial governance and reserves adequacy** — how community mental health organisations manage the structural gap between fixed grant revenue and rising wage costs; audit findings on financial sustainability; the role of DGR status and philanthropy in bridging the gap.
