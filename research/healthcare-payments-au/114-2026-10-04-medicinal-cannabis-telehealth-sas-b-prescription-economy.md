# Medicinal Cannabis: Australia's Other $1 Billion Telehealth Prescription Economy

**One-line summary:** Australia's medicinal cannabis market has reached AUD $1 billion in annual spend, driven by the same direct-to-consumer telehealth platform architecture as GLP-1 obesity medicines — but with a structural difference that may be harder to fix: almost all products remain unapproved by the TGA, prescribed through an access pathway now described by the regulator itself as "not fit-for-purpose."

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 113 examined how Australia's GLP-1 prescription economy is built on telehealth platforms operating outside the PBS and traditional GP system. Medicinal cannabis is the original and larger version of that model — the dominant use case for the Special Access Scheme, with ~80% of all SAS-B applications, a market exceeding $1 billion annually, and the same concerns about asynchronous prescribing, vertical integration, and clinical governance that now define the GLP-1 debate.

---

## Background

Australia legalised medicinal cannabis for therapeutic use in February 2016 under the Narcotic Drugs Amendment Act 2016. The framework created three pathways for patients to access cannabis products not registered on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG): the Special Access Scheme Category B (SAS-B, for individual patient access approved by the TGA), the Authorised Prescriber (AP) scheme (for practitioners approved to prescribe to a class of patients without per-patient TGA approval), and the Clinical Trials pathway.

The SAS pathway was designed for exceptional clinical circumstances — unapproved products not yet through the full registration process, prescribed where no registered alternative existed. For most therapeutic goods, this remains an edge case. For medicinal cannabis, it became the mainstream. As of 2024, medicinal cannabis accounts for an estimated 80% or more of all SAS-B applications processed by the TGA, consuming a regulatory infrastructure designed for rare individual cases.

The result is a parallel pharmaceutical economy: over one million Australians have accessed medicinal cannabis prescriptions since 2016, with monthly volumes exceeding 80,000 approvals by 2024, almost entirely through telehealth platforms operating outside the Medicare and PBS frameworks.

## Key Findings

### 1. Scale and Growth: The Numbers

The TGA published cumulative SAS-B data through 2024. The growth is without precedent in Australian pharmaceutical access history:

- **2020**: 57,711 SAS-B approvals for medicinal cannabis
- **2021**: 86,400+
- **2022**: 119,000+
- **2023**: 156,000+
- **2024**: 177,762 — a threefold increase in four years
- **January–February 2025**: 33,520 — a further 47% increase year-on-year
- **Full-year 2025**: estimated 224,000+ SAS-B approvals (up ~26% on 2024)

Total prescriptions since 2016 (SAS-B and AP combined) now exceed **3.6 million**.

The market has crossed **AUD $1 billion** in annual revenue by 2025 (Prohibition Partners; Grand View Research), making medicinal cannabis Australia's largest non-PBS prescription medicine category by patient spend. Market research projects continued growth to AUD $1.17 billion in 2026 and potentially $4+ billion by 2035 at current trajectories.

Over 1,000 individual products are now available in Australia through SAS pathways. Almost none hold full ARTG registration — meaning they have not been assessed by the TGA for safety, quality, or efficacy.

### 2. The Telehealth Platform Market

The medicinal cannabis access market is dominated by vertically integrated telehealth clinics that combine consultation, prescription, and product supply in a single business model — an architecture almost identical to the GLP-1 telehealth platforms examined in Report 113.

**Montu / Alternaleaf** is the market leader. Montu (private, Sydney-founded 2020) operates:
- **Alternaleaf** — telehealth clinic serving **200,000+ active patients** (self-reported as of 2025), Australia's largest single medicinal cannabis prescriber
- **Saged** — clinician education and clinical support platform
- **UMeds** — product wholesaler and distributor to independent pharmacies

Alternaleaf advertises telehealth consultations starting from $79, with ongoing prescription management and product delivery integrated into the platform. Doctors employed by or contracting to Alternaleaf are specifically recruited for medicinal cannabis telehealth, with advertised rates of $360/hour for GP contract work.

Other significant platform operators include **Polln**, **CDA Clinics** (Clinical Defence Australia, which operates a network of dedicated medicinal cannabis clinics), **Medleaf Clinics**, and multiple smaller operations.

**Vitura Health** (ASX-listed) is the only major publicly-listed entity in the ecosystem. Vitura operates CannView pharmacy-distribution platform and holds a significant share of the wholesale supply chain.

The CHOICE consumer group investigated the model in 2024, documenting "5-minute consults" — patients describing appointments lasting less than five minutes, with prescriptions issued after minimal clinical assessment. The CHOICE investigation found that some clinics were co-owned by product sponsors — creating vertically integrated models where the prescribing platform has a financial interest in which product is prescribed.

### 3. The Prescribing Conditions: Evidence vs. Practice

The three most common indications for which SAS-B approvals are granted are:

1. **Chronic pain** (~42% of approvals, 2025)
2. **Anxiety** (~33% of approvals)
3. **Sleep disorders** (~14% of approvals)

Post-traumatic stress disorder accounts for approximately 2%, with the remainder distributed across oncology, palliative care, epilepsy, and other indications.

**The evidence question is central and contested.**

- **Chronic pain**: The TGA has stated that there is "limited evidence medicinal cannabis provides clinically significant pain relief for many conditions," and that it should only be considered when standard therapies have failed. A 2025 Cochrane-level systematic review found moderate-quality evidence that cannabinoids modestly reduce chronic pain scores (NRS reduction ~0.5/10) compared to placebo, but with significant adverse event profiles — dizziness, cognitive effects, risk of dependency — at higher THC concentrations.
- **Anxiety**: The evidence picture is explicitly described by Monash University researchers (Lens, October 2025) as "mixed and contested." For THC-dominant products, clinical evidence is ambiguous and some trials show symptom worsening. For CBD, evidence is emerging but insufficient to support first-line prescribing. Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome is an established adverse outcome at sustained high-THC use.
- **Sleep disorders**: Short-term evidence is more positive for CBD-dominant products in reducing sleep latency, but long-term data on THC-dominant products raises tolerance and rebound insomnia concerns.

The RACGP's 2024 position statement is cautious: it recommends medicinal cannabis only as an adjunct to established therapies, only when prescribed by a GP with a comprehensive clinical relationship, and not as a first-line treatment for pain, anxiety, or sleep.

**The structural problem is timing**: the three most common indications (pain, anxiety, sleep) are precisely the indications with the weakest evidence for efficacy — yet they account for approximately 90% of SAS-B prescribing volume. The evidence does not drive the market; the market has outrun the evidence.

### 4. Product Risk: The High-THC Safety Problem

In February 2025, THC-dominant products represented 52% of all SAS-B approvals — the majority of a market that was, in 2020, predominantly CBD-dominant.

This product shift has safety implications. An analysis of voluntary adverse event reports to the TGA for three years from mid-2022 found that **half of all medicinal cannabis adverse event case reports were related to higher-THC products**. Documented adverse events include:
- Acute cannabis-induced psychosis
- Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (cyclical vomiting requiring emergency care)
- Suicidal ideation, behaviour, and attempts
- Accidental paediatric consumption (products delivered to households)

The Australian Medical Association (Queensland) submitted to the TGA's 2025 review that cannabis products are being prescribed "at alarming rates through direct-to-consumer telehealth models," and called for mandatory face-to-face consultation requirements for high-THC products specifically.

### 5. The Regulatory Trajectory: A Framework Not Fit-For-Purpose

In August 2025, the TGA opened a formal consultation: "Reviewing the Safety and Regulatory Oversight of Unapproved Medicinal Cannabis Products." This was the most significant regulatory challenge to the current model since 2016. The consultation received **790 submissions** — an extraordinary volume reflecting the size of the stakeholder community — from clinicians, professional colleges, pharmacists, patient groups, industry, and government.

The TGA published its consultation response on **20 February 2026**. The headline finding was blunt: there is **broad agreement that the current access framework is not fit-for-purpose** and not proportionate to the potential safety and quality risks associated with unapproved medicinal cannabis products.

Key stakeholder positions:
- **TGA**: Current framework (SAS for a product now prescribed at 177,000+ instances/year) designed for exceptional circumstances, not mass-market prescribing
- **Medical professional bodies (RACGP, AMA, PSA)**: Major concerns about prescribing without evidence, commercial conflicts, minimal post-market safety surveillance
- **Pharmacists (PSA)**: Backed TGA action, urged broader regulatory reform including mandatory product registration and pharmacist counselling requirements
- **Industry (Montu, CDA, product sponsors)**: Broadly supported reform "with careful consideration of patient access impacts"
- **Patient advocates**: Supported access but raised concerns about cost and potential restriction

**Reform directions under active consideration** as of mid-2026:
1. **Removing medicinal cannabis from the SAS pathway** and creating a dedicated scheduled access model, either under the TGA or the Therapeutic Goods (Medicinal Cannabis) Act
2. **Mandatory ARTG registration** for THC-dominant products within a defined transition period, incentivising manufacturers to conduct clinical trials
3. **Clinical practice standards** with real-time consultation requirements for all high-THC prescriptions (consistent with AHPRA October 2025 all-boards guidance)
4. **Strengthened post-market safety surveillance** and mandatory adverse event reporting
5. **Advertising enforcement**: The TGA issued $118,800 in infringement notices to Dispensed Pty Ltd (September 2025) and $99,000 to ACPharm Queensland (November 2025) for unlawful advertising of prescription medicinal cannabis products to the public

A formal reform pathway had not been legislated or announced as of October 2026, with the Medicinal Cannabis Expert Working Group (MCEWG) continuing deliberations. Its seventh meeting was held 25 February 2026 in Canberra.

### 6. The PBS Question: Why Almost Nothing Is Subsidised

Australia's Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme covers a single medicinal cannabis product: **Epidyolex® (cannabidiol 100mg/mL oral solution)**, listed for Dravet syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (severe childhood-onset epilepsies). PBS-subsidised patients pay $41.30/month (general) or $6.60 (concessional), saving eligible patients approximately $24,000/year compared to out-of-pocket cost.

**All other medicinal cannabis products — including every product prescribed through the SAS-B pathway — are unsubsidised.** Out-of-pocket costs are typically $150–$600/month, depending on product type, formulation, and dosing. For patients with chronic pain using high-THC oils or flower, $400–$500/month is common.

The PBAC has declined to recommend PBS listing for broader indications, citing:
- Insufficient clinical evidence for cost-effectiveness assessments
- Unapproved product status (no registered product to assess)
- Absence of comparative effectiveness data against established alternatives

The PBAC's evidence bar is not set arbitrarily high — the same evidence-of-efficacy requirement that prevents PBS listing is also what clinicians and the TGA itself have said is missing from the prescribing landscape. The PBS mechanism is functioning correctly; the clinical trial infrastructure that would generate the evidence has not been built.

In the 2025 federal election, the Australian Greens proposed $10 million over two years to establish a working group investigating PBS listing of medicinal cannabis, with implementation targeted from 1 July 2026. As of October 2026, no such working group has been formally established and no additional PBS listings have proceeded.

### 7. The Equity Inversion

The equity structure of medicinal cannabis access replicates the GLP-1 problem documented in Report 112 and Report 113.

Chronic pain — the dominant indication (~42% of SAS-B volume) — is disproportionately prevalent in lower-socioeconomic populations, regional and remote communities, and First Nations Australians: precisely the populations with the least capacity to sustain $200–$500/month out-of-pocket costs. Workers with occupational injuries, pensioners with musculoskeletal conditions, and regional patients with limited GP access are being prescribed through telehealth platforms at full out-of-pocket cost, in a framework that the TGA acknowledges is not adequately regulated.

Meanwhile, the platform architecture that has made medicinal cannabis accessible — telehealth-based, nationally available, no waitlist — is exactly what conventional healthcare channels cannot provide for remote or underserved patients. Tightening access risks excluding the patients the system has reached; keeping the current framework perpetuates an inequitable, evidence-light mass-market prescribing model.

The equity inversion is stark: medicinal cannabis is most accessible to digital-literate, urban patients with disposable income and credit cards. The populations most affected by the conditions it is prescribed for cannot afford the subscription model and lack the health literacy to navigate a telehealth platform.

## Tensions and Open Questions

1. **The access vs. safety paradox**: Restricting SAS-B or requiring ARTG registration could reduce patient access to products that, whatever the quality of evidence, have community-reported therapeutic benefit. The patient advocacy community is vocal; the clinical reform agenda is also legitimate.

2. **Evidence can't be generated without access**: The PBAC needs clinical trial data to list products on the PBS; generating clinical trial data requires controlled access and research infrastructure; but the commercial market is already at 1,000+ products and $1B+ in annual spend. Who funds trials that will undercut commercial prices?

3. **Vertical integration and prescribing independence**: When a telehealth clinic is co-owned or commercially partnered with a product sponsor, the prescriber has a financial interest in which product is recommended. No current Australian regulatory framework explicitly prohibits this structure.

4. **The post-AHPRA landscape**: The October 2025 AHPRA all-boards ban on asynchronous questionnaire-only prescribing applies equally to medicinal cannabis. Platforms that had used nurse practitioners or pharmacist prescribers to circumvent the 2023 Medical Board guidance are now nominally subject to the same real-time consultation requirement. Enforcement remains the question.

5. **TGA reform timing vs. market entrenchment**: With 200,000 patients served by Alternaleaf alone, any reform that disrupts the access pathway will have immediate political consequences. The TGA's 790-submission consultation reflects how large and organised the patient and industry constituency has become.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Medicinal cannabis and workplace drug testing** — the interaction between SAS-B prescriptions, employer drug testing policies, and Workers' Compensation frameworks creates a distinct payment-system tension: patients prescribed legal cannabis by a TGA-approved pathway may face employment consequences, and neither the TGA framework nor WorkCover addresses the overlap.

2. **Pharmaceutical company vertical integration in the cannabis supply chain** — several major players in Australia's medicinal cannabis market are manufacturer-clinic combinations; the governance question of who controls prescribing when the prescriber is employed by the product manufacturer.

3. **Epidyolex and the PBS evidence pathway as a template** — how Epidyolex became the only PBS-listed cannabis product (severe paediatric epilepsy, clear evidence base, registered product, PBAC submission with RCT data) as a model for what PBS listing for other indications would require.

4. **GLP-1 meets cannabis: compounding ban and broader TGA regulatory stance** — the October 2024 TGA compounding ban on GLP-1 products and the emerging medicinal cannabis framework reform share the same regulatory logic; how the TGA is developing a consistent approach to large-scale unapproved product markets.

5. **State-level variations in medicinal cannabis access** — Queensland's 2021 amendments allowing nurse practitioners to prescribe with SAS-B approval, and how state-level prescribing rules interact with the federal TGA framework.
