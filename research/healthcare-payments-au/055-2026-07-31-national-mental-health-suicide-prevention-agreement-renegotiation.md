# The National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement: Five Years of Fragmentation, a Damning Review, and the Race to Rebuild Before 2027

**Report 055 — 2026-07-31**

**Summary:** The National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement — the $1.8 billion intergovernmental compact that was supposed to coordinate Australia's mental health and suicide prevention system — has been found "not fit for purpose" by the Productivity Commission, which released its final report in November 2025. The Agreement expires on 30 June 2027, the same month as the Community Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP) cliff. Governments have bought one year of extension time and committed to negotiating a new architecture — including an unprecedented First Ministers' Mental Health Declaration — but the deep structural tensions that made the current Agreement ineffective remain unresolved. With 3,307 Australians dying by suicide in 2024, mental health prevalence at record highs, and 230,500 people with severe mental illness unable to access psychosocial supports, the stakes of getting the successor agreement right are not abstract.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 054 examined the Medicare Levy's "fiscal fiction" — the gap between the revenue raised in the name of health and what governments actually spend on the system — and flagged the National Mental Health Agreement's June 2027 expiry as the most immediate test of whether Commonwealth-state health funding architecture can be redesigned to address structural gaps, particularly in adult psychosocial supports.

---

## Background

### What the Agreement Is

The National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement (hereafter "the Agreement" or "NMHSPA") was signed on 8 March 2022 by the Commonwealth, all states, and all territories. It replaced a patchwork of predecessor arrangements and committed all governments to work in partnership to:

- Improve the mental health of all Australians
- Reduce the rate of suicide toward zero
- Ensure the sustainability and enhancement of mental health and suicide prevention services

The Agreement is structured around **five national priority areas** (with specific actions attached to each):

1. Suicide prevention
2. Mental health promotion, prevention and early intervention
3. People with severe and complex mental illness
4. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander mental health and suicide prevention
5. Reduction of stigma and discrimination

These national priorities are implemented through the main Agreement document and supported by **Bilateral Schedules** negotiated separately with each state and territory, which set out jurisdiction-specific commitments, investment priorities, and accountability measures.

The Bilateral Schedules total approximately **$1.8 billion over 2021-22 to 2025-26** — with the Commonwealth and states sharing the investment. Examples include:
- **Queensland:** Commonwealth $150.9 million, Queensland $109.5 million+ over five years (~$260 million combined)
- **Western Australia:** $61.5 million combined over four years from 2022-23
- **NSW:** NSW has the largest economy and correspondingly the largest bilateral schedule, though precise figures vary by program

The Agreement sits within a much larger spending envelope. Total Australian Government Health portfolio investment in mental health and suicide prevention reached **$6.8 billion in 2022-23** — a record at the time — while total national (Commonwealth + states + private sector) mental health spending reached **$13.8 billion in 2023-24** (AIHW). The $1.8 billion bilateral envelope is therefore approximately 13% of the total government mental health spend over that period — significant, but not the system's primary funding mechanism.

### Five Years of Performance: What the Agreement Was Supposed to Achieve

The Agreement was the successor to the Fifth National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan (2017-2022) — itself widely acknowledged to have failed. The Fifth Plan was endorsed by COAG in August 2017 and was to conclude in 2022, but its implementation was characterised by uneven accountability, limited measurement, and no enforcement mechanism. The Agreement was meant to fix these structural problems.

It has not.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Productivity Commission Verdict: "Not Fit for Purpose"

The Productivity Commission's review of the Agreement — commissioned under the Agreement itself, which required a review before a successor could be negotiated — released its final report on **17 October 2025** and tabled it in Parliament on **11 November 2025**.

The Commission's central finding was unambiguous: the Agreement **"is not effective"** and **"a new policy architecture is needed"** to deliver meaningful change to the mental health system and improve outcomes for Australians.

RANZCP President Dr Astha Tomar characterised the finding bluntly: "The Productivity Commission's bleak assessment confirms what RANZCP members have been telling us for years: despite three years of the current Agreement, the mental health system remains fragmented, under-resourced, and failing the people who need it most."

The Commission identified three core structural failures:

**Failure 1: Fragmentation.** The mental health and suicide prevention system remains a patchwork of Commonwealth, state, territory, private, and non-government services that do not function as a coherent system. Responsibilities overlap, accountability is unclear, and service navigation is difficult for consumers and their families. The Agreement has not materially reduced this fragmentation.

**Failure 2: Inaccessibility.** Services remain out of reach for many Australians — particularly those with the most severe and complex needs. The creation of the NDIS in 2013 produced a bifurcated system with a stark per-capita spending gap: approximately **$67,000 per NDIS participant with psychosocial disability** versus approximately **$3,350 per person** for those with mental illness outside the NDIS — a 20:1 ratio. Three-quarters of adults with a significant psychosocial disability have no formal support at all.

**Failure 3: Weak accountability.** The Agreement's monitoring and reporting mechanisms are insufficient. Annual progress reports are published, but they track activity (outputs) rather than outcomes. There is no mechanism to enforce compliance or impose consequences on governments that fail to deliver their bilateral schedule commitments. The Agreement has not improved the quality of data available to assess whether the system is performing.

### 2. The State of Mental Health in Australia (2024-25)

The context in which the Agreement is being renegotiated is one of genuine system stress:

**Suicide:** In 2024, **3,307 Australians died by suicide** — a preliminary age-standardised rate of **11.8 per 100,000 population**. Men account for 76.5% of suicide deaths (rate: 18.3/100,000); women: 5.5/100,000. Suicide rates in remote and very remote Australia are the highest in five years, at **20.5 to 24.6 per 100,000** — more than double the 9.9 to 10.9 rate in major cities. Every day, approximately nine Australians die by suicide and more than 150 attempt to take their own life. (ABS Causes of Death, November 2025, preliminary.)

**Mental health prevalence:** The NMHC 2024 Report Card documents record-high mental health prevalence, particularly among young women aged 16-24 (45.5% experiencing a mental health condition). Factors that influence mental health — financial stress, loneliness, discrimination — are not improving.

**Cost barriers:** In 2023-24, more than **one in five Australians** delayed or avoided seeing a health professional due to cost. For mental health professionals specifically, this figure rises to **nearly one in four (24.6%)** — among the highest access barriers in the system.

**Economic distress:** The proportion of Australians struggling to cope on their current income more than doubled, from 17.1% (November 2020) to 34.6% (January 2024). Australians' sense of control over events in their lives has declined from 75.8% (2019) to 71.3% (2023). Both are independent risk factors for mental ill-health.

**Unmet psychosocial need:** The PC review confirmed earlier estimates: **230,500 people with severe mental illness** and a further **263,100 people with moderate mental illness** would benefit from psychosocial supports but cannot access them. This is the "missing middle" population — too unwell for primary care, not eligible for the NDIS, and underserved by state community mental health services.

### 3. The Existing Agreement's Extension and the $272.1 Million Bridge

Following the PC's recommendations, all governments agreed to extend the current Agreement to **30 June 2027**. The Commonwealth committed **$206.8 million** to fund the Agreement extension, maintaining existing bilateral schedule investments while the new architecture is co-designed.

Simultaneously, the **Community Psychosocial Support Program (CPSP)** — which provides non-clinical psychosocial support to approximately 25,000 adults with severe mental illness who are not eligible for the NDIS — was extended with **$272.1 million from the 2024-25 MYEFO**, running through to 30 June 2027.

This creates a **double cliff on 30 June 2027**: both the Agreement and the CPSP expire simultaneously. The new architecture must be operational, and the psychosocial support funding model resolved, before that date. There is no indication of what happens if negotiations are not complete.

### 4. The June 2025 Health Ministers Meeting: What Governments Agreed

At a Special Health Ministers Meeting on **13 June 2025 in Melbourne**, all Commonwealth, state and territory Health and Mental Health Ministers agreed to key commitments for the renegotiation:

- **Psychosocial supports as a central priority:** Addressing unmet psychosocial needs will be one of the central priorities in the next Agreement. This is the first time all jurisdictions have explicitly agreed to put the missing middle at the centre of a national mental health architecture.
- **Maintain existing funding levels:** All governments agreed to at least maintain existing funding for psychosocial support services to ensure service continuity for the 25,000 CPSP clients.
- **Children and young people:** Agreed to remain a priority population in the successor Agreement.
- **Lived experience consultation:** All jurisdictions agreed to consult with lived experience and sector representatives to inform negotiating positions before the next ministerial meeting.
- **National Suicide Prevention Strategy:** The new National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2025-2035 — launched 20 February 2025 — will be integrated into the new Agreement architecture as a distinct priority stream.

What was **not** agreed in June 2025: the quantum of new investment; how costs will be shared between the Commonwealth and states; whether foundational supports funding will be genuinely dedicated or contingent on state matching; and the specific governance model for the successor framework.

### 5. The Proposed New Architecture

The Productivity Commission's recommended successor architecture — which governments are now co-designing — has three pillars:

**Pillar 1: A Mental Health Declaration.** To be signed by **First Ministers** (Prime Minister and all State and Territory Premiers and Chief Ministers), not just Health Ministers. This would be a higher-level political commitment than any previous mental health agreement — acknowledging that mental health is a whole-of-government, whole-of-community challenge, not just a health system question. The Declaration would set the long-term direction for reform alongside the National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2025-2035.

**Pillar 2: A new 5-year National Agreement.** Focused on key near-term priorities (specifically including unmet psychosocial need) and structured with stronger accountability and governance mechanisms. The Commission specifically recommended improved monitoring and progress reporting — moving from activity tracking to outcome measurement.

**Pillar 3: Dedicated Schedules for priority populations.** The new Agreement would have separate, more detailed schedules for:
- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander mental health and suicide prevention (consistent with the Gayaa Dhuwi (Proud Spirit) Declaration Framework)
- Suicide prevention (linked to the National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2025-2035)
- Co-occurring mental health and problematic alcohol and other drug (AOD) use — a population with particularly complex needs and currently poor service coordination

### 6. The National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2025-2035: A Parallel Architecture

Alongside the Agreement renegotiation, the Australian Government and National Suicide Prevention Office launched the **National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2025-2035** on 20 February 2025, accompanied by **$69 million in new suicide prevention funding**.

The Strategy represents a shift from a narrowly clinical approach to a whole-of-community, whole-of-life model — reaching people earlier through non-health systems (education, employment, financial counselling, domestic violence services) and addressing the socio-economic and cultural determinants of suicidal distress. This aligns with international evidence on effective suicide prevention, which consistently demonstrates that primary prevention and social determinant interventions outperform crisis response.

The Productivity Commission's new architecture explicitly envisages the Strategy and the new National Agreement operating as companion documents — the Strategy setting the overarching long-term direction, the Agreement operationalising the health system's contribution to that direction. The First Ministers' Declaration is intended to provide political cover for both.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. Will the new architecture have enforcement teeth?** The current Agreement's failure is fundamentally an accountability failure — governments made commitments without consequences for non-delivery. The PC recommended improved monitoring, but did not recommend any enforcement mechanism or financial penalty for states that fail to deliver their bilateral schedule commitments. Unless the successor Agreement introduces genuine consequences — withholding Commonwealth grants, independent audit, or performance-linked funding — the same dynamic will recur.

**2. Can the psychosocial funding question be resolved without a new revenue source?** The $3 billion Commonwealth foundational supports commitment (including $1 billion for adult psychosocial supports contingent on state matching) remains unmatched. Resolving this in time for July 2027 requires all states and territories to agree on matching contributions — an alignment that has not yet materialised. The "at least maintain existing funding" commitment from June 2025 is explicitly a floor, not a commitment to scale up to meet the 230,500-person unmet need.

**3. The First Ministers' Declaration: real commitment or symbolic uplift?** Elevating the mental health architecture to First Ministers' level is significant. But the same First Ministers are under fiscal pressure across the spectrum: housing, infrastructure, climate, NDIS sustainability, aged care. The Declaration's value depends on what it actually commits to — specific funding, specific accountability, specific timeframes. A declaration without funding is a press release.

**4. The $13.8 billion system and a $1.8 billion agreement.** The bilateral schedules represent only a fraction of total mental health spending. The more important questions — how block grants to states are calculated and conditioned; whether PHN mental health funding (primarily through Commonwealth-funded programs like headspace and Medicare Mental Health Centres) is integrated with state community mental health services; and whether NDIS psychosocial expenditure is steerable by national mental health priorities — are not primarily addressed by the Agreement's bilateral schedule structure. Real architectural reform requires changes to how the full $13.8 billion flows, not just the 13% that is bilaterally agreed.

**5. Co-occurring AOD and mental health: a severely underserved population.** The inclusion of a dedicated co-occurring conditions schedule in the new architecture acknowledges a population that has historically fallen between mental health and AOD service silos. Approximately half of people with a severe mental illness also have a substance use disorder, and both systems have traditionally been reluctant to claim responsibility. Whether the schedule produces integrated services or simply documents the problem remains to be seen.

**6. The 2027 double-cliff governance risk.** If the new Agreement is not finalised by 1 July 2027, there is currently no fallback. The CPSP funding ends, the bilateral schedule funding stream ends, and the National Agreement's governance structure lapses. The implied safety net — that governments will always extend rather than let services fall off a cliff — has not been tested against a genuinely contested re-negotiation.

1. **The National Agreement on Foundational Supports** — a separate intergovernmental agreement signed in 2023 to deliver foundational supports under the NDIS reforms; what it commits to, how it interacts with the NMHSPA psychosocial funding question, and whether the two agreements can be aligned for July 2027
2. **Co-occurring mental health and AOD: Australia's service gap** — how many Australians have both a mental illness and a substance use disorder; why they are poorly served by both systems; what the evidence says about integrated services; and whether the proposed new schedule can change service design
3. **NDIS cost trajectory and the psychosocial cohort** — approaching $50 billion annually; the psychosocial cohort contributing $5.7 billion; what access tightening from January 2028 means for scheme sustainability versus human outcomes, and how that intersects with the new Agreement's unmet-need priority
4. **The National Suicide Prevention Outcomes Framework** — the new measurement architecture linked to the 2025-2035 Strategy; how suicide prevention is being measured for the first time systematically at a national level
5. **Housing as a mental health payment question** — severe mental illness correlates strongly with housing instability; AHURI evidence on housing-first models; whether foundational supports can work without a stable housing foundation; and how housing policy intersects with the new Agreement's architecture
