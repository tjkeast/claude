# ACT Drug Decriminalisation: Nearly Three Years of Real-World Evidence

**Report 069 — 2026-08-14**

**Summary:** The Australian Capital Territory's personal drug possession decriminalisation scheme — the first in Australian history — has now been operating for almost three years. The independent UNSW Social Policy Research Centre evaluation, delivered to government on 31 March 2026, concluded that the reforms are working as intended, with no significant negative outcomes in the first two years. Opposition claims of surging drug use and overdose deaths are contested by the evaluators and appear partly explained by national drug use trends rather than decriminalisation effects. But the scheme's more important lesson for the healthcare payments series is structural: the ACT has created a pathway *out* of criminal justice and *toward* health assessment — but the health system on the receiving end of that pathway remains underfunded, fragmented, and unable to provide timely treatment to everyone referred.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 068 mapped the expiring National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 and noted that the ACT's decriminalisation experiment had, by August 2026, produced approximately 33 months of real-world data. That report identified the ACT scheme as one of five key threads for the NDS successor: if decriminalisation demonstrably reduces harm and increases treatment engagement, it becomes the default argument for embedding health-first drug policy into the next national framework. The UNSW evaluation now provides that evidence — making this the right moment to assess what it found, what was contested, and what it means for healthcare funding.

---

## Background: What Was Legislated and Why

### The Legislation

The ACT's decriminalisation reform was enacted through the **Drugs of Dependence (Personal Use) Amendment Act 2022**, which passed the ACT Legislative Assembly on 20 October 2022. The Act came into effect on **28 October 2023** after a 12-month transition period for community education.

The Act was introduced by the ACT Labor–Greens government, primarily under the stewardship of Attorney-General Shane Rattenbury (Greens). Its stated purpose was to shift the territory's response to personal drug use from "crime and punishment" toward "harm minimisation, health and rehabilitation" — the phrase used directly in the legislative explanatory memorandum.

The ACT was an appropriate jurisdiction to pioneer reform: it is small (approximately 462,000 people), has relatively high socioeconomic indicators, already had well-developed harm reduction infrastructure (CanTEST drug checking, high needle and syringe program coverage, established diversion programs), and had the lowest rate of criminal proceedings for primary illicit drug possession offences of any Australian jurisdiction before the law changed.

### What the Law Actually Does

The reform **does not legalise** any drug. It reduces the maximum penalty for personal possession of small quantities of specified drugs from criminal conviction to one of three options at police discretion:

1. **A formal caution** — recorded but non-criminal
2. **An $100 expiation notice** (a fine) — payable without requiring a court appearance
3. **Diversion to a Canberra Health Services (CHS) drug diversion session** — a structured health education and assessment session

Critically, **police retain discretion** on which pathway to use. The law does not mandate diversion; it creates diversion as a preferred option alongside a low-level administrative penalty.

### Substances and Possession Thresholds

The scheme applies to the following drugs and possession limits:

| Drug | Personal Use Threshold |
|---|---|
| Methylamphetamine (ice) | 1.5 grams |
| Cocaine | 1.5 grams |
| Amphetamine | 1.5 grams |
| MDMA/ecstasy | 1.5 grams or 5 doses |
| LSD | 5 doses |
| Heroin | 1.0 gram |

Possession above these thresholds, or possession in circumstances suggesting supply (packaging, scales, cash), remains a criminal offence. Cannabis in the ACT is subject to a separate earlier decriminalisation (the *Drugs of Dependence (Personal Cannabis Use) Amendment Act 2019*, which permits personal cultivation of two plants and possession of 50 grams).

### The Diversion Pathway in Practice

When police invoke the health diversion pathway, the **Police and Court Drug Diversion Service** (Canberra Health Services) contacts the person and arranges a time for a structured health session. The session covers:
- A clinical assessment of the person's drug use
- Brief intervention (psychoeducation, motivational discussion)
- Referral to further services if clinically indicated

Once the session is completed, police are notified and take no further action. Non-compliance — failing to attend or engage — results in the matter returning to police for consideration of further action (typically the $100 fine or, in repeat cases, the residual possibility of minor criminal proceedings).

The ACT already operated the **Early Intervention and Drug Diversion Program (IDDP)** before 2023, which achieved 174 referrals in 2022–23. The post-decriminalisation scheme built on this infrastructure rather than creating a new system from scratch.

## Key Findings

### 1. Implementation Data: Early Numbers and Compliance Rates

The first empirical data point emerged three months post-commencement. ACT Policing reported that **70 people** were apprehended for small drug quantities in the initial quarter (late October 2023 to late January 2024). Of those:
- **67 of 70** chose the diversion pathway (96% uptake rate)
- **13 of 67** were deemed non-compliant (did not follow through with the health session)
- **54 of 70** successfully completed a diversion health session in the initial quarter

Over a longer window — **28 October 2023 to 31 March 2024** — ACT Policing recorded **116 incidents** of small-quantity drug possession eligible for the new scheme:
- **75 people attended** a diversion session
- **41 did not** (35% non-attendance rate)

The overall diversion completion rate improved through 2024. By the two-year mark, the UNSW evaluation team reported that **at least three-quarters of people diverted** had opted for a health referral and completed a health assessment — substantially higher than the initial months and consistent with research showing that awareness of a new scheme takes time to build.

A specific finding from the Drug Trends sentinel surveillance program (NDARC, UNSW) was instructive: awareness of the reform *increased* between 2023 and 2024 among people who regularly use illicit drugs in Canberra, but **understanding remained mixed** — many participants confused decriminalisation with legalisation, and held inaccurate beliefs about police behaviour. This sustained a recommendation from the UNSW evaluation for ongoing public information and community education investment.

### 2. The UNSW SPRC Evaluation (March 2026)

The ACT Government commissioned the **Social Policy Research Centre (SPRC) at UNSW Sydney**, in collaboration with the Australian National University (ANU) and the Canberra Alliance for Harm Minimisation and Advocacy (CAHMA), to conduct an independent evaluation of the Act's operation.

The evaluation team used:
- Administrative data from ACT Policing (diversion rates, seizure data)
- Canberra Health Services clinical data (diversion session completion, referral outcomes)
- AIHW data (hospital presentations, overdose notifications)
- Drug Trends sentinel surveillance (Ecstasy and Related Drugs Reporting System and Illicit Drug Reporting System participants in Canberra)
- A community survey on awareness and attitudes
- Qualitative interviews with people who use drugs, police, health workers, and community organisations

The **final evaluation report** was delivered to the ACT Government on **31 March 2026**. The headline findings, as summarised in available public communications:

- **The reforms are working as intended and reducing harms** for people who use drugs in the ACT
- **No significant negative outcomes** were identified in the first two years of operation
- **Awareness of the scheme increased substantially** between years one and two, though understanding of its precise parameters remained imperfect
- A **recommendation for sustained public education investment** — particularly addressing the decriminalisation/legalisation confusion — was included
- The evaluation confirmed that **rates of drug use are not strongly linked to levels of punishment** for personal possession — consistent with the international literature (Portugal, Switzerland, Czechia, Norway on cannabis)

The evaluation did **not** find that decriminalisation caused the increases in drug use metrics cited by the opposition (see below).

### 3. The Contested Data: What the Opposition Claims — and What It Means

The political contest over the scheme has focused on a set of statistics tabled by the ACT Liberal opposition, particularly Deputy Leader **Jeremy Hanson MLA**, who moved a motion in the ACT Legislative Assembly calling for repeal. The opposition's data claims:

- **Cocaine use up ~70%** in the ACT since decriminalisation
- **Heroin use up ~30%**
- **Methamphetamine use up ~40%**
- **16 suspected overdose deaths** in the ACT in the period to mid-2025
- **More than 1,100 drug-related emergency presentations** in 2024–25
- **Drug-driving charges up more than 20%** since 2022–23

These figures require significant contextualisation:

**National cocaine trend:** The AIHW NDSHS 2022–23 (conducted during the same period the ACT changes were being legislated and implemented) found that cocaine use nationally had risen to **4.5% of Australians aged 14+** — up from 2.5% in 2019 and representing the highest recorded rate in survey history. The ACT's increases are occurring against a national trend of surging cocaine prevalence driven by supply-side changes (record seizures suggest record importation), not decriminalisation. Attributing the ACT cocaine increase to the October 2023 law change requires demonstrating that the ACT trend diverged from the national trend — which the available data does not support.

**Emergency presentations and ambulance data:** The clinical analysis cited in contemporaneous reporting suggested that ambulance presentations (the more precise indicator) **remained stable**, with the gross hospital presentation increase partly reflecting changes in how presentations are categorised and coded. The UNSW evaluation team did not identify emergency department trends as a significant negative outcome.

**Overdose deaths:** Sixteen suspected overdose deaths in the ACT over approximately 18 months represents a serious public health concern, but needs to be assessed against the ACT's baseline rate and national trends. Australia's drug-related overdose mortality has been rising nationally, driven by the opioid and poly-drug crisis documented in Reports 059–061, not by decriminalisation policy.

**Drug-driving:** Drug-driving charge increases likely reflect **increased testing** by ACT Policing rather than increased drug use. The Drug Trends surveillance showed no corresponding increase in driving after drug use as a self-reported behaviour among sentinel populations.

The opposition's use of aggregate statistics without national trend comparators, or without controlling for enforcement intensity changes, is methodologically weak — and the UNSW evaluation team's finding of "no significant negative outcomes" directly contradicts the repeal argument.

### 4. What the ACT Harm Reduction Cost–Benefit Analysis Found (March 2025)

Parallel to the decriminalisation evaluation, the ACT Government commissioned an independent **Harm Reduction Review and Cost–Benefit Analysis**, led by researchers at ANU and the Burnet Institute, finalised in **March 2025**.

The analysis modelled the costs and benefits of **existing and potential new harm reduction interventions** in the ACT through to 2030, including:
- Opioid Agonist Treatment (OAT) — current coverage and expansion scenarios
- Take-home naloxone — current distribution and scale-up
- Drug checking services (CanTEST) — existing and expanded
- Needle and syringe programs (NSP) — current and expanded
- **Supervised Injecting Facilities (SIFs)** — new implementation modelling

The CBA's most politically significant finding was on SIFs: a supervised injecting facility in the ACT was found to be **cost-effective, even in a smaller jurisdiction** like the territory. The Burnet Institute analysis published in the journal *Addiction* in early 2026 (Bowring et al., 2026) quantified this finding, modelling health outcomes across overdose prevention, HIV/HCV prevention, and reduced emergency services use.

This analysis directly informed the ACT Government's subsequent policy consideration of a supervised injecting facility — a logical next step from decriminalisation that has faced political resistance from the Liberal opposition and within Labor's own caucus.

### 5. National Context: NSW Rejected Decriminalisation; Other States Are Watching

The ACT's experiment took place against a national backdrop where:

- The **2024 NSW Drug Summit** — 700+ participants, 3,669 written submissions — recommended broad drug reform, but NSW Premier Chris Minns and Health Minister Ryan Park declared **decriminalisation off the table** before the summit began
- The NSW Government responded to the Summit's 56 recommendations in **October 2025**, supporting or supporting in principle over 50 of them — but critically, without decriminalisation and without a significant funding commitment to the AOD treatment sector
- The response was widely criticised by harm reduction advocates; Uniting NSW/ACT described it as a "win for bad politics over good policy"
- The NSW response did commit to reforming the Early Drug Diversion Initiative to address restrictions on multiple-drug possession, criminal history barriers, and threshold quantities — incremental adjustments that fall well short of full decriminalisation

Public opinion data from a 2026 paper in *Drug and Alcohol Review* (Lloyd et al.) analysing NDSHS trends from 2001 to 2022–23 showed that **most Australians support a health rather than criminal response** to drug possession across all drug types. However:
- Only **5% support prison** for cannabis possession; 14% for ecstasy; 21% for methamphetamine; 24% for heroin
- Support for **legalisation** is highest for cannabis (44.6%) and lowest for methamphetamine (13.4%)
- The implication: public opinion broadly supports what the ACT has done, but the political economy — fuelled by tabloid coverage of opposition statistics — creates significant barriers to replication elsewhere

Victoria is the jurisdiction most likely to follow the ACT, given its existing harm reduction infrastructure (North Richmond Supervised Injecting Room, extensive NSP network, Drug Court) and Labor government. Queensland and Western Australia remain further from reform.

### 6. The Payment and Funding Implications: The System That Catches Diverted People Is Still Under-Resourced

The decriminalisation scheme's core logic is that a person caught with personal-use quantities of drugs should be directed to a health system rather than a criminal justice system. But this logic only works if the health system has the capacity to respond.

In the ACT context, the **diversion session at Canberra Health Services** is funded through the **ACT Territory Health Budget** — not through Medicare, not through a Commonwealth DAP grant, but through direct territory appropriation. The session functions as a brief intervention, analogous to what Report 067 identified as the "missing MBS item" — but without the national reimbursement architecture.

The gaps exposed by the scheme:

**Gap 1: What happens after the diversion session?** The CHS session is a single-contact brief intervention. For people who need ongoing treatment — pharmacotherapy for opioid use disorder, residential rehabilitation, specialist AOD counselling — they are referred to the existing ACT treatment system. That system has wait times; the sector-wide treatment capacity gap documented in Report 068 (more than 1,800 people waiting for NGO services in NSW alone, proportionate problems in the ACT) means that diversion to health does not reliably translate to timely treatment access.

**Gap 2: The MBS remains disconnected.** A person diverted from the ACT scheme who then sees their GP in follow-up — for naltrexone prescription, pharmacotherapy, or ongoing mental health support — faces the same MBS gaps documented in Reports 065–067. There is no dedicated MBS item for the GP to bill for alcohol or drug brief intervention follow-up in a decriminalisation context.

**Gap 3: Non-compliance has no therapeutic pathway.** People who don't attend the CHS session (approximately 25–35% in early data) have the matter returned to police. There is no follow-up therapeutic pathway for non-compliant individuals — no assertive outreach model, no peer support worker engagement. This is the case where treatment engagement is arguably most needed.

**Gap 4: The scheme doesn't touch supply-side costs.** Decriminalisation reduces policing, court, and prison costs for *personal possession* — a real saving, and the ACT was already the lowest-cost jurisdiction for drug possession prosecutions in Australia. But it does not change the supply-side enforcement expenditure, which — as the DPMP budget analysis documented in Report 068 — consumes 64% of Australia's $5.45 billion annual drug policy spend.

## Tensions and Open Questions

### Tension 1: Decriminalisation Is the Entry Point — What About the Treatment System Capacity?

The structural argument for decriminalisation is that it redirects people from criminal justice to health. But this only works if health has capacity. The ACT's diversion scheme is creating referral pathways into a system that, like all Australian AOD treatment systems, does not have the throughput to service everyone referred. The $727 million DAP redesign (Report 068) and the PHN commissioning system are the funding mechanisms that will determine whether the health system can absorb diverted people effectively — or whether decriminalisation creates a funnel into a waiting room.

### Tension 2: The Evidence vs. The Politics

The UNSW evaluation found no significant negative outcomes. The Liberal opposition tabled statistics claiming significant increases in drug use and related harms. Both sets of claims are being held simultaneously in the ACT's political discourse. This pattern is predictable — it mirrors exactly the contested early evidence debates in Portugal (1990s and early 2000s), Oregon (following Measure 110 in 2020–2023), and British Columbia (from January 2023). In all three cases, the initial statistical "debate" obscured the more important structural question: is the health system adequately resourced to replace the criminal justice system as the primary response? Oregon's Measure 110 failed — repealed in early 2024 — largely because treatment funding commitments were not met. The ACT's decriminalisation will face the same test.

### Tension 3: The Supervised Injecting Facility Question

The March 2025 CBA found a SIF cost-effective for the ACT. The ACT Government has been considering a SIF since at least 2019, when the Burnet Institute first completed a feasibility study (published 2020). The decriminalisation scheme and the SIF are natural complements — decriminalisation reduces the legal barrier for people who use drugs in public to seek help; a SIF reduces the health risk of injecting without supervision. But political resistance within the ACT Labor party (not just from the Liberal opposition) has delayed the SIF decision. The question of whether decriminalisation can realise its full harm reduction potential without a SIF is a key unresolved tension.

### Tension 4: Can the Model Scale?

The ACT's population of 462,000 and its socioeconomic characteristics (high education, relatively high income, high service density) make it an atypical test case. The UNSW evaluation team has been careful not to overclaim generalisability. A decriminalisation model in Western Sydney, Mount Druitt, or regional Queensland — with higher baseline drug use prevalence, lower socioeconomic indicators, and fewer diversionable health services — would face structurally different conditions. The National Drug Strategy successor framework will need to grapple with this explicitly: decriminalisation as a national policy requires a national treatment system capable of receiving diverted people, which does not currently exist.

### Tension 5: Thresholds and the "Personal Use" Problem

The ACT thresholds (1.5g ice; 1g heroin) were set based on an assessment of typical personal use quantities. But they create a hard boundary that does not capture the reality of dependent drug use — a person with severe opioid use disorder may require quantities above 1g heroin per day for maintenance. The threshold regime means that some dependent drug users will remain subject to criminal penalties simply because their use quantity exceeds the personal-use ceiling. The alternative — threshold-free decriminalisation for all personal use quantities — is politically untenable at present but is the direction that the harm reduction evidence base points.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **Primary Health Networks (PHNs) as the AOD service commissioners:** The PHN system is the mechanism through which Commonwealth DAP funding is translated into local service contracts. How do PHNs commission AOD services, what governance oversight exists, and how does PHN commissioning connect — or fail to connect — to First Nations community-controlled organisations? This is the mid-level between the DAP national framework and individual service providers.

2. **The National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030 and vaping:** Australia's world-leading tobacco harm reduction record is being tested by the vaping crisis — disposable e-cigarettes causing youth nicotine initiation, and the disruption of therapeutic vaping as a cessation pathway. The TGA scheduling changes (nicotine vapes as prescription medicines) and PBS cessation pharmacotherapy items together constitute a payment story that hasn't been told in this series.

3. **Supervised injecting facilities: funding and regulatory architecture:** Report 062 documented the national SIF stalemate. The ACT CBA (March 2025) adds a new evidence layer specific to the ACT. A report specifically focused on the legal, regulatory, and funding architecture of existing and proposed SIFs would fill a gap in the series — how are Sydney's MSIC and Melbourne's NRCH funded, who is legally accountable, what federal/state interaction is required, and what would Commonwealth funding look like?

4. **Oregon's Measure 110 failure as a cautionary tale:** Oregon's cannabis-tax-funded decriminalisation scheme — voted in by 58% of Oregonians in 2020, operative from 2021, repealed and recriminalised in early 2024 — provides the most directly comparable failed experiment for Australian policy purposes. What went wrong (treatment funding was slow and insufficient), and what lessons does it carry for the ACT's decriminalisation and any national expansion?

5. **First Nations healing and AOD: community-controlled alternative pathways:** Reports 013, 014, and 058 have touched on First Nations AOD gaps. This thread — not yet done in depth — would focus on ACCHOs and healing centres as culturally specific AOD service models, how they interact with mainstream DAP funding, what the payment structure for community-controlled AOD services looks like, and what the evidence says about their effectiveness versus mainstream services.
