# Methamphetamine Harm Reduction: The Pharmacotherapy Vacuum, the Psychosis Pipeline, and Australia's Record 15,791 kg

**Report 063 — 2026-08-08**

**Summary:** Methamphetamine is now Australia's most hospitalisation-intensive drug (29% of all drug-related hospital admissions), yet unlike opioid use disorder — which has three PBS-listed pharmacotherapies — there is no approved pharmacotherapy for methamphetamine use disorder anywhere in the world. Australia hosts one of the most active research pipelines globally, with 2025 trials of lisdexamfetamine and mirtazapine producing the strongest signals yet, but neither is registered for routine use. Treatment is funded almost entirely through block grants with no MBS hook, harm reduction receives just 1.6% of the total drug policy budget, and contingency management — the highest-evidence behavioural intervention for stimulant use — remains completely unfunded.

**Why this follows from yesterday:** Report 062 documented that amphetamines now account for 50.1% of injections at the Sydney Medically Supervised Injecting Centre, representing a fundamental shift from the heroin-era model that SIFs were built for. That finding raises an immediate question: if the drug profile has shifted to stimulant-dominant, what harm reduction and treatment infrastructure exists for methamphetamine users? The answer reveals one of the starkest gaps in Australian health payment architecture.

---

## Background

Australia has been living through what one analyst described as "a second meth epidemic" — quieter in public profile than the early 2000s "ice panic" but worse in its measurable harms. The National Drug Strategy Household Survey (NDSHS) 2022-23 shows that 1.0% of Australians (approximately 200,000 people) used methamphetamine in the prior 12 months, down from a peak of 2.1% in 2013. This apparent stabilisation in prevalence, however, masks a deeply concerning divergence.

**Wastewater data tells a different story.** The Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission's National Wastewater Drug Monitoring Program (Report 25, August 2025) estimated that Australians consumed **15,791 kg of methamphetamine** in the 12 months to August 2025 — a 23.2% increase on the previous record. The estimated street value of illicit stimulant consumption reached **$14.3 billion** in 2024-25. The divergence between stable survey prevalence and record wastewater consumption almost certainly reflects a smaller but more intensive cohort of heavy users driving the aggregate.

The harms data confirms the severity. In 2023, **547 overdose deaths involved amphetamine-type stimulants** — a figure that has risen fivefold in rate terms since 2000. A Burnet Institute analysis found a fourfold increase in methamphetamine-related deaths between 2001 and 2020. NDARC data published August 2026 confirmed a **29% increase in ATS deaths** in the most recent period. Drug-related **hospitalisations are at record levels: 57,000 in 2023-24**, up 10.5% year-on-year, with ATS accounting for **29% of all drug-related hospital admissions** — the largest single drug category — equating to approximately **15,100 methamphetamine-specific admissions** in 2023-24, up from 11,300 in 2022-23.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Pharmacotherapy Vacuum

For opioid use disorder, Australia has a sophisticated publicly funded pharmacotherapy system: **methadone, sublingual buprenorphine/naloxone, and long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB)** are all PBS-listed under the Opioid Treatment Program. Patients can access these through authorised prescribers and pharmacies. These medicines act as an "anchor" — dramatically improving treatment retention, reducing overdose risk, and providing a PBS funding hook that makes opioid treatment scalable through pharmacy infrastructure.

For methamphetamine, **none of this exists**. There is no approved pharmacotherapy for methamphetamine use disorder anywhere in the world. No MBS item triggers for methamphetamine treatment. No PBS-listed medicine provides substitution, reduction, or maintenance. The treatment system falls back entirely on psychosocial interventions — counselling, motivational interviewing, cognitive-behavioural therapy, residential rehabilitation — which are effective but resource-intensive, produce lower retention, and are funded through block grants with no per-patient federal subsidy mechanism equivalent to Medicare or PBS.

This is not a minor gap. The contrast in clinical outcomes is significant: opioid pharmacotherapy produces retention rates of 50–80% in treatment at 12 months; psychosocial-only methamphetamine treatment produces substantially lower rates, with 25% of ATS treatment episodes ending unplanned (compared to lower rates in pharmacotherapy-supported opioid treatment).

### 2. The Research Pipeline: LiMA, Tina, and What Comes Next

Australia hosts one of the world's most active methamphetamine pharmacotherapy research programs, centred at the **National Centre for Clinical Research on Emerging Drugs (NCCRED)**, an NIAS-funded consortium headquartered at UNSW/NDARC.

**The LiMA Trial (2025, published in *Addiction*)** is the most significant result. Led by Professor Nadine Ezard at NCCRED/St Vincent's Hospital Sydney, LiMA tested **lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse)** — an ADHD medication and Schedule 8 amphetamine prodrug — as *agonist substitution therapy*, the stimulant analogue of methadone. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 164 adults with methamphetamine dependence across six outpatient stimulant clinics in Adelaide, Melbourne, Newcastle, and Sydney (funded by NHMRC grant APP1109466), participants receiving lisdexamfetamine 250 mg/day had **8.8 fewer days of methamphetamine use** during the 12-week maintenance phase compared to placebo — treatment satisfaction was 3.8 times higher. Prof Ezard has noted that "experienced clinicians could consider off-label prescription of lisdexamfetamine, with close monitoring of risks and benefits." A Phase 3 trial required for TGA registration has not yet been funded.

**The Tina Trial (2025)** tested mirtazapine 30 mg/day (an antidepressant) in a Phase III RCT of 344 participants across six sites — billed as the first Phase 3 pharmacotherapy trial for methamphetamine use disorder globally. Results showed a 7-day reduction in methamphetamine use (vs 4.8 days in the placebo arm) — statistically significant, but with debated clinical significance in the 2.2-day difference.

Other trials in the pipeline include the **NABU pilot** (naltrexone + bupropion; feasible and safe in 20-person pilot, full RCT pending funding), the **KAPPA trial** (ketamine-assisted psychotherapy at St Vincent's Sydney, results pending), and early-stage investigation of **semaglutide** (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) by the Burnet Institute. The **N-ICE Trial** of N-acetylcysteine (2021) was negative.

None of these are approved or PBS-listed. The path from positive Phase 2/3 trial to TGA approval to PBS listing — which for a novel indication typically takes 5-10 years under current settings — means the pharmacotherapy vacuum will persist for several years even if fast-tracked.

### 3. The Psychosis Pipeline

Methamphetamine-associated psychosis (MAP) is a major driver of acute health system load and the most dangerous interface between AOD and mental health services.

**Prevalence:** 15–23% of methamphetamine users in community settings develop psychosis. In treatment settings, the rate rises to up to 60%, reflecting severity selection. A systematic review found that amphetamine use disorder **tripled** the risk of psychosis onset. Having a family history of psychotic disorder dramatically elevates individual risk.

**Hospitalisation:** Methamphetamine-related psychosis hospitalisation rates rose from 2.5 per 100,000 (males, 2010-11) to **47.2 per 100,000** (2018-19) — an 18-fold increase in less than a decade. In 2020-21, drug-induced psychosis was the **primary diagnosis in 49% of ATS hospitalisations** — approximately 6,452 admissions. By 2023-24, with total meth hospitalisations at ~15,100, psychosis-primary admissions will have grown substantially.

**The interface problem:** Acute MAP presentations land across emergency departments (state-funded, not designed for prolonged AOD engagement), public mental health inpatient units (state-funded, psychiatric orientation), and community AOD services (Commonwealth/state block grant, psychosocial model). These systems are **funded separately, governed separately, and frequently incompatible**. The RANZCP has explicitly stated that neither mental health nor AOD services are resourced for the complex, revolving-door presentations of severe MAP. The Victorian Dual Diagnosis Initiative (VDDI) and its NEXUS program at St Vincent's Melbourne represent a partial solution, but coverage remains limited to urban Victoria. No national MAP pathway or funding mechanism exists.

### 4. The National Ice Action Strategy: $451.5 Million, Partially Effective

Australia's primary policy response has been the **National Ice Action Strategy (NIAS)**, a Council of Australian Governments-endorsed program that began in 2016-17 following the National Ice Taskforce report.

Total investment: **$451.5 million over 6 years** (2016-17 to 2021-22), with a further **$315 million** committed for 2022-26 by the Morrison Government. Key funded programs included:
- **PHN AOD treatment commissioning (~80% of NIAS funding: $241.5 million)** — expanded treatment service capacity through Primary Health Networks
- **National Centre for Clinical Research on Emerging Drugs (NCCRED)** — produced the LiMA, Tina, N-ICE, NABU, KAPPA trials
- **Local Drug Action Teams (LDATs)** — $19.2 million to ADF; 280+ community-based teams established
- **Cracks in the Ice** digital information hub
- **ACIC wastewater monitoring** partnership

The Australian National Audit Office assessed the NIAS rollout as **"partially effective"** in a 2019-20 performance audit — most of the 30 actions were implemented, but monitoring and evaluation were insufficient to demonstrate whether goals were being achieved. The prevalence decline from 2.1% (2013) to 1.0% (2022-23) cannot be attributed to NIAS: much of it preceded the spending, and the 2014-16 market transition from powder (speed) to crystal (ice) may have concentrated use among a smaller, heavier cohort rather than reducing total use.

From 2027, the NIAS has been absorbed into the broader **Drug and Alcohol Program (DAP)**, which will receive $727 million over 3 years (2026-27 to 2028-29) then $244.2 million annually ongoing. All current DAP grants, due to expire June 2026, have been extended one year while the program is redesigned. A consultation process is underway for the post-2027 DAP framework — a critical window for embedding methamphetamine-specific pharmacotherapy commissioning pathways if and when LiMA or Tina are registered.

### 5. Contingency Management: The Highest-Evidence Unfunded Intervention

**Contingency management (CM)** provides tangible rewards — vouchers, prize draws, or small payments — for verified drug-free urine samples, with escalating rewards for consecutive negative results. CM has the **largest evidence base and highest effect size of any psychosocial intervention for stimulant use disorder**, outperforming CBT, motivational interviewing, and 12-step approaches in head-to-head trials. The United States has begun large-scale CM rollout through SAMHSA's Contingency Management for Stimulant Use Disorders initiative.

In Australia, CM is **completely unfunded** and has never been implemented as a routine clinical service. A 2024 qualitative study of Australian health service providers (Clay et al., *Drug and Alcohol Review*) identified the key barriers: no payer has accepted responsibility for CM funding; public and political concern about "paying people to stop using drugs"; limited clinical champions; and operational barriers around urine testing infrastructure and prize management.

NCCRED has funded a seed project ("Incentivise") to explore implementation pathways, and a Phase 2 trial (NCT05854667) is testing combined lisdexamfetamine + CM. But no funded CM program exists nationally, and no state or Commonwealth government has committed to implementing it at scale.

### 6. The Spending Inversion

A 2024 UNSW/NDARC analysis of Australian government drug expenditure in 2021-22 found:
- **Law enforcement: 64% ($3.5 billion)**
- **Treatment: 27%**
- **Prevention: 7%**
- **Harm reduction: 1.6% (~$90 million)**

Burnet Institute modelling estimates that harm reduction services deliver **$10 in benefits per $1 spent** — yet harm reduction receives the smallest share of spending. Law enforcement receives 40 times more funding than harm reduction while the drug driving the most hospitalisations (methamphetamine) has no approved pharmacotherapy and no dedicated harm reduction infrastructure beyond general AOD services.

The treatment gap compounds this. A landmark 2025 study by Ritter et al. (*Drug and Alcohol Review*) estimated that **approximately 500,000 Australians with substance use disorder received no treatment in 2023**. For methamphetamine specifically, 46–75% of people with methamphetamine use disorder received no treatment in 2022-23. Even among those who do access treatment, the absence of pharmacotherapy means engagement rates are lower than for opioid treatment, and 25% of ATS episodes end unplanned.

## Tensions and Open Questions

**1. LiMA's 8.8-day result vs the regulatory timeline.** The LiMA result is the strongest pharmacotherapy signal for methamphetamine use disorder yet published. But the path from published Phase 2/3 trial to TGA registration to PBS listing is measured in years, not months — and requires a funded Phase 3 trial. No government has committed to funding that trial. The window in the DAP redesign (2027) may be the nearest policy mechanism.

**2. The stimulant transition in harm reduction infrastructure.** As documented in Report 062, amphetamines now account for 50.1% of injections at the Sydney MSIC. More broadly, the majority of Australian methamphetamine is now **smoked** (ice form), not injected — making needle and syringe programs (NSPs) less relevant as harm reduction tools for this cohort. Australia has no supervised smoking facilities equivalent to those emerging in parts of Europe. The harm reduction toolkit for smoked methamphetamine is poorly developed.

**3. The psychosis funding gap is structural.** MAP crosses AOD and mental health systems that will not converge without dedicated joint funding. The current system creates perverse incentives: emergency departments stabilise and discharge; community mental health services decline to engage people who are actively using; AOD services lack the psychiatric capacity for active psychosis. Dual-diagnosis investment remains scattered and limited.

**4. Contingency management's political blockage.** CM's evidence base is comparable to methadone for opioids, yet its "paying people to stop using drugs" framing generates political resistance that pharmacotherapy does not face. The question is whether the DAP redesign creates a funding pathway that normalises CM as evidence-based treatment rather than moral controversy.

**5. The law enforcement vs harm reduction ratio.** The 40:1 spending ratio (law enforcement:harm reduction) has no evidence basis and inverses the estimated returns ($10 return per $1 harm reduction; law enforcement has produced no measurable reduction in supply or use). The DAP redesign and the National Drug Strategy successor (for which consultation is underway) represent the primary policy levers to rebalance this.

## Threads to Branch Into Next

1. **The National Drug Strategy 2027 successor** — the current strategy (2017-2026) expires at the end of this year. Consultation is underway. Whether the successor includes binding investment targets for pharmacotherapy commissioning, contingency management, and harm reduction — or remains an aspiration document — will shape the next decade of Australian drug policy.

2. **First Nations communities and methamphetamine harm reduction** — methamphetamine use is deeply embedded in some remote and very remote communities with effectively no access to pharmacotherapy, drug checking, or harm reduction infrastructure. ACCHOs have developed contextually appropriate responses, but the Commonwealth-state funding interface and legal frameworks create a harm reduction dead zone for the most vulnerable populations.

3. **GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide/Ozempic) as addiction treatment** — the emerging animal and early-human literature on GLP-1 agonists reducing stimulant cravings and reinforcement is one of the most intriguing developments in addiction pharmacology. Australia's semaglutide trial (Burnet Institute) is at the frontier of this; the PBS listing of semaglutide for metabolic indications creates a potential pathway if evidence solidifies.

4. **Dual diagnosis funding and the AOD-mental health interface** — the structural incompatibility between AOD and mental health funding, governance, and clinical frameworks creates a revolving door for people with methamphetamine-induced psychosis. Victoria's VDDI/NEXUS model is the most developed attempt at a solution; whether it can be scaled or Commonwealth-funded is an open policy question.

5. **The Drug and Alcohol Program redesign (2027)** — as the primary Commonwealth funding mechanism for all non-PBS, non-MBS AOD services, the DAP redesign process is the central policy event for drug treatment funding in the next three years. The consultation process underway will determine whether pharmacotherapy commissioning pathways, contingency management, and harm reduction receive structural funding commitments.
